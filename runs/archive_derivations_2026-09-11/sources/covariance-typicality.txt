## 8.1 Covariance decomposition across an event (K)

Part 6 produced a *conditional* (good–set) covariance bound—on a region (K) where the matrix hinge holds—of the schematic form
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{\Lambda,K}}(F,G)\big|;\lesssim; e^{-\eta,\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}F,\mathrm{supp}G)}.
]
Part 7 produced mechanisms intended to make such a region (K) *typical* (high probability), via drift/functional inequalities. Part 8 now formalizes the bridge:

> **Goal of localization:** upgrade a covariance bound under (\mu_{\Lambda,K}:=\mu_\Lambda(\cdot\mid K)) to a covariance bound under the full Gibbs measure (\mu_\Lambda), paying an explicit error proportional to (\mu_\Lambda(K^c)).

Nothing deep is happening here—this is the law of total covariance—but the *bookkeeping* matters, because we want an error term that is (i) explicit, (ii) uniform in volume, and (iii) doesn’t contaminate the distance exponent coming from the massive Maxwell inverse.

Throughout this subsection, ((\Omega,\mathcal F)) is a measurable space (in our application (\Omega=M_\Lambda)), (\mu=\mu_\Lambda) is a probability measure, and (K\in\mathcal F) satisfies
[
0<\mu(K)<1.
]
For a measurable (H), write
[
\mu_K(H);:=;\mu(H\mid K)=\frac{\mu(H\cap K)}{\mu(K)},
\qquad
\mu_{K^c}(H);:=;\mu(H\mid K^c)=\frac{\mu(H\cap K^c)}{\mu(K^c)}.
\tag{8.1}
]
For integrable (F,G), recall
[
\mathrm{Cov}_\nu(F,G):=\nu(FG)-\nu(F)\nu(G).
]

---

### Lemma 8.1 Covariance decomposition across an event

For any bounded measurable (F,G:\Omega\to\mathbb R),
[
\mathrm{Cov}_\mu(F,G)
=====================

\mu(K),\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu_K}(F,G)
;+;
\mu(K^c),\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu_{K^c}}(F,G)
;+;
\mu(K)\mu(K^c),\Delta_KF,\Delta_KG,
\tag{8.2}
]
where
[
\Delta_KF;:=;\mu_K(F)-\mu_{K^c}(F),
\qquad
\Delta_KG;:=;\mu_K(G)-\mu_{K^c}(G).
\tag{8.3}
]

**Proof.** Let (\alpha:=\mu(K)\in(0,1)). Then (\mu) decomposes as the convex mixture
[
\mu(\cdot)=\alpha,\mu_K(\cdot)+(1-\alpha),\mu_{K^c}(\cdot).
\tag{8.4}
]
In particular,
[
\mu(FG)=\alpha,\mu_K(FG)+(1-\alpha),\mu_{K^c}(FG),
\tag{8.5}
]
and
[
\mu(F)=\alpha,\mu_K(F)+(1-\alpha),\mu_{K^c}(F),
\qquad
\mu(G)=\alpha,\mu_K(G)+(1-\alpha),\mu_{K^c}(G).
\tag{8.6}
]
Introduce the shorthand
[
A:=\mu_K(F),\quad B:=\mu_{K^c}(F),\quad C:=\mu_K(G),\quad D:=\mu_{K^c}(G).
]
Then
[
\mu(F)\mu(G)=\big(\alpha A+(1-\alpha)B\big)\big(\alpha C+(1-\alpha)D\big)
=\alpha^2 AC+\alpha(1-\alpha)(AD+BC)+(1-\alpha)^2 BD.
\tag{8.7}
]
Now compute
[
\mu(FG)-\mu(F)\mu(G)
====================

\alpha\mu_K(FG)+(1-\alpha)\mu_{K^c}(FG)-\Big[\alpha^2 AC+\alpha(1-\alpha)(AD+BC)+(1-\alpha)^2 BD\Big].
]
Add and subtract (\alpha AC) and ((1-\alpha)BD) to isolate conditional covariances:
[
\begin{aligned}
\mu(FG)-\mu(F)\mu(G)
&=\alpha\big(\mu_K(FG)-AC\big)+(1-\alpha)\big(\mu_{K^c}(FG)-BD\big) \
&\quad+\Big(\alpha AC+(1-\alpha)BD-\alpha^2AC-\alpha(1-\alpha)(AD+BC)-(1-\alpha)^2BD\Big).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.8}
]
The first two terms are (\alpha,\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu_K}(F,G)+(1-\alpha),\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu_{K^c}}(F,G)). For the bracketed term, simplify:
[
\alpha AC-\alpha^2AC=\alpha(1-\alpha)AC,\qquad (1-\alpha)BD-(1-\alpha)^2BD=\alpha(1-\alpha)BD,
]
so the bracket becomes
[
\alpha(1-\alpha)\big(AC+BD-AD-BC\big)=\alpha(1-\alpha)(A-B)(C-D).
\tag{8.9}
]
Recognizing (A-B=\Delta_KF) and (C-D=\Delta_KG), we obtain exactly (8.2)–(8.3). (\square)

*A version of this identity appears in the earlier localization notes, with the same three-term structure.* 

---

### Corollary 8.2 A raw localization error bound in terms of (\mu(K^c))

For bounded measurable (F,G),
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*\mu(F,G)\big|
;\le;
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu_K}(F,G)\big|
;+;
8,|F|*\infty,|G|*\infty,\mu(K^c).
\tag{8.10}
]

**Proof.** Start from the decomposition (8.2) and use (\mu(K)\le 1):
[
|\mathrm{Cov}*\mu(F,G)|
\le
|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu_K}(F,G)|
+
\mu(K^c),|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{K^c}}(F,G)|
+
\mu(K)\mu(K^c),|\Delta_KF|,|\Delta_KG|.
\tag{8.11}
]
Two elementary bounds suffice:

1. For any probability measure (\nu) and bounded (F,G),
   [
   |\mathrm{Cov}*\nu(F,G)|
   =
   \big|\nu\big((F-\nu F)(G-\nu G)\big)\big|
   \le
   |F-\nu F|*\infty,|G-\nu G|*\infty
   \le
   (2|F|*\infty)(2|G|*\infty)
   =
   4|F|*\infty|G|_\infty.
   \tag{8.12}
   ]
2. Since (|\mu_K(F)|\le|F|*\infty) and (|\mu*{K^c}(F)|\le|F|*\infty),
   [
   |\Delta_KF|=|\mu_K(F)-\mu*{K^c}(F)|\le 2|F|*\infty,
   \qquad
   |\Delta_KG|\le 2|G|*\infty.
   \tag{8.13}
   ]

Insert (8.12)–(8.13) into (8.11):
[
\mu(K^c),|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{K^c}}(F,G)|
\le 4|F|*\infty|G|*\infty,\mu(K^c),
]
and
[
\mu(K)\mu(K^c)|\Delta_KF||\Delta_KG|
\le
\mu(K^c)\cdot (2|F|*\infty)(2|G|*\infty)
========================================

4|F|*\infty|G|*\infty,\mu(K^c).
]
Summing gives (8.10). (\square)

*This is exactly the “error ledger” we want: conditional covariance on (K) plus an explicit additive term proportional to (\mu(K^c)).* 

---

### Remark 8.3 What this buys us in the mass-gap chain

Corollary 8.2 says: once Part 6 gives an exponential-in-distance bound on (\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu_K}(F,G)) (via Helffer–Sjöstrand + massive Maxwell kernel decay), the only obstruction to upgrading that bound to (\mu) is proving that (\mu(K^c)) is small enough.

Crucially, the localization term in (8.10) is **distance-independent**. So if we can arrange (for a carefully chosen canonical (K)) that (\mu(K^c)) decays *exponentially in the volume* (or at least fast enough that it is negligible compared to (e^{-\eta,\mathrm{dist}}) in the regimes we care about), then localization does **not** spoil the distance exponent coming from the Green kernel of (M^{-1}).

Section 8.2 will specify the canonical choice of (K) (an averaged plaquette “badness” constraint), and §8.3 will isolate the remaining “typicality” estimate required to make (\mu(K^c)) quantitatively small.





### 8.2 Choice of canonical set: an averaged plaquette badness constraint

Section 8.1 is the purely algebraic part of localization: it tells us that to upgrade a “good–set covariance bound” to a full covariance bound, we pay an additive error proportional to (\mu_\Lambda(K^c)). The present subsection fixes the canonical choice of the event (K) that will be used in that decomposition.

There are two design constraints that matter for the rest of the program:

1. **Gauge invariance.** Since we ultimately care about gauge-invariant observables and the Gibbs measure is gauge invariant, the localization event should be gauge invariant as well—otherwise “typicality” becomes a hostage to arbitrary gauge representatives.

2. **Averaging rather than maxima.** Sets defined by (\max_p(\text{badness}_p)) typically have complements controlled only by a union bound, which introduces an explicit (|P(\Lambda)|) prefactor and is exactly the sort of volume leakage that later wrecks exponential-in-distance estimates. Averaged constraints naturally come with (|P(\Lambda)|^{-1/2}) Lipschitz scaling, which is the right shape for concentration once a volume-uniform functional inequality is available.

Accordingly, we choose (K) by imposing a smallness condition on an **averaged plaquette badness**.

---

#### 8.2.1 A plaquette “badness interface” and the averaged functional

Fix once and for all a function
[
\vartheta:G\to[0,\infty)
]
satisfying the following standing hypotheses:

* (\vartheta\in C^1(G)),
* (\vartheta) is conjugation invariant: (\vartheta(hgh^{-1})=\vartheta(g)) for all (g,h\in G),
* (\vartheta(\mathbf 1)=0),
* (|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty:=\sup*{g\in G}|\nabla_G\vartheta(g)|<\infty).

In the intended application we take (\vartheta=\widetilde z) from §7.2,
[
\widetilde z(g)=1-\frac1n\Re\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(g)),
]
so that (\vartheta) is smooth, conjugation invariant, and (0\le \vartheta\le 2). We keep (\vartheta) abstract for the localization bookkeeping, because the only properties needed in §8.2–§8.3 are the (C^1) bound and conjugation invariance.

For each plaquette (p\in P(\Lambda)), define the lifted plaquette observable
[
\vartheta_p:M_\Lambda\to[0,\infty),
\qquad
\vartheta_p(U):=\vartheta\big(U_p(U)\big),
\tag{8.14}
]
where (U_p(U)\in G) is the oriented plaquette holonomy (cf. §2.3).

**Definition 8.4 (averaged plaquette badness and canonical set).**
Define the averaged badness functional
[
\mathcal B_\Lambda(U)
;:=;
\frac{1}{|P(\Lambda)|}\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\vartheta_p(U)
==========================================================

\frac{1}{|P(\Lambda)|}\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\vartheta\big(U_p(U)\big),
\tag{8.15}
]
and for (\varepsilon>0) define the canonical “good” event
[
K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)
;:=;
\big{,U\in M_\Lambda:\ \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)\le \varepsilon,\big}.
\tag{8.16}
]

Two immediate remarks:

* If (\vartheta=\widetilde z) (trace defect), then (\mathcal B_\Lambda) is essentially the **Wilson action density**:
  [
  S_W(U)=\beta\sum_{p}\widetilde z\big(U_p(U)\big)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)=\frac{1}{\beta,|P(\Lambda)|},S_W(U).
  \tag{8.17}
  ]
  So (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) is a low-action-density event.
* Because (\vartheta\ge 0), one always has (\mathcal B_\Lambda(U)\ge 0). If (\vartheta) is bounded (as for (\widetilde z)), then (\mathcal B_\Lambda) is bounded uniformly in (\Lambda) as well.

We record gauge invariance explicitly.

**Lemma 8.5 (gauge invariance of (\mathcal B_\Lambda) and (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon))).**
The functional (\mathcal B_\Lambda) is gauge invariant. Consequently, the event (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) is gauge invariant.

**Proof.**
Under a gauge transformation (g\in G^{V(\Lambda)}), the plaquette holonomy transforms by conjugation at the basepoint:
[
U_p(g\cdot U)=g_x,U_p(U),g_x^{-1},
]
for the appropriate corner (x) of (p). Since (\vartheta) is conjugation invariant, (\vartheta(U_p(g\cdot U))=\vartheta(U_p(U))) for every plaquette (p). Averaging over (p) gives (\mathcal B_\Lambda(g\cdot U)=\mathcal B_\Lambda(U)), and therefore (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) is gauge invariant. (\square)

---

#### 8.2.2 The max-version set and a basic inclusion

The averaged set (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) is designed for concentration. Sometimes it is also useful to name the stronger “uniform smallness” event.

**Definition 8.6 (pointwise plaallowess / max version).**
Define
[
K_\Lambda^{\max}(\varepsilon)
;:=;
\big{,U\in M_\Lambda:\ \vartheta\big(U_p(U)\big)\le \varepsilon\ \text{for every }p\in P(\Lambda),\big}.
\tag{8.18}
]

**Lemma 8.7 (trivial inclusion).**
[
K_\Lambda^{\max}(\varepsilon)\ \subseteq\ K_\Lambda(\varepsilon).
\tag{8.19}
]

**Proof.** If (\vartheta(U_p)\le \varepsilon) for all (p), then their average is also (\le\varepsilon). (\square)

Finally, to keep the interface with the small-field region from Part 5 visible, we record the easy direction: linkwise small field implies small plaquette badness (hence implies membership in (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) for a suitable (\varepsilon)).

**Lemma 8.8 (linkwise small field (\Rightarrow) small averaged badness).**
Assume (\vartheta) satisfies a quadratic upper bound near the identity: there exist (r_0>0) and (c_+<\infty) such that
[
\vartheta(g)\ \le\ c_+,d_G(g,\mathbf 1)^2
\qquad\forall g\in B_{r_0}^G(\mathbf 1).
\tag{8.20}
]
(For (\vartheta=\widetilde z), this holds by the Taylor expansion at (\mathbf 1); cf. §7.2.)

Then for any (0<r\le r_0/4),
[
K_\Lambda(r)\ \subseteq\ K_\Lambda^{\max}(16c_+r^2)\ \subseteq\ K_\Lambda(16c_+r^2),
\tag{8.21}
]
where (K_\Lambda(r)) is the linkwise small-field region from §5.3.

**Proof.**
If (U\in K_\Lambda(r)), then every link (U_b) lies in (B_r^G(\mathbf 1)). For a plaquette (p) with boundary word (U_p=U_{b_1}U_{b_2}U_{b_3}^{-1}U_{b_4}^{-1}), repeated use of the triangle inequality plus left/right invariance gives
[
d_G(U_p,\mathbf 1)
\le
d_G(U_{b_1},\mathbf 1)+d_G(U_{b_2},\mathbf 1)+d_G(U_{b_3},\mathbf 1)+d_G(U_{b_4},\mathbf 1)
\le 4r.
\tag{8.22}
]
Thus (U_p\in B_{4r}^G(\mathbf 1)\subset B_{r_0}^G(\mathbf 1)), and (8.20) yields
[
\vartheta(U_p)\le c_+,d_G(U_p,\mathbf 1)^2 \le c_+,(4r)^2 = 16c_+r^2.
\tag{8.23}
]
This holds for every plaquette (p), giving (U\in K_\Lambda^{\max}(16c_+r^2)), and then Lemma 8.7 gives the averaged inclusion. (\square)

Remark 8.9. The inclusion (8.21) is one-way. An averaged constraint (\mathcal B_\Lambda\le\varepsilon) does *not* force every plaquette (let alone every link) to be small. That asymmetry is deliberate: (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) is chosen for **typicality**, not for uniform pointwise control.

---

#### 8.2.3 Lipschitz scaling of (\mathcal B_\Lambda) and why averaging is the right normalization

The typicality estimate in §8.3 will require that (\mathcal B_\Lambda) be Lipschitz with a constant that improves with volume. This is exactly where locality and bounded overlap enter.

Let (\nabla) be the Riemannian gradient on (M_\Lambda) with respect to the product metric (g_\Lambda), and decompose it into link-gradients as in §3.5 / §7.2:
[
|\nabla f|*{g*\Lambda}^2=\sum_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)}|\nabla_\ell f|_{\mathfrak g}^2.
\tag{8.24}
]
Let (\deg_P(\ell)) denote the number of plaquettes whose boundary contains the link (\ell). By the bounded overlap constant from §3.4, there is (\nu<\infty) (depending only on the lattice dimension) such that
[
\deg_P(\ell)\le \nu
\qquad\forall \ell\in E(\Lambda).
\tag{8.25}
]

**Lemma 8.10 (uniform linkwise gradient bound for (\vartheta_p)).**
For every plaquette (p), every link (\ell), and every (U\in M_\Lambda),
[
|\nabla_\ell \vartheta_p(U)|
\ \le
|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty\ \mathbf 1*{{\ell\in\partial p}}.
\tag{8.26}
]

**Proof.**
If (\ell\notin\partial p), then (\vartheta_p(U)) is independent of (U_\ell), so (\nabla_\ell\vartheta_p\equiv 0).

If (\ell\in\partial p), varying (U_\ell) while holding the other three boundary links fixed changes (U_p) by left/right multiplication and possibly inversion in (G). Since the metric on (G) is bi-invariant, left/right multiplication and inversion are isometries, so composing (\vartheta) with this map does not increase the gradient norm. Therefore the link-gradient norm is bounded by (\sup_G|\nabla_G\vartheta|=|\nabla_G\vartheta|_\infty). (\square)

We now bound the gradient of the averaged functional (\mathcal B_\Lambda).

**Lemma 8.11 (Lipschitz constant of the averaged badness).**
There exists a constant (L_0<\infty), depending only on (\nu), the dimension-dependent ratio (|E(\Lambda)|/|P(\Lambda)|), and (|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty), such that for every finite periodic (\Lambda),
[
\sup*{U\in M_\Lambda}|\nabla \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)|*{g*\Lambda}
\ \le
\frac{L_0}{\sqrt{|P(\Lambda)|}}.
\tag{8.27}
]
In particular, (\mathcal B_\Lambda) is (\frac{L_0}{\sqrt{|P(\Lambda)|}})-Lipschitz with respect to the Riemannian distance on ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)).

**Proof.**
Fix a link (\ell). Differentiating the average (8.15) in the (\ell)-direction gives
[
\nabla_\ell \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)
=================================

# \frac{1}{|P(\Lambda)|}\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)} \nabla_\ell \vartheta_p(U)

\frac{1}{|P(\Lambda)|}\sum_{p:,\ell\in\partial p} \nabla_\ell \vartheta_p(U).
\tag{8.28}
]
By the triangle inequality, Lemma 8.10, and (\deg_P(\ell)\le \nu),
[
|\nabla_\ell \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)|
\le
\frac{1}{|P(\Lambda)|}\sum_{p:,\ell\in\partial p}|\nabla_\ell \vartheta_p(U)|
\le
\frac{\deg_P(\ell)}{|P(\Lambda)|},|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty
\le
\frac{\nu}{|P(\Lambda)|},|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty.
\tag{8.29}
]
Now sum over all links:
[
|\nabla \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)|^2
================================

\sum_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)}|\nabla_\ell \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)|^2
\le
|E(\Lambda)|\left(\frac{\nu}{|P(\Lambda)|},|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty\right)^2.
\tag{8.30}
]
On the periodic hypercubic lattice in fixed dimension, (|E(\Lambda)|/|P(\Lambda)|) is a dimension-dependent constant (in (d=4), (|E|/|P|=2/3)). Thus there is (C*{E/P}<\infty), independent of (\Lambda), such that
[
|E(\Lambda)|\le C_{E/P},|P(\Lambda)|.
\tag{8.31}
]
Insert (8.31) into (8.30) to get
[
|\nabla \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)|^2
\le
\frac{C_{E/P},\nu^2,|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty^2}{|P(\Lambda)|},
\tag{8.32}
]
hence
[
\sup*{U}|\nabla \mathcal B_\Lambda(U)|
\le
\frac{\sqrt{C_{E/P}},\nu,|\nabla_G\vartheta|*\infty}{\sqrt{|P(\Lambda)|}}.
]
This is (8.27) with (L_0:=\sqrt{C*{E/P}},\nu,|\nabla_G\vartheta|_\infty). (\square)

**Remark 8.12 (why this exact scaling matters).**
The bound (8.27) is the quantitative reason we localize using an **averaged** event. Once a volume-uniform LSI (or a suitable concentration mechanism) is available, a Lipschitz constant of order (|P|^{-1/2}) yields Gaussian tails with an exponent proportional to (|P(\Lambda)|). This is precisely the scale needed for (\mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)^c)) to be negligible in the covariance decomposition of §8.1 without spoiling the distance exponent coming from the massive Maxwell inverse.

---

This fixes the canonical choice of (K) and the geometric estimate (the (|P|^{-1/2}) Lipschitz scaling) that will be fed into the typicality step. Section 8.3 isolates the remaining probabilistic/functional-inequality input needed to turn this into an explicit bound on (\mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)^c)).




### 8.3 Typicality / concentration estimate for (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon))

Section 8.1 shows that localization across an event (K) costs an additive error proportional to (\mu_\Lambda(K^c)). For the canonical choice
[
K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)={,\mathcal B_\Lambda\le \varepsilon,},
\qquad
\mathcal B_\Lambda(U)=\frac1{|P(\Lambda)|}\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\vartheta\big(U_p(U)\big),
]
the central task is therefore:

> **Typicality goal.** Prove that (\mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)^c)=\mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda>\varepsilon)) is *exponentially small in (|P(\Lambda)|)* (with constants uniform in (\Lambda)), so that the distance exponent coming from the massive-Maxwell inverse is not polluted.

The geometric ingredient needed for such a statement was already established in §8.2.3: (\mathcal B_\Lambda) is (\mathrm{O}(|P(\Lambda)|^{-1/2}))-Lipschitz. What remains is a **concentration mechanism** for Lipschitz observables under (\mu_\Lambda), and a **centering/mean control** ensuring the hinge threshold (\varepsilon) lies above the typical value of (\mathcal B_\Lambda).

---

#### 8.3.1 Gaussian concentration from a global log-Sobolev inequality

We use the standard Herbst argument. We state it in a form adapted to our Riemannian setting ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)).

Assumption 8.13 (volume-uniform global LSI).
There exists a constant (\rho_0>0), independent of (\Lambda), such that for every smooth (f),
[
\mathrm{Ent}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f^2)
;\le;
\frac{2}{\rho_0}\int_{M_\Lambda}|\nabla f|*{g*\Lambda}^2,d\mu_\Lambda.
\tag{8.33}
]
(Equivalently, (\mu_\Lambda) satisfies an LSI with constant (C_{\mathrm{LS}}=1/\rho_0) in the common normalization.)

Proposition 8.14 (Herbst concentration from LSI).
Assume (8.33). Let (H:M_\Lambda\to\mathbb R) be (L)-Lipschitz with respect to the Riemannian distance on ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)), equivalently
[
|\nabla H(U)|*{g*\Lambda}\le L\qquad\forall U\in M_\Lambda.
\tag{8.34}
]
Then for all (t\ge 0),
[
\mu_\Lambda\big(H-\mu_\Lambda H\ge t\big)
;\le;
\exp!\left(-\frac{\rho_0,t^2}{2L^2}\right),
\qquad
\mu_\Lambda\big(H-\mu_\Lambda H\le -t\big)
;\le;
\exp!\left(-\frac{\rho_0,t^2}{2L^2}\right).
\tag{8.35}
]

Proof.
Fix (s>0) and define the log-moment generating function
[
\Lambda(s):=\log\mu_\Lambda\big(e^{sH}\big).
]
Let
[
g_s(U):=\frac{e^{\frac{s}{2}H(U)}}{\big(\mu_\Lambda(e^{sH})\big)^{1/2}},
\qquad\text{so that}\qquad
\mu_\Lambda(g_s^2)=1.
]
By the definition of entropy,
[
\mathrm{Ent}*{\mu*\Lambda}(g_s^2)
=================================

# \mu_\Lambda(g_s^2\log g_s^2)

# \mu_\Lambda!\left(\frac{e^{sH}}{\mu_\Lambda(e^{sH})}\Big(sH-\Lambda(s)\Big)\right)

s,\Lambda'(s)-\Lambda(s).
\tag{8.36}
]
On the other hand,
[
\nabla g_s=\frac{s}{2},g_s,\nabla H,
\qquad
|\nabla g_s|^2=\frac{s^2}{4},g_s^2,|\nabla H|^2,
]
hence, using (|\nabla H|\le L) and (\mu_\Lambda(g_s^2)=1),
[
\int |\nabla g_s|^2,d\mu_\Lambda
\le
\frac{s^2L^2}{4}.
\tag{8.37}
]
Insert (8.36)–(8.37) into the LSI (8.33):
[
s\Lambda'(s)-\Lambda(s)
\le
\frac{2}{\rho_0}\cdot \frac{s^2L^2}{4}
======================================

\frac{s^2L^2}{2\rho_0}.
\tag{8.38}
]
Since (\Lambda'(0)=\mu_\Lambda(H)), integrating (8.38) from (0) to (s) yields the standard bound
[
\Lambda(s)-s\Lambda'(0)\le \frac{s^2L^2}{2\rho_0},
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\log\mu_\Lambda\big(e^{s(H-\mu_\Lambda H)}\big)\le \frac{s^2L^2}{2\rho_0}.
\tag{8.39}
]
Finally, Markov’s inequality gives for (t\ge 0),
[
\mu_\Lambda(H-\mu_\Lambda H\ge t)
=================================

\mu_\Lambda\big(e^{s(H-\mu_\Lambda H)}\ge e^{st}\big)
\le
e^{-st},\mu_\Lambda\big(e^{s(H-\mu_\Lambda H)}\big)
\le
\exp!\left(-st+\frac{s^2L^2}{2\rho_0}\right).
]
Optimizing over (s>0) at (s=\rho_0 t/L^2) yields the upper-tail bound in (8.35). The lower-tail bound follows by applying the same argument to (-H). (\square)

Remark 8.15 (where the “LSI bottleneck” sits).
Proposition 8.14 is exactly the point at which a **global LSI** (uniform in (\Lambda)) becomes a quantitative tool downstream. If one only has a global Poincaré inequality, one gets (\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal B_\Lambda)=\mathrm{O}(|P|^{-1})) and hence only polynomial (Chebyshev) tails, which is typically too weak for the localization error budget in §8.1.

---

#### 8.3.2 Concentration of (\mathcal B_\Lambda) and an exponential volume bound for (\mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)^c))

We now apply Proposition 8.14 to (H=\mathcal B_\Lambda). Recall the Lipschitz estimate from Lemma 8.11: there is (L_0<\infty), independent of (\Lambda), such that
[
\sup_{U\in M_\Lambda}|\nabla\mathcal B_\Lambda(U)|*{g*\Lambda}
\le
\frac{L_0}{\sqrt{|P(\Lambda)|}}.
\tag{8.40}
]
Thus (\mathcal B_\Lambda) is (\frac{L_0}{\sqrt{|P(\Lambda)|}})-Lipschitz.

Corollary 8.16 (conditional: Gaussian concentration for (\mathcal B_\Lambda)).
Assume the global LSI (8.33) holds with constant (\rho_0>0). Then for all (t\ge 0),
[
\mu_\Lambda\big(\mathcal B_\Lambda-\mu_\Lambda\mathcal B_\Lambda\ge t\big)
;\le;
\exp!\left(
-\frac{\rho_0 t^2}{2}\cdot \frac{|P(\Lambda)|}{L_0^2}
\right),
\tag{8.41}
]
and the same bound holds for the lower tail.

Proof.
Apply Proposition 8.14 with (H=\mathcal B_\Lambda) and (L=L_0/\sqrt{|P(\Lambda)|}). (\square)

To convert (8.41) into a bound on (\mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda>\varepsilon)), we must position (\varepsilon) *above the mean* by a uniform margin.

Corollary 8.17 (volume-exponential typicality of (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) under a mean gap).
Assume the global LSI (8.33). Fix (\varepsilon>0). Suppose there exists (t>0), independent of (\Lambda), such that
[
\mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda)\ \le\ \varepsilon-t.
\tag{8.42}
]
Then
[
\mu_\Lambda\big(K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)^c\big)
=============================================

\mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda>\varepsilon)
;\le;
\exp!\left(
-\frac{\rho_0 t^2}{2}\cdot \frac{|P(\Lambda)|}{L_0^2}
\right).
\tag{8.43}
]

Proof.
If (8.42) holds then ({\mathcal B_\Lambda>\varepsilon}\subseteq {\mathcal B_\Lambda-\mu_\Lambda\mathcal B_\Lambda\ge t}). Apply (8.41). (\square)

Remark 8.18 (what this does to the localization error ledger).
Combine Corollary 8.2 with (8.43): for bounded (F,G),
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)\big|
\le
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{\Lambda,K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)}}(F,G)\big|
+
8|F|*\infty|G|*\infty,
\exp!\left(
-\frac{\rho_0 t^2}{2}\cdot \frac{|P(\Lambda)|}{L_0^2}
\right).
\tag{8.44}
]
The first term is where the distance-dependent decay (from the massive Maxwell inverse) enters; the second term is distance-independent and exponentially small in volume, hence does not spoil the distance exponent.

---

#### 8.3.3 The remaining “typicality” input (explicitly isolated)

Corollary 8.17 reduces typicality of (K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) to verifying (8.42), i.e. a **uniform gap between the hinge threshold (\varepsilon)** and the **mean badness** (\mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda)).

Assumption 8.19 (uniform mean-below-threshold typicality; open input).
There exist (\varepsilon>0) and (t>0), independent of (\Lambda), such that
[
\mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda)\ \le\ \varepsilon-t
\qquad\text{for all finite periodic }\Lambda.
\tag{8.45}
]

Remarks.

1. **Why this is the real content.** Concentration (8.41) is “automatic” once a uniform LSI is available and once we chose an averaged observable with Lipschitz constant (\mathrm{O}(|P|^{-1/2})). The genuinely model-dependent step is to show that the Gibbs state actually sits below the required threshold (\varepsilon) (with a margin (t)).
2. **Reduction to a one-plaquette expectation.** On a periodic lattice, translation invariance implies
   [
   \mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda)=\mu_\Lambda(\vartheta_{p_0})
   \quad\text{for any fixed reference plaquette }p_0,
   ]
   so (8.45) is equivalent to a uniform finite-volume control of a single local observable.
3. **In the Wilson choice (\vartheta=\widetilde z).** Using (8.17),
   [
   \mu_\Lambda(\mathcal B_\Lambda)=\frac{1}{\beta |P(\Lambda)|},\mu_\Lambda(S_W),
   ]
   so (8.45) is a uniform bound on the action density (equivalently, on the derivative of the free energy with respect to (\beta)). Establishing such bounds in the weak-coupling regime is a substantive input (typically via expansion, RG control, or a separate coercive mechanism).
4. **Relation to the outline’s flagged gap.** In the dependency ledger, Assumption 8.19 is precisely the “typicality/localization” gap: without it, Gaussian concentration around the mean does not imply that the hinge-valid region is typical.

---

This completes the localization-typicality interface: once (i) a uniform global LSI is available and (ii) the mean-below-threshold input (8.45) is proved, the complement probability (\mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)^c)) becomes exponentially small in (|P(\Lambda)|), and therefore the localization error from §8.1 does not interfere with the distance-dependent exponential decay produced by the massive Maxwell Green kernel.
