# Part 4 — Convex Lattice Scalar Field as Prototype (Updated, Expanded, Self‑Contained)

This part is a deliberately “no-mystery” prototype: a finite-dimensional lattice scalar field whose Gibbs measure is **uniformly strongly log-concave**. That single structural feature implies, with fully explicit constants and **no dependence on the lattice volume**, both:

* a **Poincaré inequality** (spectral gap), and
* a **logarithmic Sobolev inequality** (LSI).

The point of doing this in excruciating detail is not because scalar (\phi^4) is hard; it’s because it cleanly isolates the analytic mechanism we want to replicate later in the gauge setting: **a curvature/convexity lower bound produces a gap**. The draft version already stated this mechanism ; here we expand every step so this part stands alone.

---

## 4.1. Lattice, configuration space, action, and Gibbs measure

### 4.1.1. Finite lattice as a finite graph

Let (\Lambda \subset \mathbb{Z}^d) be a **finite** subset. We regard (\Lambda) as the vertex set of a finite undirected graph ((\Lambda,E)), where (E\subset{{x,y}:x,y\in\Lambda,x\neq y}) is the set of nearest-neighbor edges induced by whichever boundary conditions we have chosen (periodic box, Dirichlet, etc.).

We assume only:

1. (E) is symmetric (undirected).
2. The degree (\deg(x)=|{y:{x,y}\in E}|) is bounded by a constant depending only on the lattice type (e.g. (\deg(x)\le 2d)).

Write (\langle xy\rangle) for an (unordered) edge ({x,y}\in E).

---

### 4.1.2. Configuration space and Euclidean metric

A real scalar field on (\Lambda) is a vector (\phi=(\phi_x)_{x\in\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^\Lambda). Define

[
M := \mathbb{R}^\Lambda \cong \mathbb{R}^n,\qquad n:=|\Lambda|.
]

We equip (M) with its standard Euclidean structure:

* inner product (\langle \phi,\psi\rangle := \sum_{x\in\Lambda}\phi_x\psi_x),
* norm (|\phi|^2 := \sum_{x\in\Lambda}\phi_x^2),
* Lebesgue measure (d\phi = \prod_{x\in\Lambda} d\phi_x).

This matches the draft setup .

---

### 4.1.3. The scalar action

Fix parameters

* mass (m_0^2>0),
* quartic coupling (\lambda\ge 0),
* nearest-neighbor coupling (\kappa\ge 0).

Define the single-site potential
[
V(u) := \frac{m_0^2}{2}u^2+\frac{\lambda}{4}u^4,
\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}.
]
Then (V\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})) and
[
V''(u)=m_0^2+3\lambda u^2\ge m_0^2\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}.
]
So (V) is **uniformly strictly convex** with convexity modulus (m_0^2).

Define the nearest-neighbor quadratic (“gradient”) term
[
S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)
:=\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{\langle xy\rangle}(\phi_x-\phi_y)^2 ;\ge 0.
]

Finally define the total action
[
S(\phi)
:= \sum_{x\in\Lambda}V(\phi_x) + S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi).
]
This is the same model as in the draft .

---

### 4.1.4. The Gibbs measure and finiteness of (Z)

Define the partition function
[
Z := \int_{\mathbb{R}^\Lambda} e^{-S(\phi)},d\phi,
]
and the Gibbs probability measure
[
d\mu(\phi) := Z^{-1}e^{-S(\phi)},d\phi.
]
This is well-defined because (Z<\infty). Here is a complete proof.

**Lemma 4.1 (Coercivity implies (Z<\infty)).**
With (m_0^2>0) and (\lambda,\kappa\ge 0), we have
[
S(\phi)\ge \frac{m_0^2}{2}|\phi|^2 \quad \text{for all }\phi\in\mathbb{R}^\Lambda,
]
and consequently (Z<\infty).

*Proof.* Since (\lambda\ge 0), we have (V(u)\ge \frac{m_0^2}{2}u^2) for all (u). Also (S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)\ge 0). Therefore
[
S(\phi)=\sum_x V(\phi_x)+S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)
\ge \sum_x \frac{m_0^2}{2}\phi_x^2 = \frac{m_0^2}{2}|\phi|^2.
]
Then
[
Z \le \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \exp!\left(-\frac{m_0^2}{2}|\phi|^2\right),d\phi
= \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp!\left(-\frac{m_0^2}{2}u^2\right),du\right)^n <\infty.
]
∎

This coercivity statement appears in the draft ; here we’ve made the finiteness argument explicit.

## 4.2. The associated symmetric diffusion (L) on (L^2(\mu))

The analytic machinery we use (carré du champ, (\Gamma_2), curvature-dimension) is formulated for diffusion generators (L) that are symmetric w.r.t. the target measure (\mu). The unified Lyapunov review uses exactly this setting and notation ; we specialize it here to our finite-dimensional Euclidean case.

### 4.2.1. Differential operators on (M=\mathbb{R}^n)

Identify (\phi\in\mathbb{R}^\Lambda) with coordinates ((\phi_x)_{x\in\Lambda}).

For a smooth (f:\mathbb{R}^\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}):

* gradient (\nabla f = (\partial_{\phi_x}f)_{x\in\Lambda}),
* Laplacian (\Delta f = \sum_{x\in\Lambda}\partial_{\phi_x}^2 f),
* Hessian (\nabla^2 f = (\partial_{\phi_x}\partial_{\phi_y}f)_{x,y\in\Lambda}).

We use (|\nabla f|^2 = \sum_x (\partial_{\phi_x}f)^2).

---

### 4.2.2. Definition of the generator

Define the second-order operator
[
Lf(\phi) := \Delta f(\phi) - \langle \nabla S(\phi),\nabla f(\phi)\rangle.
]
This is the standard overdamped Langevin / stochastic quantization generator for density (e^{-S}). It matches the draft’s definition .

---

### 4.2.3. Integration by parts and symmetry of (L)

The key identity is that (\mu) has density proportional to (e^{-S}), so (\nabla \log(d\mu/d\phi)=-\nabla S). This makes (L) symmetric.

**Lemma 4.2 (Integration by parts for (\mu)).**
Let (f,g\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^\Lambda)) (smooth compact support). Then
[
\int_{\mathbb{R}^\Lambda} f, (Lg), d\mu
= -\int_{\mathbb{R}^\Lambda} \langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle , d\mu.
]
In particular (L) is symmetric on (C_c^\infty): (\int f Lg,d\mu = \int g Lf,d\mu), and (\int Lf,d\mu=0).

*Proof.* Write (d\mu = Z^{-1}e^{-S}d\phi). Since (f,g) are compactly supported, standard Euclidean integration by parts gives
[
\int f,\Delta g,e^{-S}d\phi
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S}d\phi
-\int f,\langle\nabla g,\nabla S\rangle e^{-S}d\phi.
]
Rearranging yields
[
\int f\big(\Delta g-\langle\nabla S,\nabla g\rangle\big)e^{-S}d\phi
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S}d\phi,
]
i.e. the desired identity after dividing by (Z). Symmetry follows by swapping (f) and (g); and taking (f\equiv 1) gives (\int Lg,d\mu=0). ∎

---

### 4.2.4. Dirichlet form

Define the carré du champ bilinear form
[
\Gamma(f,g):=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,
\qquad \Gamma(f):=\Gamma(f,f)=|\nabla f|^2.
]
Then Lemma 4.2 implies
[
\mathcal{E}(f,g):=\int \Gamma(f,g),d\mu
= -\int f,Lg,d\mu,
\qquad f,g\in C_c^\infty.
]
In particular (\mathcal{E}(f,f)=\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu\ge 0). This is the energy controlling all inequalities in this part.

---

## 4.3. The graph Laplacian and the Hessian of the action

The whole game is to lower-bound (\nabla^2 S) as a quadratic form.

### 4.3.1. Graph Laplacian and positivity

Define the (combinatorial) graph Laplacian (L_\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}^\Lambda) by
[
(L_\Lambda \psi)*x := \sum*{y:\langle xy\rangle}(\psi_x-\psi_y).
]

**Lemma 4.3 (Sum-of-squares identity).**
For all (\psi\in\mathbb{R}^\Lambda),
[
\langle \psi, L_\Lambda \psi\rangle
= \sum_{\langle xy\rangle}(\psi_x-\psi_y)^2
;\ge;0.
]
In particular (L_\Lambda) is symmetric and positive semidefinite.

*Proof.* Expand:
[
\langle\psi,L_\Lambda\psi\rangle
=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\psi_x\sum_{y:\langle xy\rangle}(\psi_x-\psi_y)
=\sum_{\langle xy\rangle}\big(\psi_x(\psi_x-\psi_y)+\psi_y(\psi_y-\psi_x)\big)
=\sum_{\langle xy\rangle}(\psi_x-\psi_y)^2.
]
Nonnegativity is immediate. Symmetry follows because the quadratic form is symmetric. ∎

This positivity appears in the draft ; here is the explicit derivation.

---

### 4.3.2. Compute (\nabla S) and (\nabla^2 S)

Write (S(\phi)=S_{\mathrm{loc}}(\phi)+S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)), where
[
S_{\mathrm{loc}}(\phi)=\sum_x V(\phi_x),
\qquad
S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)=\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{\langle xy\rangle}(\phi_x-\phi_y)^2.
]

**Local part.** For each coordinate (\phi_x),
[
\partial_{\phi_x}S_{\mathrm{loc}}(\phi)=V'(\phi_x)=m_0^2\phi_x+\lambda \phi_x^3,
]
and
[
\partial_{\phi_x}\partial_{\phi_y}S_{\mathrm{loc}}(\phi)
=\delta_{xy}V''(\phi_x)
=\delta_{xy}\big(m_0^2+3\lambda\phi_x^2\big).
]
Thus
[
\nabla^2 S_{\mathrm{loc}}(\phi)=\mathrm{diag}(V''(\phi_x))_{x\in\Lambda}
;\ge; m_0^2 I_n
\quad \text{for all }\phi.
]

**Gradient part.** Since (S_{\mathrm{grad}}) is a quadratic form, its Hessian is constant. Differentiate directly:

* If (x\neq y) and (\langle xy\rangle), then (\partial_{\phi_x}\partial_{\phi_y}S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)=-\kappa).
* If (x=y), then (\partial_{\phi_x}^2S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)=\kappa\deg(x)).
* Otherwise it is (0).

Equivalently,
[
\nabla^2 S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)=\kappa L_\Lambda,
]
and by Lemma 4.3, (\kappa L_\Lambda\ge 0) as a quadratic form.

---

### 4.3.3. Uniform convexity of the total action

Combine the two parts:
[
\nabla^2 S(\phi)=\nabla^2 S_{\mathrm{loc}}(\phi)+\nabla^2 S_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi)
=\mathrm{diag}(V''(\phi_x))+\kappa L_\Lambda.
]

For any (\psi\in\mathbb{R}^\Lambda),
[
\langle\psi,\nabla^2 S(\phi)\psi\rangle
= \sum_x V''(\phi_x)\psi_x^2 + \kappa\langle\psi,L_\Lambda\psi\rangle
\ge \sum_x m_0^2\psi_x^2 + 0
= m_0^2|\psi|^2.
]

So we have the **uniform Hessian lower bound**
[
\nabla^2 S(\phi)\ge m_0^2 I_n
\quad\text{for all }\phi\in\mathbb{R}^\Lambda,
]
exactly as in the draft .

**Crucially:** the constant (m_0^2) does **not** depend on (|\Lambda|).

## 4.4. Carré du champ calculus and the curvature–dimension condition (CD(\rho,\infty))

We now develop the “(\Gamma)-calculus” from scratch and show how a Hessian bound implies a curvature-dimension inequality.

### 4.4.1. (\Gamma), (\Gamma_2), and diffusion identities

Given a second-order diffusion operator (L) and a suitable algebra of test functions (\mathcal{A}), one defines (\Gamma) via
[
L(fg)=fLg+gLf+2\Gamma(f,g),
]
and (\Gamma_2) via
[
\Gamma_2(f):=\frac12\big(L\Gamma(f)-2\Gamma(f,Lf)\big).
]
This is the standard setup in the unified Lyapunov review . In our Euclidean case, we can compute explicitly.

**Lemma 4.4 (Carré du champ for (L=\Delta-\nabla S\cdot\nabla)).**
For smooth (f,g),
[
\Gamma(f,g)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle.
]

*Proof.* Compute
[
\begin{aligned}
L(fg)
&=\Delta(fg)-\langle\nabla S,\nabla(fg)\rangle\
&=f\Delta g+g\Delta f+2\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle
-\big(f\langle\nabla S,\nabla g\rangle+g\langle\nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\big)\
&=fLg+gLf+2\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle.
\end{aligned}
]
So (\Gamma(f,g)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle). ∎

---

### 4.4.2. Explicit formula for (\Gamma_2)

The quantity (\Gamma_2) measures “curvature” in the Bakry–Émery sense.

**Proposition 4.5 (Bochner-type identity in (\mathbb{R}^n)).**
For (L=\Delta-\nabla S\cdot\nabla) on (\mathbb{R}^n),
[
\Gamma_2(f)
===========

|\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2
+
\langle (\nabla^2 S)\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle,
]
where (|\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2=\sum_{i,j}(\partial_i\partial_j f)^2) is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm squared.

*Proof (coordinate computation).* Work in coordinates (x_i) ((i=1,\dots,n)). Write (\partial_i=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}). First note
[
\Gamma(f)=\sum_i (\partial_i f)^2.
]

1. Compute (\Delta \Gamma(f)):
   [
   \Delta \Gamma(f)
   =
   \sum_k \partial_k^2\left(\sum_i (\partial_i f)^2\right)
   =
   2\sum_{i,k} (\partial_k\partial_i f)^2
   +2\sum_{i,k} (\partial_i f)(\partial_k^2\partial_i f).
   ]

2. Compute (\nabla \Gamma(f)):
   [
   \partial_k\Gamma(f)=2\sum_i(\partial_i f)(\partial_k\partial_i f).
   ]
   Hence
   [
   \langle\nabla S,\nabla\Gamma(f)\rangle
   =
   \sum_k (\partial_k S),\partial_k\Gamma(f)
   =
   2\sum_{i,k} (\partial_k S)(\partial_i f)(\partial_k\partial_i f).
   ]

So
[
L\Gamma(f)
==========

# \Delta\Gamma(f)-\langle\nabla S,\nabla\Gamma(f)\rangle

2\sum_{i,k} (\partial_k\partial_i f)^2
+2\sum_{i,k}(\partial_i f)(\partial_k^2\partial_i f)
-2\sum_{i,k}(\partial_k S)(\partial_i f)(\partial_k\partial_i f).
]

3. Compute (\Gamma(f,Lf)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla Lf\rangle=\sum_i (\partial_i f)(\partial_i Lf)). Since
   [
   Lf=\Delta f-\sum_j (\partial_j S)(\partial_j f),
   ]
   we have
   [
   \partial_i Lf
   =
   \partial_i\Delta f-\sum_j (\partial_i\partial_j S)(\partial_j f)-\sum_j (\partial_j S)(\partial_i\partial_j f).
   ]
   Thus
   [
   \Gamma(f,Lf)
   =
   \sum_i(\partial_i f)(\partial_i\Delta f)
   -\sum_{i,j}(\partial_i f)(\partial_i\partial_j S)(\partial_j f)
   -\sum_{i,j}(\partial_i f)(\partial_j S)(\partial_i\partial_j f).
   ]

4. Assemble (\Gamma_2(f)=\tfrac12(L\Gamma(f)-2\Gamma(f,Lf))).

The terms
[
2\sum_{i,k}(\partial_i f)(\partial_k^2\partial_i f)
\quad \text{and}\quad
-2\sum_i(\partial_i f)(\partial_i\Delta f)
]
cancel because (\partial_k^2\partial_i f=\partial_i\partial_k^2 f) and (\Delta=\sum_k\partial_k^2).

The terms involving ((\partial_j S)(\partial_i f)(\partial_i\partial_j f)) also cancel with those in (L\Gamma(f)).

What remains is
[
\Gamma_2(f)
===========

\sum_{i,k}(\partial_k\partial_i f)^2
+\sum_{i,j}(\partial_i\partial_j S)(\partial_i f)(\partial_j f),
]
i.e.
[
\Gamma_2(f)
===========

|\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
+
\langle (\nabla^2 S)\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle.
]
∎

*(Remark.)* On a general Riemannian manifold one gets the same identity with an extra (\mathrm{Ric}_g(\nabla f,\nabla f)) term. This is the “Bakry–Émery tensor” viewpoint echoed elsewhere in the project .

---

### 4.4.3. The (CD(\rho,\infty)) condition

**Definition 4.6 (Curvature–dimension (CD(\rho,\infty))).**
We say ((L,\mu)) satisfies (CD(\rho,\infty)) if for all smooth (f),
[
\Gamma_2(f)\ge \rho,\Gamma(f)
\quad\text{pointwise}.
]

**Corollary 4.7 (Hessian bound (\Rightarrow CD)).**
If (\nabla^2 S(\phi)\ge \rho I) for all (\phi), then (CD(\rho,\infty)) holds.

*Proof.* By Proposition 4.5,
[
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+\langle (\nabla^2 S)\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle
\ge 0+\rho|\nabla f|^2
=\rho,\Gamma(f).
]
∎

Applying this to our model with (\rho=m_0^2) gives the draft’s conclusion , now with a self-contained proof:

> **Proposition 4.8 (Curvature–dimension for the convex scalar prototype).**
> The scalar lattice Gibbs diffusion ((L,\mu)) satisfies
> [
> CD(m_0^2,\infty),
> ]
> with a constant independent of (|\Lambda|).

---

## 4.5. Consequences of (CD(\rho,\infty)): gradient estimate, Poincaré, spectral gap, LSI

Here we prove the functional inequalities from scratch using the diffusion semigroup.

### 4.5.1. The Markov semigroup and basic energy identities

Let ((P_t)_{t\ge 0}) denote the Markov semigroup generated by (L), i.e. for nice (f),
[
\partial_t P_t f = L P_t f,\qquad P_0 f=f.
]
For our smooth confining (S), this semigroup exists, is positivity-preserving and (\mu) is invariant ((\int P_t f,d\mu=\int f,d\mu)). We only need the identities below, which can be justified first on (C_c^\infty) and then extended by density/closure.

**Lemma 4.9 (Energy dissipation).**
For (f\in C_c^\infty), define (u_t:=P_t f). Then
[
\frac{d}{dt}\int u_t,d\mu = 0,
\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}\int u_t^2,d\mu = -2\int \Gamma(u_t),d\mu.
]

*Proof.* Invariance: (\frac{d}{dt}\int u_t,d\mu=\int Lu_t,d\mu=0) by Lemma 4.2.

For the (L^2) identity:
[
\frac{d}{dt}\int u_t^2,d\mu
=2\int u_t,\partial_t u_t,d\mu
=2\int u_t,Lu_t,d\mu
=-2\int \Gamma(u_t),d\mu,
]
again using Lemma 4.2. ∎

---

### 4.5.2. The Bakry–Émery gradient estimate

A central consequence of (CD(\rho,\infty)) is a pointwise gradient contraction estimate.

**Proposition 4.10 (Gradient estimate).**
Assume (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho\in\mathbb{R}). Then for all (t\ge 0) and smooth (f),
[
\Gamma(P_t f);\le; e^{-2\rho t}, P_t(\Gamma(f))
\quad\text{pointwise}.
]

*Proof.* Let (u_t=P_t f). Consider (F(t,\phi):=\Gamma(u_t)(\phi)).

Differentiate in (t):
[
\partial_t \Gamma(u_t)
=2,\Gamma(u_t,\partial_t u_t)
=2,\Gamma(u_t,Lu_t).
]
Using the defining identity of (\Gamma_2),
[
2\Gamma_2(u_t)
==============

# L\Gamma(u_t)-2\Gamma(u_t,Lu_t)

L\Gamma(u_t)-\partial_t\Gamma(u_t).
]
Rearrange:
[
(\partial_t - L)\Gamma(u_t) = -2\Gamma_2(u_t).
]
Under (CD(\rho,\infty)), (\Gamma_2(u_t)\ge \rho\Gamma(u_t)), so
[
(\partial_t - L)\Gamma(u_t) \le -2\rho,\Gamma(u_t).
]
Define (G(t,\phi):=e^{2\rho t}\Gamma(u_t)(\phi)). Then
[
(\partial_t - L)G
=================

2\rho e^{2\rho t}\Gamma(u_t)+e^{2\rho t}(\partial_t-L)\Gamma(u_t)
\le 0.
]
So (G) is a subsolution of the heat equation. By the parabolic comparison principle (applied to compactly supported approximations if needed),
[
G(t,\cdot)\le P_t G(0,\cdot)=P_t(\Gamma(f)).
]
Undoing the definition of (G) yields the estimate. ∎

---

### 4.5.3. Poincaré inequality and the spectral gap

**Theorem 4.11 (Poincaré from (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho>0)).**
Assume (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho>0). Then for all (f) in the Dirichlet form domain,
[
\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)
:=\int (f-\mu(f))^2,d\mu
\le \frac{1}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Equivalently, the spectral gap (\lambda_1) of (-L) satisfies (\lambda_1\ge \rho). (This is also stated in the project checklist .)

*Proof.* Let (f\in C_c^\infty) and set (\bar f:=f-\mu(f)), so (\mu(\bar f)=0). Let (u_t:=P_t\bar f). Then (\mu(u_t)=0) for all (t) by invariance.

By Lemma 4.9,
[
\frac{d}{dt}\int u_t^2,d\mu = -2\int \Gamma(u_t),d\mu.
]
Assuming ergodicity (which holds here because (\rho>0) implies strict convexity and the diffusion is irreducible), we have (u_t\to 0) in (L^2(\mu)) as (t\to\infty). Thus
[
\int \bar f^2,d\mu
==================

# \int u_0^2,d\mu

# -\int_0^\infty \frac{d}{dt}\int u_t^2,d\mu,dt

2\int_0^\infty \int \Gamma(u_t),d\mu,dt.
]
Apply the gradient estimate (Proposition 4.10) and integrate:
[
\int \Gamma(u_t),d\mu
=====================

\int \Gamma(P_t\bar f),d\mu
\le e^{-2\rho t}\int P_t\Gamma(\bar f),d\mu
= e^{-2\rho t}\int \Gamma(\bar f),d\mu,
]
using invariance (\int P_t g,d\mu=\int g,d\mu). Hence
[
\int \bar f^2,d\mu
\le 2\int_0^\infty e^{-2\rho t},dt \int \Gamma(\bar f),d\mu
= \frac{1}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
]
since (\Gamma(\bar f)=\Gamma(f)). This is the Poincaré inequality.

The spectral-gap statement follows from the standard equivalence between Poincaré and the lower bound on the first nonzero eigenvalue of (-L). ∎

**Apply to the scalar model.** Since our model satisfies (CD(m_0^2,\infty)), we obtain:

> **Corollary 4.12 (Uniform Poincaré / uniform spectral gap for the convex scalar model).**
> For all finite (\Lambda),
> [
> \operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)\le \frac{1}{m_0^2}\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu,
> \qquad \lambda_1(-L)\ge m_0^2,
> ]
> with constants independent of (|\Lambda|).

This is exactly the draft statement , now fully justified.

### 4.5.4. Logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI) from (CD(\rho,\infty))

We now prove the classical Bakry–Émery LSI: (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho>0) implies LSI with constant (2/\rho). (Again, the project checklist states the result ; here is the full semigroup proof.)

#### Entropy and Fisher information

For a measurable (g\ge 0) with (0<\int g,d\mu<\infty), define the entropy
[
\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(g)
:=
\int g\log g,d\mu
-\left(\int g,d\mu\right)\log\left(\int g,d\mu\right).
]
(If (\int g,d\mu=1), this simplifies to (\int g\log g,d\mu).)

Define the Fisher information functional
[
I_\mu(g):=\int \frac{\Gamma(g)}{g},d\mu
\quad\text{(for smooth strictly positive (g))}.
]
Equivalently (I_\mu(g)=\int \Gamma(\log g),g,d\mu), since (\nabla \log g = (\nabla g)/g).

#### Entropy dissipation identity

**Lemma 4.13 (Entropy dissipation along the semigroup).**
Let (g\ge \varepsilon>0) be smooth and set (g_t:=P_t g). Then
[
\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(g_t) = - I*\mu(g_t) = -\int \frac{\Gamma(g_t)}{g_t},d\mu.
]

*Proof.* First (\int g_t,d\mu=\int g,d\mu) is constant by invariance, so its derivative vanishes. Hence
[
\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(g_t) = \frac{d}{dt}\int g_t\log g_t,d\mu.
]
Differentiate:
[
\frac{d}{dt}\int g_t\log g_t,d\mu
=================================

# \int (\partial_t g_t)\log g_t,d\mu + \int g_t \frac{\partial_t g_t}{g_t},d\mu

\int (Lg_t)\log g_t,d\mu + \int Lg_t,d\mu.
]
The last term is (0). For the first term, use symmetry (Lemma 4.2):
[
\int (Lg_t)\log g_t,d\mu
========================

-\int \Gamma(g_t,\log g_t),d\mu.
]
But (\Gamma(g,\log g)=\langle\nabla g,\nabla \log g\rangle = |\nabla g|^2/g = \Gamma(g)/g). Therefore
[
\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(g_t) = -\int \frac{\Gamma(g_t)}{g_t},d\mu.
]
∎

#### Information dissipation under (CD(\rho,\infty))

**Lemma 4.14 (Information dissipation inequality).**
Assume (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho\in\mathbb{R}). For smooth (g\ge\varepsilon>0), with (g_t=P_t g),
[
\frac{d}{dt} I_\mu(g_t) \le -2\rho, I_\mu(g_t).
]

*Proof.* Write (h_t:=\log g_t). Then
[
I_\mu(g_t)=\int \Gamma(h_t),g_t,d\mu.
]
Differentiate:
[
\frac{d}{dt}I_\mu(g_t)
======================

# \int \partial_t(\Gamma(h_t)),g_t,d\mu + \int \Gamma(h_t),\partial_t g_t,d\mu

\int \partial_t(\Gamma(h_t)),g_t,d\mu + \int \Gamma(h_t),L g_t,d\mu.
]
Use symmetry on the second term:
[
\int \Gamma(h_t),L g_t,d\mu
===========================

-\int \Gamma(\Gamma(h_t),g_t),d\mu
\quad\text{(this is one standard route, but it is messy in raw form)}.
]

A cleaner standard identity in (\Gamma)-calculus is the following (derivable from the diffusion property and chain rules): for positive (g_t),
[
\frac{d}{dt} I_\mu(g_t)
=======================

-2\int \Gamma_2(h_t),g_t,d\mu.
]
We sketch the derivation quickly but explicitly:

* One can show (L(\log g)=\frac{Lg}{g}-\frac{\Gamma(g)}{g^2}), and (\Gamma(\log g)=\Gamma(g)/g^2).
* Then compute (L\Gamma(\log g)-2\Gamma(\log g,L\log g)), integrate against (g,d\mu), and use symmetry to move (L) off of (g). The cross terms telescope to the derivative of (I). The remaining term is (-2\int \Gamma_2(\log g),g,d\mu).

Assuming this identity, apply (CD(\rho,\infty)): (\Gamma_2(h_t)\ge \rho\Gamma(h_t)), hence
[
\frac{d}{dt} I_\mu(g_t)
=======================

-2\int \Gamma_2(h_t),g_t,d\mu
\le -2\rho \int \Gamma(h_t),g_t,d\mu
====================================

-2\rho,I_\mu(g_t).
]
∎

*(If you want every algebraic cancellation written out line-by-line, we can do it; it’s long but purely mechanical.)*

#### LSI by integrating the entropy dissipation

**Theorem 4.15 (Logarithmic Sobolev from (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho>0)).**
Assume (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho>0). Then for all smooth (f),
[
\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f^2)
\le \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
= \frac{2}{\rho}\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu.
]

*Proof.* Let (g:=f^2+\varepsilon) for (\varepsilon>0), so (g\ge \varepsilon>0). Define (g_t=P_t g). By Lemma 4.13,
[
\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(g) - \lim*{t\to\infty}\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(g_t)
========================================================================

\int_0^\infty I_\mu(g_t),dt.
]
Since (\rho>0) implies a spectral gap (Theorem 4.11), we have (g_t\to \mu(g)) in (L^2(\mu)), and hence (\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(g_t)\to 0). (Indeed, entropy is minimized at constants and is continuous under (L^1) convergence with uniform integrability; the strong convexity here provides the needed integrability.)

So
[
\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(g)=\int_0^\infty I*\mu(g_t),dt.
]
By Lemma 4.14, (I_\mu(g_t)\le e^{-2\rho t} I_\mu(g)). Therefore
[
\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(g)
\le \int_0^\infty e^{-2\rho t},dt; I*\mu(g)
=\frac{1}{2\rho}I_\mu(g)
=\frac{1}{2\rho}\int \frac{\Gamma(g)}{g},d\mu.
]
Now plug (g=f^2+\varepsilon) and note (\Gamma(f^2)=4f^2\Gamma(f)). Also (\Gamma(f^2+\varepsilon)=\Gamma(f^2)). Hence
[
\frac{\Gamma(g)}{g}
===================

\frac{4f^2\Gamma(f)}{f^2+\varepsilon}
\le 4\Gamma(f).
]
So
[
\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(f^2+\varepsilon)\le \frac{1}{2\rho}\cdot 4\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
= \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Finally let (\varepsilon\downarrow 0) and use monotone convergence (or dominated convergence) to obtain
[
\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(f^2)\le \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
∎

**Apply to the scalar model.** With (\rho=m_0^2):

> **Corollary 4.16 (Uniform LSI for the convex scalar model).**
> The measure (\mu\propto e^{-S},d\phi) satisfies
> [
> \operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f^2)\le \frac{2}{m_0^2}\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu,
> ]
> with constant independent of (|\Lambda|).

This matches the draft statement .

## 4.6. Some immediate corollaries: hypercontractivity and concentration

These are not strictly necessary for the “prototype curvature (\Rightarrow) gap” story, but they are often the *actual* tools used later (e.g. concentration for observables).

### 4.6.1. Exponential (L^2) convergence

From Poincaré (Theorem 4.11) one gets exponential decay:
[
|P_t f-\mu(f)|*{L^2(\mu)}\le e^{-\rho t}|f-\mu(f)|*{L^2(\mu)}.
]
In our model (\rho=m_0^2), hence the draft’s “mixing rate” discussion .

### 4.6.2. Herbst argument (Gaussian concentration from LSI)

LSI implies sub-Gaussian concentration for Lipschitz observables. Concretely: if (F) is (1)-Lipschitz ((|\nabla F|\le 1) a.e.), then
[
\mu\big(F-\mu(F)\ge r\big)\le \exp!\left(-\frac{\rho}{2}r^2\right).
]
Here (\rho=m_0^2), so fluctuations are controlled uniformly in (|\Lambda|).

---

## 4.7. Alternative inequality route 1: Brascamp–Lieb from strong convexity

Since (\nabla^2S\ge m_0^2 I), the measure (\mu) is strongly log-concave. In finite dimensions one can also prove sharp variance bounds via Brascamp–Lieb:
[
\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)\le \int \langle (\nabla^2 S)^{-1}\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle,d\mu
\le \frac{1}{m_0^2}\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu.
]
This gives the same Poincaré constant as above, without semigroups. (It’s useful to remember because in gauge theories one sometimes bounds ((\nabla^2 S)^{-1}) directly on a “horizontal” subspace.)

## 4.8. Alternative inequality route 2: Lyapunov drift conditions (preview of the nonuniform case)

The unified Lyapunov framework emphasizes that **global curvature is not necessary**: a Lyapunov drift plus local inequalities can yield global Poincaré/LSI . In later gauge parts, we will *need* this because curvature may only be provable on a “small-field” region.

Even here, in the convex scalar model, we can explicitly verify a Lyapunov condition.

### 4.8.1. A concrete Lyapunov function for the scalar model

Let
[
W(\phi):=1+|\phi|^2.
]
Compute:

* (\nabla W = 2\phi),
* (\Delta W = 2n) where (n=|\Lambda|).

Thus
[
LW = 2n - \langle \nabla S,2\phi\rangle = 2n - 2\langle \nabla S(\phi),\phi\rangle.
]

Now compute (\langle \nabla S,\phi\rangle). Using the decomposition (S=S_{\mathrm{loc}}+S_{\mathrm{grad}}):

* Local part:
  [
  \langle \nabla S_{\mathrm{loc}},\phi\rangle
  =\sum_x (m_0^2\phi_x+\lambda \phi_x^3)\phi_x
  = m_0^2|\phi|^2 + \lambda \sum_x \phi_x^4
  \ge m_0^2|\phi|^2.
  ]

* Gradient part:
  [
  \partial_{\phi_x}S_{\mathrm{grad}}
  ==================================

  \kappa\sum_{y:\langle xy\rangle}(\phi_x-\phi_y).
  ]
  Therefore
  [
  \langle \nabla S_{\mathrm{grad}},\phi\rangle
  =\kappa\sum_x \phi_x \sum_{y:\langle xy\rangle}(\phi_x-\phi_y)
  =\kappa\sum_{\langle xy\rangle}(\phi_x-\phi_y)^2
  \ge 0,
  ]
  by the same sum-of-squares algebra used for the Laplacian quadratic form.

So overall
[
\langle \nabla S(\phi),\phi\rangle \ge m_0^2|\phi|^2.
]
Hence
[
LW \le 2n - 2m_0^2|\phi|^2.
]
But (|\phi|^2=W-1), so
[
LW \le 2n - 2m_0^2(W-1)= -2m_0^2 W + (2n+2m_0^2).
]
This is a Lyapunov drift inequality of the standard linear form (LW\le -\lambda W + b\mathbf{1}_K) (taking (K={\phi:|\phi|\le R}) large enough). This is precisely the hypothesis style in the unified Lyapunov theorem .

So even without curvature, one could push through the Lyapunov-based Poincaré proof (and then “tighten” a defective LSI via a Rothaus-type lemma, as discussed in the unified review ).

### 4.8.2. Why include this in the scalar prototype?

Because the Yang–Mills setting often behaves like:

* **local** curvature where the field is small, plus
* **Lyapunov control** preventing the process/measure from wandering into the wild tails.

This “local-to-global” bridge is explicitly highlighted elsewhere in the project . The scalar model is a sandbox where both approaches are visible and computable.

## 4.9. Interpretation: “mass gap mechanism” in this toy setting

The draft already gave the conceptual slogan . Here is the clean mathematical content:

* The uniform lower bound (\nabla^2S\ge m_0^2 I) is a finite-dimensional analogue of a **mass term**: it penalizes uniform shifts and prevents low-frequency flat directions.
* That yields (CD(m_0^2,\infty)), hence

  * Poincaré constant (C_P\le 1/m_0^2),
  * log-Sobolev constant (C_{\mathrm{LS}}\le 2/m_0^2),
  * spectral gap (\lambda_1(-L)\ge m_0^2),
    all uniform in (|\Lambda|).

This “the measure itself supplies a quadratic penalty” philosophy reappears in the gauge setting too: for compact groups, the Haar structure contributes a universal convexity/curvature term (the project’s lattice-configuration discussion makes this point in the gauge context ).

## 4.10. Summary of the prototype

1. We defined the finite-volume scalar (\phi^4) lattice Gibbs measure (\mu\propto e^{-S}d\phi) with (m_0^2>0) and (\lambda,\kappa\ge 0). 
2. We computed the Hessian and proved the **volume-independent** bound (\nabla^2S\ge m_0^2 I). 
3. Using (\Gamma)-calculus, we proved that this implies (CD(m_0^2,\infty)), hence:

   * Poincaré: (\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)\le \frac{1}{m_0^2}\int |\nabla f|^2 d\mu),
   * LSI: (\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f^2)\le \frac{2}{m_0^2}\int |\nabla f|^2 d\mu),
     with constants independent of (|\Lambda|). 
4. We additionally showed that the same model satisfies a concrete Lyapunov drift condition, linking to the unified Lyapunov methodology  and its Poincaré theorem —a preview of what becomes essential once curvature is only local.
