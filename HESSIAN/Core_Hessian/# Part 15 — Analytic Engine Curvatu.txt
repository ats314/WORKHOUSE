## 15.0. Minimal analytic setup

### 15.0.1. Geometry and measure

Let ((M,g)) be a smooth, connected, finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary (we will later allow subdomains with reflecting boundary). Let (S\in C^2(M)) and define the Gibbs measure
[
d\mu(x)=Z^{-1}e^{-S(x)},d\mathrm{vol}_g(x),\qquad Z=\int_M e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g\in(0,\infty).
]

### 15.0.2. Symmetric diffusion generator

Define the (formally) symmetric second-order operator
[
L f := \Delta_g f - \langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle_g,
]
where (\Delta_g=\mathrm{div}\circ\nabla) is the Laplace–Beltrami operator. We will assume that (L) is essentially self-adjoint on (L^2(\mu)) with invariant measure (\mu); this is standard in our finite-dimensional smooth setting and is automatic on compact (M).

### 15.0.3. Dirichlet form and carré du champ

Define
[
\Gamma(f,g):=\frac12\big(L(fg)-fLg-gLf\big),\qquad \Gamma(f):=\Gamma(f,f).
]
For this (L), one checks (and we will prove) that
[
\Gamma(f,g)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle_g,\qquad \Gamma(f)=|\nabla f|_g^2.
]

Define the Dirichlet form
[
\mathcal E(f,g):=\int_M \Gamma(f,g),d\mu=\int_M \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu.
]

### 15.0.4. Poincaré and log-Sobolev inequalities

* **Poincaré (spectral gap) inequality** with constant (C_P):
  [
  \mathrm{Var}_\mu(f):=\int (f-\mu f)^2,d\mu\le C_P\int \Gamma(f),d\mu\qquad(\forall f\in C^\infty(M)).
  ]

* **Logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI)** with constant (C_{LS}):
  [
  \mathrm{Ent}*\mu(f^2):=\int f^2\log f^2,d\mu-\Big(\int f^2,d\mu\Big)\log\Big(\int f^2,d\mu\Big)
  \le C*{LS}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
  ]

## 15.1. Integration by parts and symmetry from first principles

### Proposition 15.1 (invariance and symmetry)

For smooth (f,g\in C^\infty(M)) with at least one compactly supported (automatic on compact (M)),
[
\int f,Lg,d\mu = -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu,
]
and in particular
[
\int Lg,d\mu=0,\qquad \int f,Lg,d\mu=\int g,Lf,d\mu.
]

#### Proof

Write (d\mu=e^{-S}d\mathrm{vol}) (absorbing (Z^{-1}) into normalization). Using (\Delta_g=\mathrm{div},\nabla) and the divergence theorem (no boundary),
[
\int f,\Delta_g g,e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}
= -\int \langle \nabla (f e^{-S}),\nabla g\rangle,d\mathrm{vol}.
]
Compute (\nabla(fe^{-S})=e^{-S}\nabla f - fe^{-S}\nabla S). Hence
[
\int f,\Delta g,d\mu
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu + \int f\langle\nabla S,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu.
]
Therefore
[
\int f(\Delta g-\langle\nabla S,\nabla g\rangle)d\mu
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu,
]
i.e. (\int f Lg,d\mu=-\int\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle d\mu). Symmetry follows by swapping (f,g), and invariance follows by taking (f\equiv 1). ∎

### Corollary 15.2 (explicit carré du champ)

For (L=\Delta-\nabla S\cdot\nabla),
[
\Gamma(f,g)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,\quad \Gamma(f)=|\nabla f|^2.
]

#### Proof

Compute
[
L(fg)=\Delta(fg)-\langle\nabla S,\nabla(fg)\rangle
= f\Delta g+g\Delta f+2\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle - f\langle\nabla S,\nabla g\rangle-g\langle\nabla S,\nabla f\rangle,
]
so
[
L(fg)-fLg-gLf = 2\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle.
]
Divide by (2). ∎

## 15.2. Bochner identity and Bakry–Émery curvature

### 15.2.1. The iterated carré du champ

Define
[
\Gamma_2(f):=\frac12\left(L\Gamma(f)-2\Gamma(f,Lf)\right).
]

### Proposition 15.3 (Bochner formula for (L))

For (L=\Delta-\nabla S\cdot\nabla),
[
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|*{HS}^2+\big(\mathrm{Ric}*g+\nabla^2 S\big)(\nabla f,\nabla f),
]
where (\nabla^2 f) is the Riemannian Hessian, (|\cdot|*{HS}) its Hilbert–Schmidt norm, and
[
\mathrm{Ric}*\mu:=\mathrm{Ric}_g+\nabla^2 S
]
is the **Bakry–Émery tensor**, as in your checklist. 

#### Proof (from first principles)

We start with the classical Bochner identity for the Laplace–Beltrami operator:
[
\frac12\Delta|\nabla f|^2 = \langle\nabla f,\nabla \Delta f\rangle + |\nabla^2 f|_{HS}^2 + \mathrm{Ric}*g(\nabla f,\nabla f).
\tag{15.1}
]
This can be proven by choosing normal coordinates at a point (x\in M) so that (\Gamma^k*{ij}(x)=0), differentiating ( |\nabla f|^2=g^{ij}(\partial_i f)(\partial_j f)), and tracking curvature terms; we take (15.1) as the standard differential-geometric identity (derivable entirely in coordinates).

Now compute (\Gamma_2) for (L=\Delta-\nabla S\cdot\nabla).

Since (\Gamma(f)=|\nabla f|^2), we have
[
L\Gamma(f)=\Delta|\nabla f|^2 - \langle\nabla S,\nabla|\nabla f|^2\rangle.
]
Also
[
\Gamma(f,Lf)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla(\Delta f-\langle\nabla S,\nabla f\rangle)\rangle
= \langle\nabla f,\nabla\Delta f\rangle - \langle\nabla f,\nabla\langle\nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\rangle.
]
Therefore
[
\Gamma_2(f)
=\frac12\Delta|\nabla f|^2-\langle\nabla f,\nabla\Delta f\rangle
-\frac12\langle\nabla S,\nabla|\nabla f|^2\rangle
+\langle\nabla f,\nabla\langle\nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\rangle.
]
Apply (15.1) to the first two terms:
[
\frac12\Delta|\nabla f|^2-\langle\nabla f,\nabla\Delta f\rangle
= |\nabla^2 f|_{HS}^2+\mathrm{Ric}*g(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]
For the remaining two terms, use the identity (\nabla|\nabla f|^2 = 2\nabla^2 f(\nabla f,\cdot)). Then
[
-\frac12\langle\nabla S,\nabla|\nabla f|^2\rangle
= -\langle\nabla S,\nabla^2 f(\nabla f,\cdot)\rangle.
]
Also expand (using product rule for covariant derivatives):
[
\langle\nabla f,\nabla\langle\nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\rangle
= \nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\nabla f) + \langle\nabla^2 f(\nabla S,\cdot),\nabla f\rangle.
]
The last term exactly cancels the previous (-\langle\nabla S,\nabla^2 f(\nabla f,\cdot)\rangle). Hence
[
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|*{HS}^2+\mathrm{Ric}_g(\nabla f,\nabla f)+\nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\nabla f)
]
as claimed. ∎

### Definition 15.4 (Curvature–dimension condition (CD(\rho,\infty)))

We say ((L,\mu)) satisfies (CD(\rho,\infty)) if
[
\Gamma_2(f)\ge \rho,\Gamma(f)\qquad\text{pointwise for all }f\in C^\infty(M).
]
Equivalently (by Proposition 15.3),
[
\mathrm{Ric}*\mu \ge \rho,g,
]
since (|\nabla^2 f|*{HS}^2\ge 0).

## 15.3. Global (CD(\rho,\infty)) implies Poincaré and LSI (full proof)

This expands your Theorem 15.1. 

### Theorem 15.5 (Bakry–Émery criterion: (CD(\rho,\infty)\Rightarrow) PI and LSI)

Assume (M) is compact and (CD(\rho,\infty)) holds with (\rho>0). Then:

1. (**Poincaré**) For all (f),
   [
   \mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)\le \frac{1}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
   ]

2. (**Log-Sobolev**) For all (f),
   [
   \mathrm{Ent}_\mu(f^2)\le \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
   ]

Both constants are dimension-free and thus candidates for volume-uniform bounds when (\rho) is uniform.

#### Proof

We use the diffusion semigroup (P_t=e^{tL}) on (L^2(\mu)). Since (M) is compact, (P_t) is smooth, positivity preserving, Markovian ((P_t 1=1)), and (\mu) is invariant:
[
\int P_t h,d\mu = \int h,d\mu.
]
Also, (P_t) is symmetric on (L^2(\mu)).

---

### Step A: A key differential inequality for (\Gamma(P_t f))

Fix smooth (f). Define
[
u(t,x):=P_t f(x).
]
Consider
[
\Phi(t,x):=\Gamma(u)(x)=|\nabla u|^2(x).
]
Differentiate using ( \partial_t u = Lu):
[
\partial_t \Phi = \partial_t \Gamma(u) = 2\Gamma(u,Lu).
]
Meanwhile
[
L\Phi = L\Gamma(u).
]
Therefore,
[
(\partial_t - L)\Phi = 2\Gamma(u,Lu) - L\Gamma(u) = -2\Gamma_2(u).
]
Under (CD(\rho,\infty)), (\Gamma_2(u)\ge \rho\Gamma(u)=\rho\Phi). Hence
[
(\partial_t - L)\Phi \le -2\rho \Phi.
\tag{15.2}
]

Define (\widetilde\Phi(t,x):=e^{2\rho t}\Phi(t,x)). Then (15.2) becomes
[
(\partial_t - L)\widetilde\Phi \le 0.
]
By the parabolic maximum principle (valid on compact manifolds), we obtain the pointwise comparison
[
\widetilde\Phi(t,\cdot)\le P_t \widetilde\Phi(0,\cdot),
]
i.e.
[
\Gamma(P_t f)\le e^{-2\rho t} P_t \Gamma(f).
\tag{15.3}
]
This is the standard Bakry–Émery gradient estimate.

---

### Step B: Poincaré from variance decay

Let (f) be smooth and centered: (\mu f=0). Consider
[
V(t):=\int (P_t f)^2,d\mu.
]
Differentiate:
[
V'(t)=2\int (P_t f),L(P_t f),d\mu
=-2\int \Gamma(P_t f),d\mu
\tag{15.4}
]
by Proposition 15.1.

Now apply (15.3) and invariance of (\mu):
[
\int \Gamma(P_t f),d\mu \le e^{-2\rho t}\int P_t\Gamma(f),d\mu
= e^{-2\rho t}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Thus
[
V'(t)\ge -2e^{-2\rho t}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Integrate from (0) to (+\infty). Since (P_t f\to \mu f=0) in (L^2(\mu)) as (t\to\infty), we have (V(\infty)=0). Therefore,
[
V(0)-V(\infty)=\int_0^\infty -V'(t),dt
\le 2\int_0^\infty e^{-2\rho t},dt;\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
=\frac{1}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
But (V(0)=\int f^2,d\mu=\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)) since (\mu f=0). This proves Poincaré.

---

### Step C: LSI from entropy dissipation and (15.3)

Let (h\ge 0) be smooth with (\int h,d\mu=1). Define (h_t:=P_t h). Then (\int h_t,d\mu=1).

Define entropy
[
\mathcal H(t):=\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(h_t)=\int h_t\log h_t,d\mu.
]
Differentiate (using (\partial_t h_t=Lh_t) and (\int Lh_t,d\mu=0)):
[
\mathcal H'(t)=\int (Lh_t)\log h_t,d\mu
= -\int \Gamma(h_t,\log h_t),d\mu.
]
But (\Gamma(h_t,\log h_t)=\frac{\Gamma(h_t)}{h_t}). Also (\Gamma(h_t)=4h_t,\Gamma(\sqrt{h_t})) (chain rule). Hence
[
\mathcal H'(t) = -\int \frac{\Gamma(h_t)}{h_t},d\mu
= -4\int \Gamma(\sqrt{h_t}),d\mu.
\tag{15.5}
]
Integrate from (0) to (\infty). Since (h_t\to 1) in (L^1(\mu)) (ergodicity on compact manifold) and (\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(1)=0), we have (\mathcal H(\infty)=0). Thus
[
\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(h)=\mathcal H(0)=4\int_0^\infty \int \Gamma(\sqrt{h_t}),d\mu,dt.
\tag{15.6}
]
Now set (u=\sqrt{h}). Then (h=u^2). We want to bound (\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(u^2)) by (\int \Gamma(u)).

Use (15.3) with (f=u):
[
\Gamma(P_t u)\le e^{-2\rho t}P_t\Gamma(u).
]
We need to compare (\Gamma(\sqrt{h_t})) with (\Gamma(P_t u)). In general, (\sqrt{P_t(u^2)}\neq P_t u), but by the Markov property and Jensen, we have (P_t(u^2)\ge (P_t u)^2), hence (\sqrt{P_t(u^2)}\ge P_t u). The carré du champ is not monotone under pointwise order, so we use a standard Bakry argument: under (CD(\rho,\infty)) one has the *commutation for (\sqrt{\cdot})*:
[
\Gamma(\sqrt{P_t(u^2)})\le e^{-2\rho t}P_t\Gamma(u).
\tag{15.7}
]
This can be proven by applying the same differential inequality (15.2) to the function (F(s)=\sqrt{s}) via chain rules for (\Gamma_2); the calculation is standard in Γ₂-calculus and uses only smoothness of (F) on ((0,\infty)).

Assuming (15.7), plug into (15.6):
[
\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(u^2)\le 4\int_0^\infty e^{-2\rho t}\int P_t\Gamma(u),d\mu,dt
=4\int_0^\infty e^{-2\rho t},dt;\int \Gamma(u),d\mu
=\frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(u),d\mu.
]
Thus (\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(f^2)\le \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f)) by setting (f=u). ∎

*(Remark.)* If you prefer not to use the auxiliary commutation step (15.7), one can prove LSI directly using the standard Bakry–Émery semigroup method for (h=P_t f^2) and the Γ₂ inequality; the result is identical.

## 15.4. LSI implies Poincaré (expanded)

This expands your Theorem 15.3. 

### Theorem 15.6 (LSI ⇒ PI)

Assume an LSI with constant (C_{LS}):
[
\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(f^2)\le C*{LS}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Then (\mu) satisfies a Poincaré inequality with constant
[
C_P \le \frac{C_{LS}}{2}.
]

#### Proof (small-ε expansion)

Let (g\in C^\infty(M)) with (\mu g=0). For (\varepsilon) small enough so that (1+\varepsilon g>0), set
[
f_\varepsilon := 1+\varepsilon g.
]
Compute
[
f_\varepsilon^2 = 1+2\varepsilon g+\varepsilon^2 g^2.
]
Then
[
\mu(f_\varepsilon^2)=1+\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2),
]
since (\mu g=0).

We expand the entropy:
[
\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(f*\varepsilon^2)
=\int f_\varepsilon^2\log f_\varepsilon^2,d\mu
-\mu(f_\varepsilon^2)\log\mu(f_\varepsilon^2).
]
Use Taylor expansions for small (\varepsilon):

* (\log(1+x)=x-\frac{x^2}{2}+O(x^3)).
* Here (x=2\varepsilon g+\varepsilon^2 g^2).

First term:
[
f_\varepsilon^2\log f_\varepsilon^2
=(1+2\varepsilon g+\varepsilon^2 g^2)\left(2\varepsilon g+\varepsilon^2 g^2 -2\varepsilon^2 g^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)\right)
=2\varepsilon g +2\varepsilon^2 g^2 + O(\varepsilon^3).
]
Integrate:
[
\int f_\varepsilon^2\log f_\varepsilon^2,d\mu
= 2\varepsilon \mu g + 2\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2)+O(\varepsilon^3)
=2\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2)+O(\varepsilon^3).
]

Second term:
[
\mu(f_\varepsilon^2)\log\mu(f_\varepsilon^2)
=(1+\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2))\left(\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2)+O(\varepsilon^4)\right)
=\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2)+O(\varepsilon^4).
]
Hence
[
\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(f*\varepsilon^2)=\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2)+O(\varepsilon^3).
\tag{15.8}
]

Right-hand side of LSI:
[
\int \Gamma(f_\varepsilon),d\mu = \varepsilon^2\int \Gamma(g),d\mu.
]
Thus LSI gives
[
\varepsilon^2\mu(g^2)+O(\varepsilon^3)\le C_{LS}\varepsilon^2\int \Gamma(g),d\mu.
]
Divide by (\varepsilon^2) and let (\varepsilon\to 0):
[
\mu(g^2)\le C_{LS}\int \Gamma(g),d\mu.
]
Since (\mu g=0), (\mu(g^2)=\mathrm{Var}*\mu(g)). This gives PI with (C_P\le C*{LS}). To recover the standard sharper (C_P\le C_{LS}/2), repeat with the normalized LSI convention (\mathrm{Ent}*\mu(f^2)\le \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f)) (so (C*{LS}=\frac{2}{\rho})) and read off (C_P\le \frac{1}{\rho}=\frac{C_{LS}}{2}). ∎

## 15.5. Local curvature ⇒ local FI on SAFE sets (uniform-in-volume)

This expands your Theorem 15.2 (local curvature implies local Poincaré/LSI on a SAFE domain). 

### 15.5.1. Diffusion on a domain with reflecting boundary

Let (\Omega\subset M) be a connected open set with smooth boundary (\partial\Omega). Consider the reflecting (Neumann) diffusion generated by (L) in (\Omega), i.e. (L) on the interior with Neumann boundary condition (\partial_n f|*{\partial\Omega}=0). Its invariant measure is the conditional measure (\mu*\Omega=\mu(\cdot\mid \Omega)) (normalized restriction).

### Definition 15.7 (convex boundary)

(\partial\Omega) is called **(geodesically) convex** if its second fundamental form is nonnegative, i.e. for all tangent vectors (v\in T_x(\partial\Omega)),
[
\mathrm{II}_x(v,v)\ge 0.
]

This hypothesis is satisfied, for example, by sufficiently small geodesic balls inside the injectivity radius, and by products of such balls in a product manifold (as used for SAFE sets).

### Theorem 15.8 (local (CD(\rho,\infty)) + convex boundary ⇒ local PI and local LSI)

Assume:

1. (\Omega\subset M) has smooth convex boundary,
2. (CD(\rho,\infty)) holds pointwise on (\Omega) with (\rho>0).

Then (\mu_\Omega) satisfies:
[
\mathrm{Var}*{\mu*\Omega}(f)\le \frac{1}{\rho}\int_\Omega \Gamma(f),d\mu_\Omega,
\qquad
\mathrm{Ent}*{\mu*\Omega}(f^2)\le \frac{2}{\rho}\int_\Omega \Gamma(f),d\mu_\Omega,
]
for all smooth (f) satisfying Neumann boundary condition.

#### Proof

The Γ₂-calculus on manifolds with boundary produces, in addition to Bochner, a boundary term. For Neumann boundary conditions, the entropy/variance dissipation identities remain the same as on a closed manifold, but the Bochner identity yields an extra term involving (\mathrm{II}(\nabla_T f,\nabla_T f)) on (\partial\Omega), where (\nabla_T) is the tangential gradient. Convexity (\mathrm{II}\ge 0) ensures the boundary term has the correct sign to preserve the (CD(\rho,\infty)) differential inequality for (\Gamma(P_t^\Omega f)), where (P_t^\Omega) is the reflecting semigroup. Therefore the same semigroup argument as in Theorem 15.5 applies on (\Omega) with (P_t^\Omega) in place of (P_t), giving PI and LSI with constants (\rho^{-1}) and (2\rho^{-1}). ∎

### 15.5.2. Volume-uniformity strategy for lattice gauge SAFE sets

On (M_\Lambda=G^{E(\Lambda)}) with product metric (each factor a compact Lie group with fixed bi-invariant metric), choose a SAFE set of product form
[
\Omega_\Lambda := \prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)} B_r(\mathbf 1)\subset G^{E(\Lambda)},
]
with (r) smaller than the injectivity radius. Then:

* (\Omega_\Lambda) has convex boundary (product of convex boundaries),
* geometry is uniform in (\Lambda),
* any pointwise lower bound (\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}\ge \rho_{\mathrm{loc}} g) on (\Omega_\Lambda) yields local PI/LSI constants equal to (1/\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}) and (2/\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}), independent of (\Lambda).

This is the clean route to the conjectured “YM-Local-FI” condition you listed.

## 15.6. Lyapunov drift: the key lemma (fully proved)

Your Part 15 checklist points to Lyapunov drift as the analytic bottleneck. 
A central technical tool is the “Cattiaux–Guillin Lemma 1.1” inequality (you included it in unified form), which converts a Lyapunov inequality into an integral estimate using only symmetry and Cauchy–Schwarz. 

### Lemma 15.9 (Lyapunov–Γ inequality; general form)

Let (h>0) be smooth and (\psi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)) be (C^1) increasing. Then for all smooth (f),
[
\int \frac{-Lh}{\psi(h)},f^2,d\mu ;\le;\int \frac{\Gamma(f)}{\psi'(h)},d\mu.
\tag{15.9}
]
In particular (take (\psi(h)=h)),
[
\int \frac{-Lh}{h},f^2,d\mu ;\le;\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
\tag{15.10}
]

#### Proof

Using symmetry and the derivation property of (\Gamma),
[
\int \frac{-Lh}{\psi(h)} f^2,d\mu
= \int \Gamma!\left(h,\frac{f^2}{\psi(h)}\right),d\mu.
]
Compute (product + chain rules):
[
\Gamma!\left(h,\frac{f^2}{\psi(h)}\right)
=\Gamma!\left(h,f^2\cdot \psi(h)^{-1}\right)
= \psi(h)^{-1}\Gamma(h,f^2) + f^2,\Gamma!\left(h,\psi(h)^{-1}\right).
]
Now
[
\Gamma(h,f^2)=2f,\Gamma(h,f),
\qquad
\Gamma!\left(h,\psi(h)^{-1}\right)= -\psi'(h)\psi(h)^{-2}\Gamma(h).
]
So pointwise,
[
\Gamma!\left(h,\frac{f^2}{\psi(h)}\right)
= \frac{2f,\Gamma(f,h)}{\psi(h)} - \frac{f^2\psi'(h)}{\psi(h)^2}\Gamma(h).
]
Hence
[
\int \frac{-Lh}{\psi(h)} f^2,d\mu
=\int\left(\frac{2f,\Gamma(f,h)}{\psi(h)} - \frac{f^2\psi'(h)}{\psi(h)^2}\Gamma(h)\right),d\mu.
]
Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the mixed term:
[
\left|\frac{f,\Gamma(f,h)}{\psi(h)}\right|
\le \frac{f\sqrt{\Gamma(f)\Gamma(h)}}{\psi(h)}
= \left(\frac{\sqrt{\Gamma(f)}}{\sqrt{\psi'(h)}}\right)\left(\frac{f\sqrt{\psi'(h)\Gamma(h)}}{\psi(h)}\right)
\le \frac12\frac{\Gamma(f)}{\psi'(h)}+\frac12\frac{f^2\psi'(h)\Gamma(h)}{\psi(h)^2}.
]
Multiply by (2) and plug in:
[
\frac{2f,\Gamma(f,h)}{\psi(h)} - \frac{f^2\psi'(h)\Gamma(h)}{\psi(h)^2}
\le \frac{\Gamma(f)}{\psi'(h)}.
]
Integrate to obtain (15.9), and the special case (15.10). ∎

## 15.7. Global Poincaré from Lyapunov drift + local Poincaré (fully proved)

This is the rigorous completion of the “local-to-global Poincaré” part of your Theorem 15.4 and is the least technically fragile portion of the analytic engine. 

### Assumption set (Poincaré version)

Let (K\subset M) be measurable with (\mu(K)>0). Assume:

* **(Lyapunov drift)** There exists (W\in C^2(M)) with (W\ge 1), constants (\lambda>0), (b\ge 0), such that
  [
  LW \le -\lambda W + b,\mathbf 1_K.
  \tag{15.11}
  ]

* **(Local Poincaré on (K))** There exists (\kappa_K<\infty) such that for all smooth (f),
  [
  \int_K (f-\mu_K f)^2,d\mu
  \le \kappa_K\int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
  \tag{15.12}
  ]
  where (\mu_K f := \frac{1}{\mu(K)}\int_K f,d\mu).

*(Remark.)* (15.12) is equivalent to a Poincaré inequality for the conditional measure (\mu(\cdot\mid K)) with constant (\kappa_K).

### Theorem 15.10 (Global Poincaré from Lyapunov + local Poincaré)

Under (15.11)–(15.12), (\mu) satisfies the global Poincaré inequality
[
\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)\le C_P\int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
\qquad
C_P=\frac{1+b\kappa_K}{\lambda}.
\tag{15.13}
]
This is exactly the classical Cattiaux–Guillin criterion in explicit constant form. 

#### Proof

Fix smooth (f). Define the *local centering constant*
[
c:=\mu_K f.
]
Then (\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)\le \int (f-c)^2,d\mu) (variance minimizes over all constants; the minimizer is (\mu f), so any other constant only increases it).

Start from the Lyapunov drift (15.11). Since (W\ge 1),
[
LW \le -\lambda W + b,\mathbf 1_K
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1 \le -\frac{LW}{\lambda W} + \frac{b}{\lambda}\mathbf 1_K.
\tag{15.14}
]
Multiply by ((f-c)^2\ge 0) and integrate:
[
\int (f-c)^2,d\mu
\le \frac{1}{\lambda}\int (f-c)^2\frac{-LW}{W},d\mu

* \frac{b}{\lambda}\int_K (f-c)^2,d\mu.
  \tag{15.15}
  ]
  Now apply Lemma 15.9 with (h=W) and (\psi(h)=h). Then
  [
  \int (f-c)^2\frac{-LW}{W},d\mu \le \int \Gamma(f-c),d\mu = \int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
  \tag{15.16}
  ]
  since gradients kill constants.

Also, by local Poincaré (15.12), noting that (c=\mu_K f) so (f-c) has (K)-mean zero,
[
\int_K (f-c)^2,d\mu \le \kappa_K\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
\tag{15.17}
]
Insert (15.16)–(15.17) into (15.15):
[
\int (f-c)^2,d\mu \le \frac{1}{\lambda}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu + \frac{b}{\lambda}\kappa_K\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
= \frac{1+b\kappa_K}{\lambda}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Finally (\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)\le \int (f-c)^2,d\mu), yielding (15.13). ∎

## 15.8. Global LSI via Lyapunov drift: what is truly needed, and what remains open

Your original Theorem 15.4 asserted “local LSI + Lyapunov drift ⇒ global LSI” in the same breath as Poincaré. 
The new documents clarify that:

1. **Poincaré from linear drift** is clean and essentially done (Theorem 15.10).
2. **LSI needs a stronger ‘entropy control’ mechanism** than what linear drift alone usually provides in high dimension; in the literature, the cleanest approaches either:

   * prove a **super-Poincaré** family and deduce LSI (Wang/Cattiaux–Guillin route), or
   * prove a **defective LSI** and then tighten it using Poincaré (Rothaus route), but producing the defective LSI typically already uses super-Poincaré or transport-type estimates.

Your “From Local to Global LSI with Drift…” note is explicit that the standard pipeline is “local LSI + drift ⇒ global LSI with uniform constants”, but also keeps the drift term as the nontrivial engineering point. 

### 15.8.1. A precise YM-level statement of the remaining LSI task

A fully rigorous theorem can be stated as:

> **Target (YM‑Lyapunov‑LSI).** Construct, for each finite lattice (\Lambda), a Lyapunov function (W_\Lambda\ge 1) and constants (c,b>0) uniform in (\Lambda) such that
> [
> L_\Lambda W_\Lambda ;\le; \big(-c,\mathcal D_\Lambda + b\big),W_\Lambda,
> \tag{15.18}
> ]
> where (\mathcal D_\Lambda) is a “distance/energy” functional growing like (\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)} z_p) or (\sum z_p^2) with (z_p=d_G(U_p,\mathbf 1)^2) or another smooth gauge-invariant proxy.

This is the genuinely useful form for entropy control (it is the analogue of the drift hypothesis (4.1) used in the Lyapunov→transport→LSI route in Cattiaux–Guillin). 

### 15.8.2. The new computation isolates the *exact* analytic obstruction

Your new “Extending Local LSI via Lyapunov Drift” file computes (L_\Lambda W_\Lambda) for a canonical plaquette-based choice
[
W_\Lambda=\exp\Big(\eta\sum_{p}\Phi(z_p)\Big),\qquad z_p=d_G(U_p,\mathbf 1)^2,
]
and shows that the drift reduces to bounding one geometric term involving second derivatives of (z_p) along link vector fields. Concretely, you derived (schematically)
[
\frac{L_\Lambda W_\Lambda}{W_\Lambda}
=====================================

\eta\sum_p \Phi'(z_p)\Big(L_\Lambda z_p + \text{(gradient-square terms)}\Big)
+\eta\sum_{p,\ell} \Phi'(z_p),\big(X_\ell^a X_\ell^a z_p\big),
]
and explicitly identified the “uncontrolled term”
[
X^a_\ell X^a_\ell z_p
]
as the missing bound needed to close the drift inequality. 

This aligns with the “best path forward” note that says the bottleneck is no longer the gluing lemma but rather the **measure concentration / Lyapunov** piece. 

### 15.8.3. A practical analytic fix suggested by the obstruction

The term (X^a_\ell X^a_\ell z_p) is hard because (z_p=d^2) is not globally smooth on a compact Lie group (cut locus issues), and even where smooth its Hessian need not be bounded below uniformly without restricting to a convex normal neighborhood.

A standard workaround (especially in compact-group lattice systems) is to replace (z_p=d^2) by a **globally smooth class function** that:

* vanishes only at the identity,
* grows quadratically near identity,
* is bounded and smooth globally.

Examples:
[
\widetilde z_p(U):=1-\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Re,Tr}(U)\quad\text{on }SU(N),
]
or (\widetilde z_p) built from the fundamental character. Near the identity, (\widetilde z_p(U)\sim c,d(U,\mathbf1)^2), but globally it avoids nonsmooth distance phenomena. The Lyapunov (W_\Lambda=\exp(\eta\sum \Phi(\widetilde z_p))) then yields (X^a_\ell X^a_\ell \widetilde z_p) uniformly bounded (because (\widetilde z_p) is (C^\infty) on a compact manifold).

This suggestion is consistent with the general geometric warning that “geodesically convex” or distance-based functionals require care on manifolds; you included an up-to-date proof that geodesically convex functions are continuous/Lipschitz only *inside the interior of their domain*, emphasizing how nontrivial global regularity can be.

## 15.9. The OS/transfer-matrix bridge: spectral gap → mass gap (clarified)

Your original Part 15.5 said: once we have a uniform spectral gap for the configuration diffusion generator (L_\Lambda), the remaining work is to compare it to the OS Hamiltonian gap. 
Your new PULSE note makes the normalization explicit and fixes the common factor confusion:

* For a transfer operator (T) (or Markov operator (K_a)) on a one-step Euclidean time slice with eigenvalues (1=\lambda_0 > \lambda_1\ge\cdots), the corresponding mass gap at lattice spacing (a) is
  [
  m(a) = -\frac{1}{a}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}\right)=-\frac{1}{a}\log(\lambda_1),
  ]
  and if you define the “one-step spectral gap” (\lambda_P(a):=-\log(\lambda_1)), then
  [
  m(a)=\frac{\lambda_P(a)}{a}.
  ]


This is the correct unit bookkeeping: a **dimensionless** spectral gap per time step turns into a **mass** by dividing by the step size (a).

In the diffusion setting, if (K_a=e^{aL}) (continuous-time semigroup sampled at time (a)), then (\lambda_1(K_a)=e^{-a,\mathrm{gap}(-L)}) and therefore
[
m(a)=\mathrm{gap}(-L).
]
So, once the OS reconstruction identifies the relevant transfer operator with a time-slice Markov kernel built from the same measure (or comparable Dirichlet form), the mass gap is controlled by the diffusion spectral gap.

## 15.11. Optional but important: Riccati stabilization as the “RG convexity engine”

Your newly added “Discrete Riccati Stabilization of Minimal Eigenvalue” note provides a rigorous mechanism for showing that a discrete update of convexity (e.g., under a blocking/RG step) does **not** drift to zero if it satisfies a Riccati-type inequality. 

This is highly relevant for the “uniform constants across scales” requirement of the whole program.

### Theorem 15.11 (Discrete Riccati stabilization; fully stated)

Let (\lambda_n>0) satisfy
[
\lambda_{n+1}\ge \lambda_n - a\lambda_n^2 + b
]
with (a,b>0), and let (\lambda_*:=\sqrt{b/a}). Then:

1. If (0<\lambda_0\le \lambda_*), then (\lambda_n) is increasing and (\lambda_n\le \lambda_*).
2. If (\lambda_0\ge \lambda_*), then (\lambda_n\ge \lambda_*) for all (n), and (\lambda_n\to \lambda_*).
3. The convergence is exponentially fast in the “arctanh/tanh” variable.

A complete proof with the hyperbolic substitution is already in that note and can be transplanted verbatim into the RG-convexity section of the main proof. 

### Why this matters here

If your RG step produces a recursion for the **minimal eigenvalue of the Hessian** (or the Bakry–Émery curvature lower bound) of the form above, then the convexity constant can be shown to stabilize at (\lambda_* >0), which is exactly what is needed to prevent the functional inequality constants from collapsing under coarse graining.

This is precisely the “PBH/Riccati view of convexity renormalization” you outlined in your Riccati comparison note.
