Part 4 — Wilson plaquette action: second variation at the vacuum and the discrete Maxwell operator

### 4.1. Lattice cells and configuration manifold

Let (\Lambda\subset \mathbb{Z}^d) be a finite lattice region. Fix:

* a set (E(\Lambda)) of **oriented edges** (links), written (\ell=(x,\mu)) for the oriented link from (x) to (x+\hat\mu);
* a set (P(\Lambda)) of **oriented plaquettes**, written (p=(x;\mu,\nu)) with (\mu<\nu), oriented counterclockwise in the ((\mu,\nu))-plane.

Let (G) be a compact Lie group and (\mathfrak g) its Lie algebra. Fix an (\mathrm{Ad})-invariant inner product (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}) on (\mathfrak g). (For (G=\mathrm{SU}(N)) we will specialize to (\langle A,B\rangle_\lambda:=\lambda(-\mathrm{Tr}(AB))) for some (\lambda>0).)

Define the configuration manifold
[
M_\Lambda ;:=; G^{E(\Lambda)} ,
]
whose elements are link fields (U=(U_\ell)_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)}).

For an oriented plaquette (p=(x;\mu,\nu)), define its **plaquette holonomy**
[
U_p ;:=; U_{x,\mu},U_{x+\hat\mu,\nu},U_{x+\hat\nu,\mu}^{-1},U_{x,\nu}^{-1}.
\tag{4.1}
]
Define the vacuum configuration (U^{(0)}\in M_\Lambda) by
[
U^{(0)}_\ell = e \quad \forall \ell\in E(\Lambda).
\tag{4.2}
]

We equip each (G) factor with the bi-invariant Riemannian metric induced by (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}), and (M_\Lambda) with the product metric. In particular, for any (X\in \mathfrak g), the curve (t\mapsto \exp(tX)) is a geodesic in (G) through (e).

---

### 4.2. Tangent identification at the vacuum

Identify
[
T_{U^{(0)}}M_\Lambda ;\cong; \mathcal{C}^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)
]
by assigning to a tangent vector its linkwise components (X=(X_\ell)*{\ell\in E(\Lambda)}\in \mathfrak g^{E(\Lambda)}), and representing the corresponding geodesic through (U^{(0)}) by
[
U(t)=\exp(tX)\quad\text{meaning}\quad U*\ell(t)=\exp(tX_\ell)\quad(\ell\in E(\Lambda)).
\tag{4.3}
]

We use the (\ell^2) inner products on cochains induced from (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}):
[
\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathcal C^1}:=\sum_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)}\langle X_\ell,Y_\ell\rangle_{\mathfrak g},
\qquad
\langle \omega,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal C^2}:=\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\langle \omega_p,\eta_p\rangle_{\mathfrak g}.
\tag{4.4}
]

---

### 4.3. The discrete curl (d_1)

Define the coboundary operator
[
d_1:\mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)\to \mathcal C^2(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)
]
by the signed sum around an oriented plaquette. For (p=(x;\mu,\nu)),
[
(d_1X)*p ;:=; X*{x,\mu} + X_{x+\hat\mu,\nu} - X_{x+\hat\nu,\mu} - X_{x,\nu}.
\tag{4.5}
]
This is the discrete analogue of (\mathrm{curl}).

Let (d_1^*) denote the adjoint of (d_1) with respect to the inner products (4.4), i.e.
[
\langle d_1X,\omega\rangle_{\mathcal C^2}=\langle X,d_1^*\omega\rangle_{\mathcal C^1}.
\tag{4.6}
]
Then (d_1^*d_1:\mathcal C^1\to\mathcal C^1) is positive semidefinite.

---

### 4.4. Linearization of plaquette holonomy

The first structural fact is: the plaquette holonomy linearizes to (d_1).

**Lemma 4.1 (Plaquette holonomy linearization).**
Let (X\in\mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)) and (U(t)=\exp(tX)) as in (4.3). For each plaquette (p),
[
U_p(t) ;=; \exp!\Big(t(d_1X)*p ;+; t^2 R_p(t)\Big),
\tag{4.7}
]
where (R_p(t)\in\mathfrak g) depends smoothly on (t) and satisfies a uniform bound
[
|R_p(t)|;\le; C,|X|*\infty^2
\quad\text{for all }|t|\le t_0,
\tag{4.8}
]
for some (t_0>0) and (C<\infty) depending only on (G) and the chosen norm on (\mathfrak g) (not on (\Lambda)).

*Proof.* Fix a plaquette (p=(x;\mu,\nu)). Writing its oriented boundary links as ((e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4)) in order, we have
[
U_p(t)=\exp(tX_{e_1})\exp(tX_{e_2})\exp(-tX_{e_3})\exp(-tX_{e_4}).
\tag{4.9}
]
Near the identity, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula gives a smooth map (\mathrm{BCH}(\cdot,\cdot)) with
[
\exp(A)\exp(B)=\exp(\mathrm{BCH}(A,B)),\qquad \mathrm{BCH}(A,B)=A+B+\mathcal O(|A||B|).
\tag{4.10}
]
Apply this iteratively to the four factors in (4.9), with (A,B=\mathcal O(t|X|*\infty)). One obtains
[
U_p(t)=\exp!\Big(t(X*{e_1}+X_{e_2}-X_{e_3}-X_{e_4}) + t^2 R_p(t)\Big)
]
for a smooth remainder (R_p(t)) satisfying (4.8). The linear term is exactly ((d_1X)_p) by definition (4.5). ∎

---

### 4.5. Wilson plaquette action and its Taylor expansion at (e)

From here on, specialize to (G=\mathrm{SU}(N)). Define the standard Wilson single-plaquette potential
[
\Phi(U);:=;\frac{\beta}{N},\mathrm{Re,Tr}(I-U),
\qquad U\in \mathrm{SU}(N),
\tag{4.11}
]
and the Wilson plaquette action
[
S_W(U);:=;\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)} \Phi(U_p).
\tag{4.12}
]

We now record the exact quadratic term of (\Phi(\exp Y)) at (Y=0).

**Lemma 4.2 (Second order expansion of (\mathrm{Re,Tr}(I-\exp Y))).**
Let (Y\in\mathfrak{su}(N)). Then as (Y\to 0),
[
\mathrm{Re,Tr}(I-e^Y);=; -\frac12,\mathrm{Tr}(Y^2);+;\mathcal O(|Y|^3).
\tag{4.13}
]
Equivalently, if we use the inner product (\langle A,B\rangle_\lambda:=\lambda(-\mathrm{Tr}(AB))) and norm (|A|*\lambda^2=\langle A,A\rangle*\lambda), then
[
\mathrm{Re,Tr}(I-e^Y);=;\frac{1}{2\lambda},|Y|*\lambda^2;+;\mathcal O(|Y|*\lambda^3).
\tag{4.14}
]

*Proof.* Use the power series (e^Y = I + Y + \tfrac12Y^2 + \tfrac16Y^3 + \cdots). Since (Y\in\mathfrak{su}(N)), (\mathrm{Tr}(Y)=0). Moreover (Y^2) is Hermitian and (\mathrm{Tr}(Y^2)\in\mathbb R). Thus
[
\mathrm{Re,Tr}(e^Y)=\mathrm{Tr}(I)+\frac12\mathrm{Tr}(Y^2)+\mathcal O(|Y|^3)
= N+\frac12\mathrm{Tr}(Y^2)+\mathcal O(|Y|^3),
]
and subtracting from (N=\mathrm{Re,Tr}(I)) yields (4.13). Rewriting (-\mathrm{Tr}(Y^2)=|Y|_\lambda^2/\lambda) gives (4.14). ∎

---

### 4.6. Quadratic expansion of (S_W) at the vacuum

Combine Lemma 4.1 and Lemma 4.2.

**Proposition 4.3 (Second variation of (S_W) at (U^{(0)})).**
Let (X\in \mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak{su}(N))) and (U(t)=\exp(tX)). Then
[
S_W(U(t))
=========

S_W(U^{(0)})
+\frac{t^2}{2},\frac{\beta}{N},|d_1X|*{\mathcal C^2,\lambda}^2
+\mathcal O!\big(t^3|X|*\infty^3\big),
\tag{4.15}
]
where (|\cdot|*{\mathcal C^2,\lambda}) is the (\ell^2)-norm induced from (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle*\lambda).

In particular, the Riemannian Hessian at the vacuum satisfies, as a quadratic form,
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)})(X,X)
==========================

# \frac{\beta}{N},|d_1X|_{\mathcal C^2,\lambda}^2

\frac{\beta}{N},\langle X,d_1^*d_1X\rangle_{\mathcal C^1,\lambda}.
\tag{4.16}
]

*Proof.* For each plaquette (p), Lemma 4.1 gives (U_p(t)=\exp(Y_p(t))) with
[
Y_p(t)=t(d_1X)_p+t^2R_p(t).
]
Insert into (\Phi(U_p(t))=\frac{\beta}{N}\mathrm{Re,Tr}(I-e^{Y_p(t)})) and apply Lemma 4.2:
[
\Phi(U_p(t))
============

\frac{\beta}{N}\left(\frac{1}{2\lambda}|Y_p(t)|*\lambda^2+\mathcal O(|Y_p(t)|*\lambda^3)\right).
]
Since (Y_p(t)=\mathcal O(t|X|*\infty)), we have (|Y_p(t)|*\lambda^3=\mathcal O(t^3|X|*\infty^3)). Also,
[
|Y_p(t)|*\lambda^2
==================

# \big|t(d_1X)*p+t^2R_p(t)\big|*\lambda^2

t^2|(d_1X)*p|*\lambda^2 + \mathcal O(t^3|X|_\infty^3),
]
because the cross term is (2t^3\langle(d_1X)*p,R_p(t)\rangle=\mathcal O(t^3|X|*\infty^3)) using (4.8). Summing over plaquettes yields (4.15).

Finally, on a Riemannian manifold, for a geodesic (\gamma(t)) with (\gamma(0)=U^{(0)}) and (\dot\gamma(0)=X),
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)})(X,X)=\frac{d^2}{dt^2}S_W(\gamma(t))\Big|_{t=0}.
]
Here (\gamma(t)=\exp(tX)) is a geodesic (product of one-parameter subgroups), so the coefficient of (t^2/2) in (4.15) is exactly the Hessian value, giving (4.16). ∎

### 4.7. Operator identity and normalization map

Equation (4.16) is already the “matrix statement”: (d_1^*d_1) is a **positive semidefinite, sparse** operator determined by the lattice incidence structure.

If we want an operator identity (relative to the chosen inner product), we read (4.16) as:

**Corollary 4.4 (Wilson Hessian = discrete Maxwell operator).**
As an operator on (\mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak{su}(N))) with respect to (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_\lambda),
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)}) ;=; \frac{\beta}{N\lambda},d_1^*d_1.
\tag{4.17}
]
In the common lattice normalization (\lambda=1) (i.e. (\langle A,B\rangle=-\mathrm{Tr}(AB))),
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)}) ;=; \frac{\beta}{N},d_1^*d_1.
\tag{4.18}
]
In particular, for (N=3),
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)}) ;=; \frac{\beta}{3},d_1^*d_1.
\tag{4.19}
]

*Explanation of the (\lambda)-dependence.*
The quadratic form (\nabla^2S_W(U^{(0)})(X,X)) is metric-independent as a number, but writing it as (\langle X,(\cdot)X\rangle_{\mathcal C^1,\lambda}) introduces the factor (1/\lambda), producing (4.17).

### 4.8. Kernel, gauge directions, and the horizontal sector

Because (d_1^*d_1) is PSD,
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)})(X,X)\ge 0\quad\forall X.
\tag{4.20}
]

Moreover,
[
\ker\big(\nabla^2S_W(U^{(0)})\big)=\ker(d_1),
\tag{4.21}
]
the space of discrete **closed 1-forms**.

Let (d_0:\mathcal C^0(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)\to \mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)) denote the discrete gradient/coboundary (introduced in your Part 3), so that (d_1 d_0=0). Then
[
\mathrm{im}(d_0)\subseteq \ker(d_1),
\tag{4.22}
]
i.e. **infinitesimal gauge directions are always zero-modes** of the Wilson Hessian at the vacuum.

Define the vertical and horizontal subspaces at the vacuum:
[
V_{U^{(0)}}:=\mathrm{im}(d_0),
\qquad
H_{U^{(0)}}:=V_{U^{(0)}}^\perp=\ker(d_0^*).
\tag{4.23}
]
On (H_{U^{(0)}}), the operator (d_1^*d_1) is still PSD; any remaining kernel corresponds to harmonic 1-forms (topological/finite-size modes), depending on boundary conditions.

### 4.9. Why this must stay a matrix statement (and where “absolute values” would enter later)

The identity (4.18) is the point where your later “PSD not adversarial” upgrade begins.

* If you **keep** (\nabla^2S_W(U^{(0)})) as the PSD operator ((\beta/N)d_1^*d_1), then any later inequality of the form
  [
  \langle \nabla f,, \nabla^2S_W(U^{(0)}),\nabla f\rangle
  ]
  is automatically nonnegative and retains the sparse geometry of the lattice (the signed incidence structure).

* The **only place absolute values appear** in the older scalar/Dobrushin-style route is when one replaces the signed matrix (\mathrm{Hess}*{\ell,\ell'}) by a uniform bound on magnitudes, schematically
  [
  \sum*{\ell'\neq \ell}\mathrm{Hess}*{\ell,\ell'},\xi*{\ell}\xi_{\ell'}
  ;\ge;
  -\sum_{\ell'\neq \ell}|\mathrm{Hess}*{\ell,\ell'}|,|\xi*\ell|,|\xi_{\ell'}|
  ;\ge;
  -J\sum_{\ell'\sim \ell}|\xi_\ell|,|\xi_{\ell'}|.
  \tag{4.24}
  ]
  That step **destroys** the curl-structure and treats every cross-term as adversarial.

Part 4’s message is: **don’t do (4.24) for the Wilson contribution**—it is already ((\beta/N)d_1^*d_1\succeq 0). The only cross-terms you will later need to control are the *perturbative* differences between (\mathrm{Hess},S_W(U)) and (\mathrm{Hess},S_W(U^{(0)})) when (U) is restricted to your small-field set (K_\Lambda) (defined later), and those are exactly where the “weights remain (\succeq I)” hinge proposition will live.
