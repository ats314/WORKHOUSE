# Part 5 — Abstract Interface to Gauge–Invariant Theories

*(This is written so it can be dropped in as Part 5/20 of the full document.)*

Up to now we have built:

* A curvature-based analytic engine for finite-dimensional Gibbs measures ((M,g,S,\mu)):
  Bakry–Émery curvature (\Rightarrow CD(\rho,\infty) \Rightarrow) Poincaré and log–Sobolev inequalities with constants (1/\rho) and (2/\rho) (Parts 2–3).

* A scalar lattice prototype where a uniform convexity bound on the potential yields a volume-independent spectral gap and LSI, with constants independent of (|\Lambda|) (Part 4).

The goal of this part is to package these ideas in a form suitable for **gauge theories**—in particular lattice Yang–Mills—but in an **abstract, model-agnostic** way. We want a clean interface:

* **Input:** a family of finite-volume configuration manifolds
  [
  (M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda,S_\Lambda,\mu_\Lambda),\qquad \Lambda\ \text{finite},
  ]
  equipped with a gauge group acting by isometries and leaving the Gibbs measures invariant, plus a curvature lower bound in **physical (horizontal)** directions.

* **Output:** Poincaré and log–Sobolev inequalities, and hence spectral gaps, for **gauge-invariant observables**, with constants **independent of (\Lambda)**.

Part II of the project will then instantiate this abstract module with the concrete geometry of lattice Yang–Mills.

## 5.1. Abstract finite-volume configuration family

We fix a family of finite index sets (\Lambda) (typically finite subsets of (\mathbb{Z}^d) or spacetime slabs). For each (\Lambda) we assume:

1. A smooth, connected, finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)).

2. A potential (S_\Lambda\in C^2(M_\Lambda)), bounded below, such that the Gibbs normalization constant is finite:
   [
   Z_\Lambda := \int_{M_\Lambda} e^{-S_\Lambda(x)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(x) < \infty.
   ]

3. The Gibbs measure
   [
   d\mu_\Lambda(x) = Z_\Lambda^{-1} e^{-S_\Lambda(x)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(x).
   ]

We define the usual diffusion generator, carré du champ and Dirichlet form:

* **Generator** on smooth (f):
  [
  L_\Lambda f = \Delta_{g_\Lambda} f - \langle \nabla S_\Lambda,\nabla f\rangle_{g_\Lambda}.
  ]

* **Carré du champ**:
  [
  \Gamma_\Lambda(f,g) = \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle_{g_\Lambda},
  \qquad
  \Gamma_\Lambda(f) = \Gamma_\Lambda(f,f) = |\nabla f|*{g*\Lambda}^2.
  ]

* **Dirichlet form**:
  [
  \mathcal{E}*\Lambda(f,g) = \int*{M_\Lambda} \Gamma_\Lambda(f,g),d\mu_\Lambda.
  ]

As recalled in Parts 1–2, ((\mathcal{E}*\Lambda,\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}*\Lambda))) is a closed, Markovian Dirichlet form and there exists a unique nonnegative self-adjoint operator (-L_\Lambda) on (L^2(\mu_\Lambda)) such that
[
\mathcal{E}*\Lambda(f,g) = \langle f,-L*\Lambda g\rangle_{L^2(\mu_\Lambda)}.
]

The **Bakry–Émery Ricci tensor** is defined by
[
\operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda} := \operatorname{Ric}*{g*\Lambda} + \nabla^2 S_\Lambda,
]
a symmetric 2-tensor on each tangent space (T_xM_\Lambda).

The associated **(\Gamma_2)-operator** is
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f,g)
:= \frac12\big(L_\Lambda\Gamma_\Lambda(f,g)
- \Gamma_\Lambda(f,L_\Lambda g) - \Gamma_\Lambda(g,L_\Lambda f)\big),
\qquad
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f) := \Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f,f).
]

By the Bochner–Bakry–Émery identity (proved in Part 2), we have, pointwise,
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)
= |\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2
+ \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu_\Lambda}(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]

This is the geometric backbone: it expresses (\Gamma_2) as a sum of a nonnegative Hessian term and a curvature term.

## 5.2. Gauge symmetry and physical subspaces

We now introduce the abstract notion of a gauge symmetry and the corresponding decomposition of tangent spaces into **pure gauge** (vertical) and **physical** (horizontal) directions.

### 5.2.1. Gauge group action

For each finite (\Lambda), assume a compact Lie group (\mathcal{G}*\Lambda) acts smoothly on (M*\Lambda):
[
\mathcal{G}*\Lambda \times M*\Lambda \to M_\Lambda,\quad (g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x.
]

Hypotheses:

1. **Isometric action.** For every (g\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda), the map (x\mapsto g\cdot x) is an isometry of ((M*\Lambda,g_\Lambda)).

2. **Gauge-invariant potential.** The potential is invariant:
   [
   S_\Lambda(g\cdot x) = S_\Lambda(x)
   \quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda,\ x\in M*\Lambda.
   ]
   Equivalently, the Gibbs measure (\mu_\Lambda) is (\mathcal{G}_\Lambda)-invariant.

This is exactly the structure realized by lattice Yang–Mills, where (M_\Lambda = G^{E(\Lambda)}) and (\mathcal{G}_\Lambda = G^{V(\Lambda)}) acts by endpoint conjugation on edge variables.

### 5.2.2. Vertical and horizontal subspaces

For each (x\in M_\Lambda), the **orbit map**
[
\Phi_x:\mathcal{G}*\Lambda\to M*\Lambda,\quad \Phi_x(g) = g\cdot x
]
has differential at the identity (\mathrm{id}\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda)
[
d\Phi_x\big|*{\mathrm{id}} : \mathfrak{g}*\Lambda \to T_x M*\Lambda,
]
where (\mathfrak{g}*\Lambda := \mathrm{Lie}(\mathcal{G}*\Lambda)).

The image
[
V_x := d\Phi_x\big|*{\mathrm{id}}(\mathfrak{g}*\Lambda) \subset T_xM_\Lambda
]
is the **vertical subspace** at (x): tangent to the gauge orbit through (x).

Using the Riemannian metric, we define the **horizontal subspace** as
[
H_x := V_x^\perp \subset T_xM_\Lambda.
]

Intuitively:

* (V_x): directions which move (x) along its gauge orbit (pure gauge).
* (H_x): directions transverse to gauge orbits (physical).

### 5.2.3. Gauge-invariant observables and horizontal gradients

Let (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) denote the algebra of smooth **gauge-invariant observables**:
[
\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}
:= { f\in C^\infty(M_\Lambda): f(g\cdot x) = f(x)\ \forall g\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda,\ x\in M*\Lambda}.
]

Typical examples in lattice Yang–Mills are Wilson loops and gauge-invariant polynomials in plaquette holonomies.

If (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) and (X\in\mathfrak{g}*\Lambda), then
[
f(\exp(tX)\cdot x) = f(x)\quad\forall t,
]
so differentiating at (t=0),
[
df_x\big(X^\sharp(x)\big) = 0\quad\forall X,
]
where (X^\sharp(x)\in V_x) is the fundamental vector field of (X). Thus
[
df_x(v) = 0\quad\forall v\in V_x,
]
and hence
[
g_\Lambda(x)\big(\nabla f(x),v\big) = 0\quad\forall v\in V_x.
]

Therefore:

> **Proposition 5.1 (Horizontal gradients of gauge-invariant observables).**
> For every (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) and every (x\in M*\Lambda),
> [
> \nabla f(x)\in H_x.
> ]

Equivalently, for gauge-invariant observables the carré du champ is purely horizontal:
[
\Gamma_\Lambda(f)(x) = |\nabla f(x)|*{g*\Lambda}^2
= |\nabla^H f(x)|*{g*\Lambda}^2,
]
where (\nabla^H f) is the projection of (\nabla f) onto (H).

This is the key structural point: **only the horizontal curvature matters** for gauge-invariant functions. Analytically, one can think of (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) as the algebra of smooth functions on the quotient (M*\Lambda/\mathcal{G}_\Lambda), with the Dirichlet form induced by the horizontal geometry.

## 5.3. Horizontal Bakry–Émery curvature hypothesis

The Bochner–Bakry–Émery identity gives, for any smooth (f),
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)
= |\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2
+ \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu_\Lambda}(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]

If (f) is gauge invariant, then (\nabla f(x)\in H_x) by Proposition 5.1. This motivates a curvature hypothesis **restricted to horizontal directions**.

### 5.3.1. Horizontal curvature lower bound

We formulate an abstract horizontal Bakry–Émery bound:

> **Hypothesis ((H_{\mathrm{curv}})) (global horizontal curvature).**
> There exists a constant (\rho_* > 0), **independent of (\Lambda)**, such that for every finite (\Lambda), every (x\in M_\Lambda), and every (v\in H_x),
> [
> \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(x)(v,v)
> = \big(\operatorname{Ric}*{g*\Lambda} + \nabla^2 S_\Lambda\big)(x)(v,v)
> ;\ge; \rho_*,|v|*{g*\Lambda}^2.
> ]

Remarks:

* This is a curvature lower bound only in the **physical** directions (H_x); we do not require any control on the vertical directions (V_x).

* The constant (\rho_*) must not deteriorate with (\Lambda); this is the “thermodynamic-limit-friendly” part.

In lattice Yang–Mills, later parts construct at least a **local** version of this bound (near the trivial configuration) by combining Haar Ricci curvature and the Hessian of the Wilson (plus extra) potential in the horizontal directions.

### 5.3.2. (CD(\rho_*,\infty)) on gauge-invariant observables

Let (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}). At any (x\in M*\Lambda), we have (\nabla f(x)\in H_x), so Hypothesis ((H_{\mathrm{curv}})) implies
[
\operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(x)(\nabla f(x),\nabla f(x))
;\ge; \rho_*,|\nabla f(x)|*{g*\Lambda}^2
= \rho_*,\Gamma_\Lambda(f)(x).
]

Hence
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)(x)
= |\nabla^2 f(x)|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2
+ \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu_\Lambda}(x)(\nabla f(x),\nabla f(x))
;\ge; \rho_*,\Gamma_\Lambda(f)(x).
]

In other words, for gauge-invariant observables we have the curvature–dimension condition
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f) ;\ge; \rho_*,\Gamma_\Lambda(f)
\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{A}_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}.
]

If we view (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) as the algebra of smooth functions on the **quotient manifold** (or orbifold) (M*\Lambda/\mathcal{G}*\Lambda), then this is precisely a (CD(\rho**,\infty)) bound on the quotient geometry.

## 5.4. Product structure and uniformity in volume

One of the perks of Bakry–Émery curvature is its good behavior under products. This is essential if we want constants that do not blow up with the lattice volume.

### 5.4.1. Product curvature for ungauged systems

Suppose we have a “local degree of freedom” ((M_{\mathrm{loc}},g_{\mathrm{loc}},S_{\mathrm{loc}},\mu_{\mathrm{loc}})) with
[
\operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*{\mathrm{loc}}} \ge \rho_{\mathrm{loc}},g_{\mathrm{loc}}.
]

For a product of (n) independent copies,
[
M = M_{\mathrm{loc}}^n,\qquad
g = \bigoplus_{i=1}^n g_{\mathrm{loc}}^{(i)},\qquad
S = \sum_{i=1}^n S_{\mathrm{loc}}^{(i)},
]
the product curvature identity gives
[
\operatorname{Ric}*\mu \ge \rho*{\mathrm{loc}},g
]
with the **same** constant (\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}), independent of (n).

This is standard and is exactly what we used in the scalar prototype: convexity plus positive Ricci at a single site gives a volume-independent spectral gap and LSI on the whole lattice.

### 5.4.2. Gauge symmetry and quotient geometry

In a gauge theory, (M_\Lambda) is typically a product of groups (one copy per edge), but we then impose a huge redundancy via the gauge group (\mathcal{G}_\Lambda).

Analytically, one can think of two equivalent pictures:

1. Work on the full product manifold (M_\Lambda) with the product curvature bound and then **restrict to gauge-invariant observables**, which only see horizontal directions; or

2. Work directly on the quotient manifold (or orbifold) (M_\Lambda/\mathcal{G}*\Lambda), where the vertical gauge directions have been modded out, and inherit (CD(\rho**,\infty)) there.

From the point of view of the Dirichlet form and (\Gamma)-calculus, these are the same: gradients of gauge-invariant observables live in (H), and the effective geometry is that of the quotient.

Hypothesis ((H_{\mathrm{curv}})) is exactly the statement that this **effective quotient geometry** carries a uniform Bakry–Émery curvature lower bound (\rho_*) independent of (\Lambda).

## 5.5. Consequences for gauge-invariant observables

Assume Hypothesis ((H_{\mathrm{curv}})) for some (\rho_* > 0) independent of (\Lambda). Then the restriction of (L_\Lambda), (\Gamma_\Lambda), and (\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}) to (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) satisfies a genuine (CD(\rho**,\infty)) condition.

Applying the finite-dimensional Bakry–Émery results from Parts 2–3 (in particular, the (CD(\rho,\infty)\Rightarrow) Poincaré/LSI machinery), we immediately obtain:

### 5.5.1. Uniform Poincaré inequality in the gauge-invariant sector

For each finite (\Lambda) and every (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) in the form domain,
[
\operatorname{Var}*{\mu_\Lambda}(f)
;\le; \frac{1}{\rho_*}
\int_{M_\Lambda} \Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
]

Equivalently, letting (-L_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) be the self-adjoint operator generated by (\mathcal{E}*\Lambda) on the closed subspace (\overline{\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}}^{,L^2(\mu_\Lambda)}), its spectral gap satisfies
[
\lambda_1^{\mathrm{inv}}(\Lambda) ;\ge; \rho_*,
]
with a lower bound **independent of (\Lambda)**.

In words: **gauge-invariant observables decorrelate on a timescale (\mathcal{O}(1/\rho_*)) under the configuration-space diffusion, uniformly in lattice size.**

### 5.5.2. Uniform log–Sobolev inequality in the gauge-invariant sector

Similarly, the log–Sobolev inequality holds for all smooth (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}):
[
\operatorname{Ent}*{\mu_\Lambda}(f^2)
;\le; \frac{2}{\rho_*} \int_{M_\Lambda} \Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
]

Standard consequences include:

* Exponential decay of relative entropy for the Langevin semigroup restricted to gauge-invariant observables;
* Concentration inequalities for gauge-invariant functionals, with constants that do not depend on (|\Lambda|);
* Uniform control of correlation lengths in terms of (1/\rho_*).

These are exactly the kinds of bounds one wants for thermodynamic-limit questions and for feeding into OS/Hamiltonian comparisons in later parts.

## 5.6. Summary and role of Part 5 in the overall program

This part isolates the **abstract analytic interface** between curvature geometry and gauge-invariant observables.

* **Data:**

  * A family ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda,S_\Lambda,\mu_\Lambda)) of finite-volume Gibbs systems.
  * A gauge group (\mathcal{G}*\Lambda) acting isometrically and leaving (\mu*\Lambda) invariant.
  * The associated vertical subspaces (V_x) and horizontal (physical) subspaces (H_x = V_x^\perp).
  * The algebra (\mathcal{A}_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) of gauge-invariant observables, with (\nabla f(x)\in H_x).

* **Hypothesis:**

  * A **horizontal Bakry–Émery curvature lower bound** ((H_{\mathrm{curv}})) with constant (\rho_* > 0) independent of (\Lambda).

* **Output:**

  * Uniform Poincaré and log–Sobolev inequalities for gauge-invariant observables, with constants (1/\rho_*) and (2/\rho_*).
  * A spectral gap (\lambda_{1}^{\mathrm{inv}}(\Lambda)\ge\rho_*) for the configuration-space diffusion restricted to the gauge-invariant sector, independent of (\Lambda).

In the broader 20-part structure:

* **Parts 2–3** developed the curvature (\Rightarrow) FI machinery in a general setting.
* **Part 4** gave a scalar example where a global curvature bound holds with (\rho_*) explicitly computable from the potential, yielding volume-independent spectral gaps and LSIs.
* **Part 5 (this part)** shows how to phrase the same mechanism in the presence of gauge symmetry, abstracting away all model-specific details.
* **Parts 6–11** will show that lattice Yang–Mills provides concrete ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda,S_\Lambda)) and (\mathcal{G}*\Lambda) for which we can prove a **local** version of ((H*{\mathrm{curv}})) (near the trivial connection), with constants independent of volume, and then feed this into Lyapunov/local-to-global machinery (Parts 12–15) to seek global, volume-uniform functional inequalities.

Conceptually: Part 5 is the **socket** into which the concrete Yang–Mills geometry plugs. Once you buy a horizontal curvature bound with a uniform constant, everything in the gauge-invariant sector—variance, entropy, spectral gap, concentration—falls into place by the same Bakry–Émery engine developed earlier.
