## Section 7. Spectral Gap and Lattice Mass Gap

This section turns the global functional inequality proved in Section 6 into two spectral statements:

1. a **configuration-space spectral gap** for the reversible diffusion whose invariant law is the lattice Yang–Mills Gibbs measure;
2. a **lattice (Osterwalder–Schrader) mass gap** for the Hamiltonian obtained from reflection positivity and time translations.

The heart of the section is a **matrix covariance estimate** that bounds connected correlations by the kernel of a **massive Maxwell operator** (M). Exponential off–diagonal decay of (M^{-1}) yields exponential clustering; exponential decay in the Euclidean time direction then forces a positive OS Hamiltonian gap by a spectral–theorem argument.

### 7.1. Basic objects: Gibbs measure, generator, Dirichlet form

Let (\Lambda) be a finite (d)-dimensional lattice (with some boundary condition fixed once and for all). Let (E(\Lambda)) denote the set of oriented links (edges) and (P(\Lambda)) the set of oriented plaquettes. The configuration space is the compact Riemannian manifold
[
M_\Lambda := G^{E(\Lambda)},
]
endowed with the product bi-invariant metric and corresponding volume measure (\mathrm{Vol}_\Lambda), which coincides with product Haar measure.

Fix a smooth gauge-invariant action (S_\Lambda:M_\Lambda\to\mathbb R). The Gibbs measure is
[
\mu_\Lambda(dU)= Z_\Lambda^{-1} e^{-S_\Lambda(U)},\mathrm{Vol}_\Lambda(dU).
]

Let (\nabla) and (\Delta) denote the gradient and Laplace–Beltrami operator on (M_\Lambda). The reversible diffusion generator is
[
L_\Lambda f := \Delta f - \langle \nabla S_\Lambda, \nabla f\rangle .
]
With this sign convention, (L_\Lambda) is symmetric on (L^2(\mu_\Lambda)) and nonpositive.

The carré du champ and Dirichlet form are
[
\Gamma_\Lambda(f,g):=\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,\qquad
\mathcal E_\Lambda(f,g):=\int \Gamma_\Lambda(f,g),d\mu_\Lambda
=\int \langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu_\Lambda,
]
and (\mathcal E_\Lambda(f,f)=\int |\nabla f|^2 d\mu_\Lambda). (In link coordinates this is the sum over links of (|\nabla_\ell f|^2) as in the explicit formula for (\Gamma_\Lambda).) 

### 7.2. From Poincaré to the configuration spectral gap

Section 6 provides a volume-uniform Poincaré inequality (and typically also a log-Sobolev inequality). We now record the standard equivalence between Poincaré and the spectral gap of (-L_\Lambda), since we will use it to justify invertibility of (-L_\Lambda) on mean-zero functions.

#### Definition 7.1 (Configuration spectral gap)

Define the (L^2(\mu_\Lambda)) spectral gap of (-L_\Lambda) by the Rayleigh quotient
[
\lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)
:= \inf\left{\frac{\mathcal E_\Lambda(f,f)}{\mathrm{Var}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f)}:\ f\in C^\infty(M_\Lambda),\ \mathrm{Var}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f)\neq 0\right},
]
where (\mathrm{Var}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f)=\int (f-\mu_\Lambda f)^2 d\mu_\Lambda).

#### Proposition 7.2 (Poincaré inequality (\Longleftrightarrow) spectral gap)

The following are equivalent:

1. (Poincaré) There exists (C_P<\infty) such that for all smooth (f),
   [
   \mathrm{Var}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f)\le C_P,\mathcal E_\Lambda(f,f).
   ]
2. (Spectral gap) (\lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)\ge 1/C_P).

Moreover, the best (smallest) Poincaré constant satisfies (C_P = 1/\lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)).

**Proof.**
(1)(\Rightarrow)(2): For any nonconstant (f),
[
\frac{\mathcal E_\Lambda(f,f)}{\mathrm{Var}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f)}\ge \frac{1}{C_P},
]
so taking the infimum gives (\lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)\ge 1/C_P).

(2)(\Rightarrow)(1): If (\lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)\ge \lambda>0), then by definition
[
\mathrm{Var}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f)\le \frac{1}{\lambda}\mathcal E_\Lambda(f,f)
]
for all (f), i.e. Poincaré holds with (C_P=1/\lambda). ∎

In particular, in the regime of Section 6 we may assume a uniform lower bound
[
\lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)\ge \lambda_0>0
\quad\text{uniformly in }\Lambda.
\tag{7.1}
]

This uniform gap implies invertibility of (-L_\Lambda) on the mean-zero subspace and exponential (L^2)-mixing of the associated Markov semigroup; we will use only the invertibility.

### 7.3. Helffer–Sjöstrand covariance representation and the Witten Laplacian on 1‑forms

To control correlations, we use a standard covariance identity (Helffer–Sjöstrand). The advantage is that it exposes an inverse operator on 1-forms, which can be compared to a *sparse* massive Maxwell operator.

#### 7.3.1. The Witten Laplacian (L_\Lambda^{(1)})

Let (\Omega^1(M_\Lambda)) denote smooth 1-forms. Define the Witten Laplacian on 1-forms associated to (L_\Lambda) by the Weitzenböck/Bakry–Émery decomposition
[
L_\Lambda^{(1)} := (-L_\Lambda)\otimes I + \big(\mathrm{Ric}*\Lambda + \nabla^2 S*\Lambda\big),
\tag{7.2}
]
acting fiberwise on 1-forms. Here (\mathrm{Ric}*\Lambda) is the Ricci curvature operator of the product manifold and (\nabla^2 S*\Lambda) the Riemannian Hessian of (S_\Lambda).

This operator is the precise one appearing in the Bochner identity and in the covariance representation. In the Bakry–Émery (\Gamma_2) calculus, one has the pointwise identity
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)=|\nabla^2 f|^2 + \langle (\mathrm{Ric}*\Lambda+\nabla^2 S*\Lambda)\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle,
]
so (\mathrm{Ric}*\Lambda+\nabla^2 S*\Lambda) is the *effective curvature* controlling functional inequalities. 

#### 7.3.2. Helffer–Sjöstrand formula

Let (F,G\in C^\infty(M_\Lambda)) with (\mu_\Lambda G=0). Since (\lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)>0), the Poisson equation
[

* L_\Lambda u = G,\qquad \mu_\Lambda u=0
  \tag{7.3}
  ]
  has a unique (smooth) solution (u).

**Step 1: Integration by parts.** Using symmetry of (L_\Lambda),
[
\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)
= \int F G,d\mu_\Lambda
= \int F (-L_\Lambda u),d\mu_\Lambda
= \int \langle \nabla F,\nabla u\rangle,d\mu_\Lambda.
\tag{7.4}
]

**Step 2: Commutation (\nabla(-L_\Lambda u)=L_\Lambda^{(1)}(\nabla u)).**
The Bochner/Weitzenböck identity for the operator (L_\Lambda) implies, for every smooth (u),
[
\nabla(-L_\Lambda u)=L_\Lambda^{(1)}(\nabla u),
\tag{7.5}
]
with (L_\Lambda^{(1)}) as in (7.2). This is exactly the mechanism by which the curvature term (\mathrm{Ric}_\Lambda) enters.

Applying (\nabla) to (7.3) and using (7.5) gives
[
L_\Lambda^{(1)}(\nabla u)=\nabla G.
\tag{7.6}
]
On the mean-zero sector, (L_\Lambda^{(1)}) is invertible (in the appropriate (L^2) sense), so
[
\nabla u = (L_\Lambda^{(1)})^{-1}\nabla G.
\tag{7.7}
]

**Step 3: Substitute into (7.4).** Combining (7.4) and (7.7) yields the covariance representation
[
\boxed{
\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)
= \int \left\langle \nabla F,\ (L_\Lambda^{(1)})^{-1}\nabla G\right\rangle,d\mu_\Lambda.
}
\tag{7.8}
]
This is the Helffer–Sjöstrand identity in the present geometric setting. (A version specialized to lattice gauge theory with the horizontal projection and Maxwell comparison is recorded in the project notes; we use it below.) 

### 7.4. Gauge invariance and the horizontal projection

Gauge invariance removes the pure gauge directions in tangent space. For covariance bounds, this matters because the Wilson action has degeneracies along gauge orbits, while the physical Hessian is coercive only on the *horizontal* subspace.

At a configuration (U\in M_\Lambda), let (V_U\subset T_U M_\Lambda) be the vertical space tangent to the gauge orbit and (H_U:=V_U^\perp) the horizontal space. Let (\Pi_{H}) be the orthogonal projection onto (H_U) (acting fiberwise).

A key fact (proved explicitly at the vacuum via the cochain model, and valid at all (U) by gauge invariance) is:

#### Lemma 7.3 (Gradients of gauge-invariant observables are horizontal)

If (F) is gauge invariant, then (\nabla F(U)\in H_U) for all (U). Equivalently,
[
\Pi_H \nabla F = \nabla F.
\tag{7.9}
]

**Sketch of proof.** For any infinitesimal gauge variation (X\in V_U), gauge invariance gives (dF(U)[X]=0). Since (dF(U)[X]=\langle \nabla F(U),X\rangle), the gradient is orthogonal to (V_U), i.e. in (H_U). ∎

In the cochain linearization at the vacuum (U^{(0)}), the vertical space is (\mathrm{im}(d_0)) and the horizontal space is (\ker(d_0^*)).  

Consequently, for gauge-invariant (F,G), the covariance formula (7.8) may be written with (\Pi_H) inserted:
[
\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)
= \int \left\langle \Pi_H\nabla F,\ (L_\Lambda^{(1)})^{-1}\Pi_H\nabla G\right\rangle,d\mu_\Lambda.
\tag{7.10}
]
(Here we used (\Pi_H\nabla F=\nabla F) but keep the projections to emphasize the physical subspace.)

### 7.5. The hinge comparison: ( (L_\Lambda^{(1)})^{-1}\preceq M_\Lambda^{-1})

We now state the structural input coming from Sections 3–6: on a canonical “good” region, the effective curvature (\mathrm{Ric}*\Lambda+\nabla^2 S*\Lambda) dominates a sparse massive Maxwell operator on lattice 1-cochains.

#### 7.5.1. The massive Maxwell operator

Let (\mathcal C^k(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)) denote (\mathfrak g)-valued (k)-cochains with the standard inner products. 
Define the discrete coboundaries (d_0:\mathcal C^0\to \mathcal C^1) and (d_1:\mathcal C^1\to\mathcal C^2) by
[
(d_0\phi)*{x,\mu}=\phi*{x+\hat\mu}-\phi_x,\qquad
(d_1X)*{x;\mu,\nu}=X*{x,\mu}+X_{x+\hat\mu,\nu}-X_{x+\hat\nu,\mu}-X_{x,\nu}.
\tag{7.11}
]


Let (d_1^*) be the Hilbert adjoint of (d_1). The operator (d_1^*d_1) is the discrete Maxwell operator on 1-cochains; it is sparse and positive semidefinite.

Fix parameters (m^2>0) and (\alpha>0). Define the **massive Maxwell operator**
[
M_\Lambda := m^2 I + \alpha, d_1^*d_1
\quad\text{on }\mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g).
\tag{7.12}
]

For the Wilson action linearized at the vacuum with (G=\mathrm{SU}(N)) and the standard metric normalization, the Wilson Hessian is proportional to (d_1^*d_1); in particular for (N=3),
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)})=\frac{\beta}{3},d_1^*d_1.
\tag{7.13}
]


The parameter (m^2) is the “Haar mass” coming from the positive Ricci curvature contribution; in the project normalization it is convenient to write (m^2=c_H/2) for a constant (c_H>0) depending only on (G) and the chosen bi-invariant metric. 

#### 7.5.2. The matrix domination hypothesis

The core local geometric estimate (proved earlier on the canonical region (K_\Lambda) via the Hessian calculations and Haar curvature) is:

> **Hinge domination (input from Sections 3–6).**
> On the canonical region (K_\Lambda\subset M_\Lambda), the effective curvature on the horizontal subspace satisfies
> [
> \Pi_H \big(\mathrm{Ric}*\Lambda+\nabla^2 S*\Lambda\big)\Pi_H \ \succeq\ \Pi_H M_\Lambda \Pi_H
> \qquad\text{(as quadratic forms on 1-forms).}
> \tag{7.14}
> ]
> Equivalently, on (K_\Lambda) one has (L_\Lambda^{(1)} \succeq M_\Lambda) on horizontal 1-forms, and therefore
> [
> \boxed{(L_\Lambda^{(1)})^{-1} \preceq M_\Lambda^{-1}\quad\text{on horizontal 1-forms.}}
> \tag{7.15}
> ]
> A precise statement in the project notes yields a covariance bound of the form
> [
> \mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)\ \le\ \int \left\langle \Pi_H\nabla F,\ M_\Lambda^{-1}\Pi_H\nabla G\right\rangle,d\mu_\Lambda
> \ +\ \text{(localization error)}.
> \tag{7.16}
> ]
>

The “localization error” is controlled by Section 6 (or, more precisely, by the concentration/localization step that ensures typicality of (K_\Lambda)). The project draft is explicit that the key missing input is a uniform typicality lemma ensuring (\mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda^c)) is small uniformly in (\Lambda). 
For the remainder of this section, we package this as an assumption:

#### Assumption 7.4 (Uniform localization to the hinge region)

There exists (c>0) such that
[
\mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda^c)\le e^{-c|P(\Lambda)|}
\tag{7.17}
]
(or any quantitatively comparable bound strong enough that the error term in (7.16) is negligible compared with the exponential decay obtained below).

With this in hand, the remaining task is to show that (M_\Lambda^{-1}) has an exponentially decaying kernel.

### 7.6. Exponential decay of (M_\Lambda^{-1})

We present two complementary decay results:

* a **general Combes–Thomas type bound** that applies directly in finite volume and yields a uniform (though not sharp) exponential rate; 
* a **sharper Fourier/contour–shift bound** on (\mathbb Z^d) giving a rate (\sim \beta^{-1/2}) for large (\beta). 

Either one yields exponential clustering; for a volume-uniform finite-volume statement, the Combes–Thomas bound is sufficient.

#### 7.6.1. Link graph distance

Define a graph on links: two oriented links (\ell,\ell'\in E(\Lambda)) are adjacent, (\ell\sim\ell'), if they appear together in some plaquette. Define the link distance
[
\mathrm{dist}_1(\ell,\ell') := \text{graph distance in }(E(\Lambda),\sim),
\tag{7.18}
]
i.e. the minimum number of plaquettes needed to connect (\ell) to (\ell'). This is the distance used in the decay bounds.

Let
[
D := \sup_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)} #{\ell':\ \ell'\sim \ell}
\tag{7.19}
]
be the maximal degree of this link graph. In (d=4), one checks (D=18) (each link lies in (6) plaquettes, and each such plaquette contributes at most (3) other links). 

#### 7.6.2. A Combes–Thomas bound for sparse positive matrices

We record a purely algebraic lemma (proved in the notes) that yields exponential decay of inverse matrix elements under bounded off-diagonal row sums.

##### Lemma 7.5 (Combes–Thomas bound on a finite graph)

Let (V) be a finite set with a graph distance (\mathrm{dist}). Let (A) be a positive definite symmetric matrix indexed by (V) such that

* (A_{xx}\ge a_0>0) for all (x\in V);
* (\sum_{y\neq x} |A_{xy}|\le B) for all (x\in V).

Then
[
|(A^{-1})_{xy}|\ \le\ \frac{2}{a_0}, e^{-\eta,\mathrm{dist}(x,y)}
\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in V,
\tag{7.20}
]
where
[
\eta := \log\Big(1+\frac{a_0}{2B}\Big)>0.
\tag{7.21}
]

**Proof.** This is proved by a resolvent/Neumann-series expansion together with a path-counting bound exploiting the finite-range structure; the full proof is given in the project notes.  ∎

##### Corollary 7.6 (Decay of the massive Maxwell inverse, finite volume)

Apply Lemma 7.5 to (A=M_\Lambda=m^2 I+\alpha d_1^*d_1) with the link graph distance (\mathrm{dist}*1). One obtains
[
\boxed{
\big|(M*\Lambda^{-1})_{\ell\ell'}\big|
\ \le\ \frac{2}{m^2}, \exp!\Big(-\eta,\mathrm{dist}_1(\ell,\ell')\Big),
}
\tag{7.22}
]
with
[
\eta = \log\Big(1+\frac{m^2}{2\alpha D}\Big).
\tag{7.23}
]
In particular, for the choice (m^2=c_H/2), (\alpha=\beta/3) (SU(3) normalization), and (d=4) so (D=18), this becomes
[
\eta = \log\Big(1+\frac{c_H}{4\beta D}\Big)
= \log\Big(1+\frac{c_H}{72\beta}\Big).
\tag{7.24}
]
(If one further fixes the group metric normalization so that (c_H) is explicit, this yields an explicit numeric constant; we keep (c_H) symbolic.)

#### 7.6.3. Sharper decay on (\mathbb Z^d)

For later scaling discussions, it is useful to record the sharper (\mathbb Z^d) decay estimate proved by Fourier analysis and analytic continuation.

##### Lemma 7.7 (Fourier/contour shift decay on (\mathbb Z^d))

On (\mathbb Z^d), let (M=m^2I+t, d_1^*d_1) on square-summable 1-forms, with (m^2>0), (t>0). Then the kernel satisfies
[
\boxed{
\big|(M^{-1})_{\ell\ell'}\big|
\ \le\ \frac{4}{m^2},e^{-\nu,\mathrm{dist}_1(\ell,\ell')},
}
\tag{7.25}
]
where
[
\nu=\nu(m^2,t)=2,\mathrm{arsinh}!\Big(\sqrt{\frac{m^2}{8td}}\Big).
\tag{7.26}
]
In the SU(3) Wilson normalization (t=\beta/3), (m^2=c_H/2), this gives
[
\nu(c_H,\beta)=2,\mathrm{arsinh}!\Big(\sqrt{\frac{3c_H}{16\beta d}}\Big).
\tag{7.27}
]

**Proof.** The proof proceeds by Fourier transforming (d_1^*d_1), identifying the multiplier and its spectral gap for complexified momentum, then shifting the contour to gain the exponential factor. The full argument (including the analytic lower bound on the complexified symbol) is given in the project notes.  ∎

### 7.7. Exponential clustering from the matrix covariance bound

We now turn the matrix bound (7.16) into exponential decay of covariances of local observables.

#### Definition 7.8 (Local observables and their support)

A smooth observable (F:M_\Lambda\to\mathbb R) is **local** if it depends only on finitely many link variables (U_\ell). Its support (\mathrm{supp}(F)\subset E(\Lambda)) is the minimal set of links on which it depends.

For two sets (A,B\subset E(\Lambda)), define
[
\mathrm{dist}*1(A,B):=\min*{\ell\in A,\ \ell'\in B}\mathrm{dist}_1(\ell,\ell').
\tag{7.28}
]

#### Proposition 7.9 (Exponential clustering; finite-volume version)

Assume:

* the matrix covariance bound (7.16) holds for gauge-invariant local observables, with localization error controlled by (7.17);
* the Green’s function decay estimate (7.22) holds for (M_\Lambda^{-1}).

Then for any bounded gauge-invariant local observables (F,G) with (\mu_\Lambda F=\mu_\Lambda G=0),
[
\boxed{
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)\big|
\ \le\ C(F,G),e^{-\eta,\mathrm{dist}*1(\mathrm{supp}F,\mathrm{supp}G)}
\ +\ \mathrm{Err}*\Lambda(F,G),
}
\tag{7.29}
]
where (\eta) is given by (7.23), and the error satisfies
[
|\mathrm{Err}*\Lambda(F,G)|
\ \le\ 8,|F|*\infty|G|*\infty,\mu*\Lambda(K_\Lambda^c)
\ \le\ 8,|F|*\infty|G|*\infty,e^{-c|P(\Lambda)|}.
\tag{7.30}
]
In particular, for fixed (F,G) and (|\Lambda|\to\infty), the error vanishes and the exponential rate (\eta) survives the thermodynamic limit. 

**Proof.**
Start from (7.16) and take absolute values:
[
|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)|
\le
\int \Big|\big\langle \Pi_H\nabla F,\ M_\Lambda^{-1}\Pi_H\nabla G\big\rangle\Big|,d\mu_\Lambda
+\big|\mathrm{(localization\ error)}\big|.
]

Write (\Pi_H\nabla F) and (\Pi_H\nabla G) in the link decomposition:
[
\Pi_H\nabla F=( (\Pi_H\nabla F)*\ell )*{\ell\in E(\Lambda)},\qquad
(\Pi_H\nabla F)*\ell\in\mathfrak g,
]
and similarly for (G). Then
[
\big\langle \Pi_H\nabla F,\ M*\Lambda^{-1}\Pi_H\nabla G\big\rangle
= \sum_{\ell,\ell'} \left\langle (\Pi_H\nabla F)*\ell,\ (M*\Lambda^{-1})*{\ell\ell'},(\Pi_H\nabla G)*{\ell'}\right\rangle_{\mathfrak g}.
]
By Cauchy–Schwarz (in (\mathfrak g) and then in (L^2(\mu_\Lambda))),
[
|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*\Lambda}(F,G)|
\le
\sum_{\ell,\ell'}
|(\Pi_H\nabla F)*\ell|*{L^2(\mu_\Lambda)}
\ |(M_\Lambda^{-1})*{\ell\ell'}|*{\mathrm{op}}
\ |(\Pi_H\nabla G)*{\ell'}|*{L^2(\mu_\Lambda)}
+\mathrm{Err}_\Lambda(F,G).
\tag{7.31}
]
Since (F) (resp. (G)) depends only on (\mathrm{supp}(F)) (resp. (\mathrm{supp}(G))), its link gradients vanish outside its support; thus the sum can be restricted to (\ell\in \mathrm{supp}(F)) and (\ell'\in \mathrm{supp}(G)).

Using (7.22) and the fact (\mathrm{dist}*1(\ell,\ell')\ge \mathrm{dist}*1(\mathrm{supp}F,\mathrm{supp}G)) for all (\ell\in\mathrm{supp}F), (\ell'\in\mathrm{supp}G), we obtain
[
|(M*\Lambda^{-1})*{\ell\ell'}|*{\mathrm{op}}
\le \frac{2}{m^2},e^{-\eta,\mathrm{dist}*1(\mathrm{supp}F,\mathrm{supp}G)}.
]
Factor this out of the sum in (7.31) to get (7.29) with
[
C(F,G):=\frac{2}{m^2}\left(\sum*{\ell\in\mathrm{supp}F}|(\Pi_H\nabla F)*\ell|*{L^2(\mu*\Lambda)}\right)
\left(\sum_{\ell'\in\mathrm{supp}G}|(\Pi_H\nabla G)*{\ell'}|*{L^2(\mu_\Lambda)}\right).
]
The error bound (7.30) is the standard covariance decomposition estimate used in the localization step (splitting on (K_\Lambda) vs (K_\Lambda^c)); the project notes record the resulting (8|F|*\infty|G|*\infty \mu_\Lambda(K_\Lambda^c)) bound.  ∎

### 7.8. From Euclidean-time decay to an OS Hamiltonian gap (lattice mass gap)

We now convert the Euclidean correlation decay into a spectral gap for the OS Hamiltonian.

#### 7.8.1. Transfer matrix and Hamiltonian

Assume the lattice measure (\mu_\Lambda) satisfies reflection positivity and the remaining OS axioms for a chosen time direction, so that OS reconstruction produces a Hilbert space (\mathcal H_\Lambda), a distinguished vacuum vector (\Omega), and a strongly continuous semigroup (e^{-tH_\Lambda}) with (H_\Lambda\ge 0) such that Schwinger functions can be written as vacuum matrix elements. (This is standard OS reconstruction; the project notes record the precise lattice statement and how to realize time translation by a transfer matrix.) 

Let (a>0) be the lattice spacing in the time direction. Let (T_\Lambda:=e^{-aH_\Lambda}) be the one-step transfer operator; it is self-adjoint on (\mathcal H_\Lambda), satisfies (|T_\Lambda|\le 1), and has top eigenvalue (1) corresponding to (\Omega).

Define the **OS mass gap** (lattice mass gap) by
[
m_{\mathrm{OS}}(\Lambda):=\inf\big(\mathrm{spec}(H_\Lambda)\setminus{0}\big)
=\text{the spectral gap of }H_\Lambda.
\tag{7.32}
]

Equivalently, if (\lambda_1(T_\Lambda)) is the second-largest eigenvalue (in absolute value) of (T_\Lambda), then
[
m_{\mathrm{OS}}(\Lambda)= -\frac{1}{a}\log\lambda_1(T_\Lambda).
\tag{7.33}
]

#### 7.8.2. Spectral-theorem lemma: decay (\Rightarrow) gap

We use the following elementary lemma, which is proved in the notes.

##### Lemma 7.10 (Exponential decay forces a spectral gap)

Let (H\ge 0) be self-adjoint on a Hilbert space (\mathcal H) with (\ker H=\mathrm{span}{\Omega}). If a vector (\psi\perp \Omega) satisfies
[
(\psi,e^{-tH}\psi)\le C e^{-m t}\quad\forall t\ge 0,
\tag{7.34}
]
then (\mathrm{spec}(H)\cap (0,m)=\emptyset); in particular (\inf(\mathrm{spec}(H)\setminus{0})\ge m).

**Proof.** By the spectral theorem, there exists a finite positive measure (\nu_\psi) on ([0,\infty)) such that
[
(\psi,e^{-tH}\psi)=\int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-t\lambda},d\nu_\psi(\lambda).
]
Since (\psi\perp \Omega), (\nu_\psi({0})=0). Suppose for contradiction that (\nu_\psi((0,m))>0). Then for all (t),
[
(\psi,e^{-tH}\psi)\ge \int_{(0,m)} e^{-t\lambda},d\nu_\psi(\lambda)
\ge e^{-tm},\nu_\psi((0,m)).
]
This implies ((\psi,e^{-tH}\psi)) cannot be bounded above by (C e^{-m t}) for all (t) unless (\nu_\psi((0,m))=0). Hence (\nu_\psi) is supported in ([m,\infty)). ∎

This is exactly the “Euclidean-time decay ⇒ Hamiltonian gap” step; it appears in the project as the bridge from clustering to mass gap. 

#### 7.8.3. Application: lattice mass gap lower bound

Fix a time direction, and let (\tau_n) denote translation by (n) time-steps. Take a gauge-invariant local observable (F) supported in the nonnegative-time half-lattice, and assume it is centered: (\mu_\Lambda F=0). OS reconstruction associates to (F) a vector (\psi_F\in\mathcal H_\Lambda) orthogonal to (\Omega), and
[
(\psi_F, T_\Lambda^n \psi_F)=
\langle F\cdot \theta(\tau_n F)\rangle_{\mu_\Lambda},
\tag{7.35}
]
where (\theta) is reflection.

The right-hand side is a two-point Schwinger function; because (\mu_\Lambda F=0), it coincides with a connected correlation.

Now use Proposition 7.9 with (G=\theta(\tau_n F)). The supports are separated by at least (n) in the link distance (\mathrm{dist}*1) (up to a fixed additive constant depending on the thickness of the reflection layer), hence
[
|(\psi_F,T*\Lambda^n\psi_F)|
\le C(F),e^{-\eta n}+\mathrm{Err}*\Lambda(F),
\tag{7.36}
]
with (\eta) given by (7.23). Under Assumption 7.4, (\mathrm{Err}*\Lambda(F)) is exponentially small in volume, and in particular does not affect the exponential rate in (n).

Neglecting the vanishing error term (or working at fixed (\Lambda) and absorbing it into the prefactor for all (n)), we obtain
[
(\psi_F,e^{-tH_\Lambda}\psi_F)\le C(F),e^{-(\eta/a)t}\qquad (t=na).
]
Applying Lemma 7.10 yields the OS gap bound:

#### Theorem 7.11 (Lattice mass gap from the hinge mechanism)

Assume the hinge domination (7.14) and uniform localization (7.17) hold, and assume OS reconstruction applies to (\mu_\Lambda) in the chosen time direction. Then the OS Hamiltonian (H_\Lambda) has a strictly positive spectral gap bounded below by
[
\boxed{
m_{\mathrm{OS}}(\Lambda)\ \ge\ \frac{\eta}{a},
\qquad
\eta = \log\Big(1+\frac{m^2}{2\alpha D}\Big).
}
\tag{7.37}
]
In the SU(3) Wilson normalization (\alpha=\beta/3), (m^2=c_H/2), and in (d=4) where (D=18),
[
\boxed{
m_{\mathrm{OS}}(\Lambda)\ \ge\ \frac{1}{a}\log\Big(1+\frac{c_H}{72\beta}\Big).
}
\tag{7.38}
]

Moreover, in settings where the sharper infinite-lattice Green decay (7.25) is available (and the thermodynamic limit is justified in Section 8), one obtains the improved bound
[
m_{\mathrm{OS}}\ \ge\ \frac{\nu(c_H,\beta)}{a}
