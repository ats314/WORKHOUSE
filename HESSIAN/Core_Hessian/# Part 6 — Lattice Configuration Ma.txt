## 6.1. Lattice configuration manifold from first principles

### 6.1.1. Finite lattice data

Let (\Lambda\subset \mathbb Z^d) be a **finite** set of vertices (“sites”). Let

* (V(\Lambda)=\Lambda) be the vertex set;
* (E(\Lambda)) be a **finite** set of *oriented* edges (links). Each (e\in E(\Lambda)) is an ordered pair (e=(x\to y)) with (x,y\in V(\Lambda)) neighbors in the chosen lattice adjacency;
* for each oriented edge (e=(x\to y)) there is an inverse oriented edge (\bar e=(y\to x)).

In many lattice gauge conventions one keeps **one orientation per undirected edge** and uses (U_{\bar e}=U_e^{-1}) as a constraint; in other conventions one includes both orientations as independent variables and then constrains. For the present geometric analysis it is cleanest to fix a set (E(\Lambda)) of independent oriented edges and treat each (e\in E(\Lambda)) as carrying one group variable. (Your draft takes precisely this viewpoint by defining (M_\Lambda=G^{E(\Lambda)}).) 

### 6.1.2. Gauge group and link variables

Fix a compact, connected Lie group (G) (for Yang–Mills the motivating example is (G=\mathrm{SU}(N))). Let (\mathfrak g := T_e G) denote its Lie algebra.

A **lattice gauge field configuration** is a choice of a group element (U_e\in G) for every edge (e\in E(\Lambda)). Thus the configuration space is the direct product

[
M_\Lambda ;:=; \prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)} G ;\cong; G^{E(\Lambda)}.
\tag{6.1}
]

This is a smooth compact manifold of dimension (|E(\Lambda)|\cdot \dim G). 

**Basic manifold facts (explicit):**

* Each factor (G) is a smooth finite-dimensional manifold.
* A finite Cartesian product of smooth manifolds is smooth with product charts.
* Compactness of (G) implies compactness of (G^{E(\Lambda)}).

## 6.2. Tangent spaces, invariant trivializations, and the product metric

Your draft uses right translations to identify tangent spaces with (\mathfrak g) link-by-link. 
Here is the full construction.

### 6.2.1. Tangent space of a product manifold

Let (M=\prod_{i=1}^n M_i). A point is (x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)). Then there is a canonical identification

[
T_x M ;\cong; \bigoplus_{i=1}^n T_{x_i} M_i,
\tag{6.2}
]

given by differentiating the projection maps (\pi_i:M\to M_i). Concretely: if (\gamma(t)=(\gamma_1(t),\dots,\gamma_n(t))) is a curve with (\gamma(0)=x), then (\gamma'(0)) corresponds to ((\gamma_1'(0),\dots,\gamma_n'(0))).

Apply this to (M_\Lambda=G^{E(\Lambda)}): for (U=(U_e)_{e\in E(\Lambda)}),

[
T_U M_\Lambda ;\cong; \bigoplus_{e\in E(\Lambda)} T_{U_e}G.
\tag{6.3}
]

### 6.2.2. Right-invariant trivialization (T_{U_e}G\cong\mathfrak g)

For each (g\in G), right multiplication is the diffeomorphism
[
R_g:G\to G,\qquad R_g(h)=hg.
]
Its differential at (h\in G) is a linear map
[
(dR_g)*h : T_h G \to T*{hg}G.
]

In particular, at the identity (e\in G), ((dR_g)_e : T_e G=\mathfrak g \to T_g G) is an isomorphism. Hence we can identify
[
T_gG ;\cong; \mathfrak g
\qquad\text{via}\qquad
X\in\mathfrak g \mapsto (dR_g)_e X \in T_g G.
\tag{6.4}
]

Equivalently, every (V\in T_gG) can be written uniquely as
[
V = (dR_g)*e X
\quad\text{for a unique }X\in\mathfrak g,
]
and we sometimes write (X = (dR*{g^{-1}})_g V).

Applying this link-by-link yields the identification used in your draft:
[
T_U M_\Lambda ;\cong; \mathfrak g^{E(\Lambda)}
\qquad\text{by}\qquad
V=(V_e)*e \mapsto X=(X_e)*e,
;X_e=(dR*{U_e^{-1}})*{U_e}V_e.
\tag{6.5}
]


### 6.2.3. Bi-invariant metric on (G)

Fix an inner product (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}) on (\mathfrak g) that is **(\mathrm{Ad})-invariant**:
[
\langle \mathrm{Ad}_g X,\mathrm{Ad}*g Y\rangle*{\mathfrak g}
============================================================

\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathfrak g}
\quad\forall g\in G,;X,Y\in\mathfrak g.
\tag{6.6}
]

From such an inner product one constructs a **bi-invariant** Riemannian metric (g_G) on (G) by transporting (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}) using left (or right) translations. One standard definition is:

For (g\in G) and (V,W\in T_gG),
[
g_G(g)(V,W)
:=
\left\langle (dL_{g^{-1}})*g V,\ (dL*{g^{-1}})*g W \right\rangle*{\mathfrak g}.
\tag{6.7}
]

* **Left invariance** is immediate because ((dL_{h^{-1}})_{hg}\circ (dL_h)*g = (dL*{g^{-1}})_g).
* **Right invariance** holds precisely because (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}) is (\mathrm{Ad})-invariant and (L_{g^{-1}}\circ R_h = \mathrm{Ad}*{h^{-1}}\circ L*{(gh)^{-1}}) at the Lie algebra level.

So (L_h) and (R_h) are isometries for all (h\in G).

*(For compact semisimple (G), the canonical choice is (\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathfrak g} = -c,B(X,Y)) where (B) is the Killing form and (c>0) is a scaling constant. This will matter in §6.3.)*

### 6.2.4. Product metric on (M_\Lambda)

Given the bi-invariant metric (g_G) on each factor, define the product metric (g_\Lambda) on (M_\Lambda) by orthogonal direct sum:

For (U=(U_e)*e) and (V=(V_e)*e, W=(W_e)*e\in T_U M*\Lambda),
[
g*\Lambda(U)(V,W)
:=
\sum*{e\in E(\Lambda)} g_G(U_e)(V_e,W_e).
\tag{6.8}
]

This is exactly the product metric described in your draft. 

**Key invariance consequence.**
For each edge (e), left/right multiplication in the (e)-th factor,
[
U_e \mapsto h,U_e
\quad\text{or}\quad
U_e \mapsto U_e,h,
]
extends to a diffeomorphism of (M_\Lambda) that is an isometry of ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)), since each factor operation is an isometry on (G) and the metric is a sum of factor metrics.

## 6.3. Ricci curvature of compact Lie groups and products

Your draft states the standing assumption (\mathrm{Ric}*G=\kappa_G g_G) and then the product theorem (\mathrm{Ric}*{g_\Lambda}=\kappa_G g_\Lambda), hence a uniform lower bound independent of (\Lambda). 
We now justify these claims from first principles, including the necessary caveats.

### 6.3.1. Levi–Civita connection for a bi-invariant metric

Let (G) have a bi-invariant Riemannian metric (g_G). Consider **left-invariant vector fields**: for (X\in\mathfrak g), define (\widetilde X) by
[
\widetilde X(g) := (dL_g)_e X.
]
Every left-invariant vector field is of this form, and
[
[\widetilde X,\widetilde Y] = \widetilde{[X,Y]}.
\tag{6.9}
]

Let (\nabla) be the Levi–Civita connection. The Koszul formula says:
[
\begin{aligned}
2,g_G(\nabla_U V, W)
&=
U,g_G(V,W)+V,g_G(W,U)-W,g_G(U,V)\
&\quad+g_G([U,V],W)-g_G([V,W],U)-g_G([W,U],V).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.10}
]

For left-invariant (U=\widetilde X), (V=\widetilde Y), (W=\widetilde Z), all metric pairings (g_G(V,W)) are constant (left invariance), so the derivative terms vanish. Thus
[
2,g_G(\nabla_{\widetilde X}\widetilde Y,\widetilde Z)
=====================================================

## g_G([\widetilde X,\widetilde Y],\widetilde Z)

## g_G([\widetilde Y,\widetilde Z],\widetilde X)

g_G([\widetilde Z,\widetilde X],\widetilde Y).
\tag{6.11}
]

Use ([\widetilde X,\widetilde Y]=\widetilde{[X,Y]}), and evaluate everything at (e) so that (g_G) becomes (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}). Then (6.11) becomes
[
2,\langle \nabla_X Y, Z\rangle
==============================

## \langle [X,Y], Z\rangle

## \langle [Y,Z], X\rangle

\langle [Z,X], Y\rangle.
\tag{6.12}
]

Now use **(\mathrm{Ad})-invariance** of (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle), equivalently:
[
\langle [X,Y], Z\rangle = -\langle Y, [X,Z]\rangle.
\tag{6.13}
]
From (6.13), one derives the symmetry identity
[
\langle [Y,Z],X\rangle = \langle Y,[Z,X]\rangle
\quad\text{and}\quad
\langle [Z,X],Y\rangle = -\langle Z,[X,Y]\rangle.
]
A quick clean way is:

* (6.13) with (X\mapsto Z) gives (\langle [Z,X],Y\rangle=-\langle X,[Z,Y]\rangle).
* Apply (6.13) again to rewrite (\langle [Y,Z],X\rangle = -\langle Z,[Y,X]\rangle = \langle Z,[X,Y]\rangle).

Plugging these into (6.12) collapses the last two terms into (\langle [X,Y],Z\rangle), yielding
[
2,\langle \nabla_X Y, Z\rangle = \langle [X,Y],Z\rangle.
]
Since this holds for all (Z), we conclude:

[
\nabla_X Y = \frac12 [X,Y]
\qquad\text{(for left-invariant vector fields)}.
\tag{6.14}
]

This is the fundamental simplification for bi-invariant metrics.

### 6.3.2. Curvature tensor for a bi-invariant metric

The Riemann curvature tensor (as an operator) is
[
R(U,V)W := \nabla_U\nabla_V W - \nabla_V\nabla_U W - \nabla_{[U,V]}W.
\tag{6.15}
]

Let (U=\widetilde X), (V=\widetilde Y), (W=\widetilde Z) left-invariant. Using (6.14),

[
\nabla_{\widetilde X}\widetilde Y = \frac12 \widetilde{[X,Y]}.
]
Hence
[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\widetilde X}\nabla_{\widetilde Y}\widetilde Z
&= \nabla_{\widetilde X}\Big(\frac12 \widetilde{[Y,Z]}\Big)
= \frac12 \cdot \frac12 \widetilde{[X,[Y,Z]]}
= \frac14 \widetilde{[X,[Y,Z]]}.
\end{aligned}
]
Similarly,
[
\nabla_{\widetilde Y}\nabla_{\widetilde X}\widetilde Z
= \frac14 \widetilde{[Y,[X,Z]]}.
]
And since ([\widetilde X,\widetilde Y]=\widetilde{[X,Y]}),
[
\nabla_{[\widetilde X,\widetilde Y]}\widetilde Z
= \nabla_{\widetilde{[X,Y]}}\widetilde Z
= \frac12 \widetilde{[[X,Y],Z]}.
]

Therefore,
[
R(\widetilde X,\widetilde Y)\widetilde Z
========================================

## \frac14 \widetilde{[X,[Y,Z]]}

## \frac14 \widetilde{[Y,[X,Z]]}

\frac12 \widetilde{[[X,Y],Z]}.
\tag{6.16}
]

Use the Jacobi identity:
[
[X,[Y,Z]] - [Y,[X,Z]] = [[X,Y],Z].
\tag{6.17}
]
Plugging (6.17) into (6.16) gives
[
R(\widetilde X,\widetilde Y)\widetilde Z
========================================

## \frac14 \widetilde{[[X,Y],Z]}

# \frac12 \widetilde{[[X,Y],Z]}

-\frac14 \widetilde{[[X,Y],Z]}.
\tag{6.18}
]

So at the Lie algebra level,
[
R(X,Y)Z = -\frac14 [[X,Y],Z].
\tag{6.19}
]

This sign convention is consistent with **positive sectional curvature** in compact cases (see §6.3.4 check below).

### 6.3.3. Ricci curvature and the Killing form

Let ({E_i}*{i=1}^m) be an orthonormal basis of ((\mathfrak g,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)), where (m=\dim\mathfrak g). The Ricci tensor is the trace of curvature:
[
\mathrm{Ric}(X,Y) = \sum*{i=1}^m \langle R(E_i,X)Y,\ E_i\rangle.
\tag{6.20}
]

Using (6.19),
[
R(E_i,X)Y = -\frac14 [[E_i,X],Y].
]
Hence
[
\mathrm{Ric}(X,Y)
=================

-\frac14 \sum_i \langle [[E_i,X],Y],E_i\rangle.
\tag{6.21}
]

Now use (\mathrm{Ad})-invariance in the form
[
\langle [A,B],C\rangle = \langle A,[B,C]\rangle.
\tag{6.22}
]
Apply (6.22) with (A=[E_i,X]), (B=Y), (C=E_i):
[
\langle [[E_i,X],Y],E_i\rangle
==============================

\langle [E_i,X], [Y,E_i]\rangle.
\tag{6.23}
]
But ([Y,E_i]= -[E_i,Y]), so
[
\langle [E_i,X],[Y,E_i]\rangle
==============================

-\langle [E_i,X],[E_i,Y]\rangle.
\tag{6.24}
]
Therefore
[
\mathrm{Ric}(X,Y)
=================

\frac14 \sum_i \langle [E_i,X],[E_i,Y]\rangle.
\tag{6.25}
]

Now introduce the **Killing form** (B(X,Y)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_X\circ \mathrm{ad}_Y)), where (\mathrm{ad}_X(Z)=[X,Z]). Because (\mathrm{ad}_X) is skew-adjoint under an (\mathrm{Ad})-invariant inner product, one has
[
B(X,Y)
======

-\sum_{i=1}^m \langle [X,E_i],[Y,E_i]\rangle.
\tag{6.26}
]
(Reason: the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product gives (\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_X\mathrm{ad}_Y)=\sum_i \langle \mathrm{ad}_X\mathrm{ad}_Y E_i, E_i\rangle), then move (\mathrm{ad}_X) across as the negative adjoint.)

Comparing (6.25) with (6.26) gives the clean identity
[
\boxed{\mathrm{Ric}(X,Y) = -\frac14,B(X,Y).}
\tag{6.27}
]

### 6.3.4. Positivity and the Einstein constant (\kappa_G)

* For a **compact semisimple** Lie algebra, the Killing form (B) is negative definite. Hence (-B) is positive definite.
* If we choose the metric at the identity to be proportional to (-B),
  [
  \langle X,Y\rangle = -c,B(X,Y),\qquad c>0,
  \tag{6.28}
  ]
  then (6.27) becomes
  [
  \mathrm{Ric}(X,Y) = \frac{1}{4c},\langle X,Y\rangle.
  \tag{6.29}
  ]
  Thus ((G,g_G)) is **Einstein**:
  [
  \boxed{\mathrm{Ric}_G = \kappa_G, g_G \quad\text{with}\quad \kappa_G=\frac1{4c}>0.}
  \tag{6.30}
  ]

This is precisely the standing assumption used in your Part 6 draft. 

**Caveat (important, but manageable):**
If (G) has a torus factor (e.g. (G=U(1)\times SU(N))), then Ricci has zero directions along the torus, so you do **not** get a strictly positive lower bound (\kappa_G>0) for the whole group. For the Yang–Mills mass gap program you are aiming at, (G=\mathrm{SU}(N)) is semisimple, so (\kappa_G>0) is consistent.

### 6.3.5. Ricci tensor of a product manifold

Let ((M_i,g_i)) be Riemannian manifolds, (i=1,\dots,n), and form the product
[
M=\prod_{i=1}^n M_i,\qquad g=\bigoplus_{i=1}^n g_i
\tag{6.31}
]
(the metric is the orthogonal direct sum on tangent spaces as in §6.2.1).

**Claim.** The Levi–Civita connection (\nabla) on (M) splits:
[
\nabla_{(X_1,\dots,X_n)}(Y_1,\dots,Y_n)
=======================================

(\nabla^1_{X_1}Y_1,\dots,\nabla^n_{X_n}Y_n),
\tag{6.32}
]
and curvature and Ricci are block diagonal:
[
\mathrm{Ric}*g = \bigoplus*{i=1}^n \mathrm{Ric}_{g_i}.
\tag{6.33}
]

**Proof sketch with the real mechanism (Koszul again):**
Take vector fields on (M) that are lifts from factors: a vector field (X^{(i)}) that only has a component in the (i)-th factor and is constant in other coordinates. For such fields:

* The metric pairing (g(X^{(i)},Y^{(j)})) vanishes when (i\ne j).
* Lie brackets ([X^{(i)},Y^{(j)}]=0) when (i\ne j) because they act on different coordinate directions.
* When (i=j), all identities reduce to the same identities on ((M_i,g_i)).

Plugging these facts into the Koszul formula (6.10) shows there are no cross-connection terms: (\nabla_{X^{(i)}}Y^{(j)}=0) when (i\ne j), and (\nabla_{X^{(i)}}Y^{(i)}) is exactly the lifted (\nabla^i). Then curvature likewise splits, and Ricci is obtained by tracing curvature in an orthonormal basis that is a union of orthonormal bases from each factor—producing (6.33). ∎

### 6.3.6. Application: uniform Ricci bound on (M_\Lambda)

Now take each (M_i=G) with the same bi-invariant metric (g_G), and (n=|E(\Lambda)|). Then (M_\Lambda=G^{E(\Lambda)}) with product metric (g_\Lambda) is exactly (6.31).

If (\mathrm{Ric}*G=\kappa_G g_G), then (6.33) gives
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{g_\Lambda}=\kappa_G g_\Lambda.
\tag{6.34}
]
In particular,
[
\boxed{\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda} \ge \kappa_G, g_\Lambda}
\quad\text{with}\quad
\kappa_G>0\ \text{depending only on }G\text{ and metric normalization, not on }\Lambda.
\tag{6.35}
]

This is exactly Theorem 6.1 in your draft.

## 6.4. Haar (Riemannian) volume and invariances

Your draft asserts: the Riemannian volume form on ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)) equals the product Haar measure (\prod_e d\mathrm{Haar}_G(U_e)), and it is invariant under linkwise left/right multiplication and under lattice gauge transformations. 
We prove all of this explicitly.

### 6.4.1. Riemannian volume on (G) is Haar (bi-invariant) for a bi-invariant metric

Let ((G,g_G)) be a Riemannian manifold and (\mathrm{vol}_{g_G}) its Riemannian volume measure.

**Lemma 6.4.1.** If (\phi:G\to G) is an isometry, then (\phi_*\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}=\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}).

*Proof.* In local coordinates, (\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}) has density (\sqrt{\det g*{ij}},dx). An isometry preserves the metric tensor and has Jacobian determinant (\pm 1) in orthonormal frames; hence it preserves the volume form (more invariantly: an isometry preserves orthonormal frames and thus the canonical top-degree form). ∎

Now, for a **bi-invariant** metric, every left translation (L_h) and right translation (R_h) is an isometry. By Lemma 6.4.1, (\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}) is both left- and right-invariant:
[
(L_h)** \mathrm{vol}*{g_G} = \mathrm{vol}*{g_G},
\qquad
(R_h)** \mathrm{vol}*{g_G} = \mathrm{vol}_{g_G}.
\tag{6.36}
]

But a (nonzero) left-invariant measure on a compact group is unique up to scalar multiple (uniqueness of Haar measure). Hence (\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}) is a Haar measure, possibly scaled. We choose the normalization so that
[
\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}(G)=1
]
(if desired), making it equal to the normalized Haar probability measure (d\mathrm{Haar}_G).

### 6.4.2. Volume on the product is product Haar

For product manifolds, the Riemannian volume form factorizes.

**Lemma 6.4.2.** Let (M=\prod_{i=1}^n M_i) with product metric (g=\bigoplus g_i). Then
[
\mathrm{vol}*g = \bigotimes*{i=1}^n \mathrm{vol}_{g_i}.
\tag{6.37}
]

*Proof.* In local product coordinates ((x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(n)})), the metric matrix is block diagonal with blocks (g_i). Therefore (\det g = \prod_i \det g_i), hence (\sqrt{\det g}=\prod_i \sqrt{\det g_i}), giving (6.37). ∎

Apply Lemma 6.4.2 with each (M_i=G) and (\mathrm{vol}_{g_G}=d\mathrm{Haar}_G). Then

[
\boxed{
d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U)
============================

\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)} d\mathrm{Haar}_G(U_e).
}
\tag{6.38}
]

This is exactly your statement. 

### 6.4.3. Invariances under linkwise multiplication

Fix an edge (e) and (h\in G). Define a map (\Phi^{L}*{e,h}:M*\Lambda\to M_\Lambda) by
[
(\Phi^{L}*{e,h}(U))*{e'}=
\begin{cases}
hU_e,& e'=e,\
U_{e'},& e'\ne e.
\end{cases}
\tag{6.39}
]
This is (L_h) acting on the (e)-th factor and identity on others.

Because (L_h) preserves (d\mathrm{Haar}_G), the product measure (6.38) is preserved. Identical reasoning applies to right multiplication (U_e\mapsto U_e h).

Thus (d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}) is invariant under **independent** left/right multiplications in each factor. 

### 6.4.4. Gauge transformations as isometries

The **lattice gauge group** is
[
\mathcal G_\Lambda := G^{V(\Lambda)}.
\tag{6.40}
]
An element (g=(g_x)*{x\in V(\Lambda)}) acts on configurations (U=(U_e)) by
[
(g\cdot U)*{(x\to y)} := g_x,U_{(x\to y)},g_y^{-1}.
\tag{6.41}
]

This is a composition of:

* left multiplication by (g_x) on the link variable, and
* right multiplication by (g_y^{-1}).

Each of those is an isometry of (G) under a bi-invariant metric, so the action is an isometry of each factor and hence an isometry of ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)). Therefore, by Lemma 6.4.1, it preserves (\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}). This is exactly the invariance statement in your draft.

## 6.5. Exponential coordinates and the “Haar potential” (S_H)

Your draft introduces exponential coordinates near the identity and defines a “Haar potential” (S_H(X)=-\log J_G(X)), where (J_G) is the Jacobian density of Haar/Riemannian volume in exponential coordinates, and states the key expansion
[
S_H(X)=\frac16 \mathrm{Ric}_G(X,X)+O(|X|^3),
\qquad
\nabla^2 S_H(0)=\frac13 \mathrm{Ric}_G.
\tag{6.42}
]

We now prove these expansions from first principles in Riemannian geometry (specialized to our setting).

### 6.5.1. Riemannian normal coordinates and Jacobian

Let ((M,g)) be any Riemannian manifold and (p\in M). The **Riemannian exponential map**
[
\exp_p:T_p M \to M
]
sends a tangent vector (X) to the point reached at time (1) by the unique geodesic with (\gamma(0)=p), (\gamma'(0)=X).

For (X) in a small neighborhood of (0\in T_pM), (\exp_p) is a diffeomorphism onto a neighborhood (U\subset M). Coordinates (x^i) on (T_pM) (using an orthonormal basis) push forward to **normal coordinates** on (U).

In these coordinates, the Riemannian volume measure has the form
[
d\mathrm{vol}*g(\exp_p X)=J_p(X), dX,
\tag{6.43}
]
where (dX) is Lebesgue measure on (T_pM\cong\mathbb R^n) in the chosen orthonormal basis, and (J_p(X)) is the Jacobian density:
[
J_p(X) = \sqrt{\det(g*{ij}(X))}.
\tag{6.44}
]

In our case (M=G) and (p=e), and with a bi-invariant metric the Riemannian exponential at (e) agrees with the group exponential, so this coordinate chart is exactly “exponential coordinates near the identity” as in your draft. 

### 6.5.2. Metric expansion in normal coordinates

A standard normal-coordinate fact (that we can justify from the geodesic equation and properties of Christoffel symbols) is:

**Lemma 6.5.1 (normal-coordinate metric expansion).**
In normal coordinates centered at (p), the metric components satisfy:
[
g_{ij}(x) = \delta_{ij} - \frac13 R_{ikjl}(p),x^k x^l + O(|x|^3),
\tag{6.45}
]
where (R_{ikjl}) are the components of the curvature tensor at (p), and (O(|x|^3)) denotes smooth terms bounded by (C|x|^3) near (0).

*Why this is true (core idea):*

* In normal coordinates, Christoffel symbols vanish at the origin: (\Gamma^k_{ij}(0)=0).
* Their first derivatives at the origin encode curvature:
  [
  \partial_l \Gamma^k_{ij}(0)= -\frac13 (R^k{}*{ilj}+R^k{}*{jli})(0).
  ]
* Expanding (g_{ij}) by Taylor and using the relation (\Gamma = \frac12 g^{-1}(\partial g)) gives (6.45).

We don’t need the full derivation machinery later—only (6.45) and its implication for the volume density.

### 6.5.3. Volume density expansion and the Ricci term

From (6.45), write
[
g(x) = I + A(x),
\quad\text{where}\quad
A_{ij}(x)= -\frac13 R_{ikjl}(p)x^k x^l + O(|x|^3).
\tag{6.46}
]
Then (A(x)=O(|x|^2)). We need (\sqrt{\det(I+A)}) to second order.

Use the determinant identity:
[
\det(I+A) = \exp(\mathrm{tr}\log(I+A)).
\tag{6.47}
]
For small (A), (\log(I+A)=A+O(|A|^2)). Since (A=O(|x|^2)), we have (O(|A|^2)=O(|x|^4)), which is beyond the order we care about. Thus
[
\log\det(I+A) = \mathrm{tr}(A) + O(|x|^4),
\tag{6.48}
]
and so
[
\sqrt{\det(I+A)} = \exp\Big(\frac12 \mathrm{tr}(A)+O(|x|^4)\Big)
= 1 + \frac12 \mathrm{tr}(A) + O(|x|^4).
\tag{6.49}
]

Now compute (\mathrm{tr}(A)=A_{ii}):
[
\mathrm{tr}(A)(x)
=================

-\frac13 R_{ikil}(p),x^k x^l + O(|x|^3).
\tag{6.50}
]

But (R_{ikil}=\mathrm{Ric}*{kl}) (this is exactly the definition of Ricci as the trace (R^i{}*{kil}), lowered by the metric at (p), which in normal coords is (\delta)). Thus
[
\mathrm{tr}(A)(x) = -\frac13 \mathrm{Ric}_{kl}(p),x^k x^l + O(|x|^3).
\tag{6.51}
]

Plugging (6.51) into (6.49) yields the classical volume expansion:
[
J_p(x)=\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(x)}
= 1 - \frac16 \mathrm{Ric}_{kl}(p),x^k x^l + O(|x|^3).
\tag{6.52}
]

### 6.5.4. Haar potential expansion and Hessian at the identity

Define, exactly as in your draft, the **Haar potential** in exponential coordinates:
[
S_H(x) := -\log J_p(x).
\tag{6.53}
]
For (J_p(x)=1+u(x)) with (u(x)=O(|x|^2)), we have
[
-\log(1+u)= -u + O(u^2) = -u + O(|x|^4).
\tag{6.54}
]
Thus from (6.52),
[
S_H(x) = \frac16 \mathrm{Ric}_{kl}(p),x^k x^l + O(|x|^3).
\tag{6.55}
]

At (p=e\in G), identifying (T_eG\cong\mathfrak g), equation (6.55) is exactly your expansion
[
\boxed{S_H(X)=\frac16\mathrm{Ric}_G(X,X)+O(|X|^3).}
\tag{6.56}
]


Differentiating twice at (0) gives the Hessian:
[
\nabla^2 S_H(0) = \frac13,\mathrm{Ric}*G
\quad\text{as bilinear forms}.
\tag{6.57}
]
Indeed, if (Q(x)=\frac16 \mathrm{Ric}*{kl}x^kx^l), then (\partial_i\partial_j Q(0)=\frac13\mathrm{Ric}_{ij}).

Therefore, if (\mathrm{Ric}_G\ge \kappa_G g_G), then as quadratic forms on (\mathfrak g),
[
\nabla^2 S_H(0) \ge \frac{\kappa_G}{3}, \mathrm{Id}.
\tag{6.58}
]

This is precisely your constant (c_H=\kappa_G/3).

## 6.6. Product Haar potential and uniform convexity at the trivial configuration

Your draft lifts the single-group Haar potential to the full configuration space by product exponential coordinates around the trivial configuration (U^{(0)}), and concludes the Hessian lower bound stays uniform in (\Lambda). 
We now write this carefully.

### 6.6.1. Exponential coordinates at the trivial configuration

Define the **trivial configuration**
[
U^{(0)} := (e)*{e\in E(\Lambda)} \in M*\Lambda.
\tag{6.59}
]
This is the identity element in the product group (G^{E(\Lambda)}).

Using the identification (T_{U^{(0)}} M_\Lambda \cong \mathfrak g^{E(\Lambda)}), define the product exponential chart
[
\mathrm{Exp}*\Lambda : \mathfrak g^{E(\Lambda)} \to M*\Lambda,\qquad
\mathrm{Exp}_\Lambda(X)*e := \exp_G(X_e).
\tag{6.60}
]
For (|X|) small, this is a diffeomorphism from a neighborhood of (0) in (\mathfrak g^{E(\Lambda)}) onto a neighborhood of (U^{(0)}) in (M*\Lambda). 

### 6.6.2. Factorization of volume density and definition of (S_{H,\Lambda})

Because (M_\Lambda) is a product manifold and (g_\Lambda) a product metric, the exponential chart (\mathrm{Exp}_\Lambda) splits into factors, and the volume density is the product of factor densities:

[
d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(\mathrm{Exp}_\Lambda(X))
==================================================

# \prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)} J_G(X_e), dX_e

\exp\Big(-\sum_{e\in E(\Lambda)} S_H(X_e)\Big), dX.
\tag{6.61}
]

Define the **configuration Haar potential**
[
S_{H,\Lambda}(X) := \sum_{e\in E(\Lambda)} S_H(X_e).
\tag{6.62}
]
Then (6.61) reads
[
d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(\mathrm{Exp}_\Lambda(X))
==================================================

e^{-S_{H,\Lambda}(X)}, dX,
\tag{6.63}
]
exactly as in your draft. 

### 6.6.3. Uniform Hessian at the origin

Since (S_{H,\Lambda}) is a sum over edges, its Hessian at (0) is block diagonal, with each block equal to (\nabla^2 S_H(0)). Concretely:

Let (X=(X_e)\in\mathfrak g^{E(\Lambda)}). Then
[
\nabla^2 S_{H,\Lambda}(0)(X,X)
==============================

\sum_{e\in E(\Lambda)} \nabla^2 S_H(0)(X_e,X_e).
\tag{6.64}
]

If (\nabla^2 S_H(0)\ge c_H \mathrm{Id}*{\mathfrak g}), then
[
\nabla^2 S*{H,\Lambda}(0)(X,X)
\ge c_H \sum_{e\in E(\Lambda)} |X_e|^2
= c_H |X|^2_{\mathfrak g^{E(\Lambda)}}.
\tag{6.65}
]
So
[
\boxed{\nabla^2 S_{H,\Lambda}(0) \ge c_H,\mathrm{Id}\quad\text{with }c_H=\kappa_G/3\text{ independent of }\Lambda.}
\tag{6.66}
]

This is your Proposition 6.2. 

### 6.6.4. Geometric vs “potential” viewpoint (equivalence)

Your draft emphasizes a useful equivalence: “positive Ricci curvature of the manifold” and “quadratic confinement in the Haar potential in exponential coordinates” are the same phenomenon, seen in two coordinate systems. 

Formally: the coefficient (\frac16\mathrm{Ric}(p)) in the Taylor expansion of (-\log\sqrt{\det g}) in normal coordinates is a coordinate expression of curvature. In compact Lie groups, that curvature is a representation-theoretic constant (via the Killing form), and therefore the local quadratic term is uniform and does **not** weaken when (|E(\Lambda)|) grows—because it is per-link.

## 6.7. Diffusion generator and analytic objects (configuration-side)

Your draft closes by introducing the “configuration diffusion” generator
[
L_\Lambda=\Delta_{g_\Lambda}-\langle \nabla S_\Lambda,\nabla\cdot\rangle
\tag{6.67}
]
as the reversible Langevin operator for the Gibbs measure (e^{-S_\Lambda}\mathrm{vol}), and it notes that the Haar/Ricci geometry provides a (\Lambda)-uniform curvature input feeding Bakry–Émery and local-to-global inequalities.  

Since you want each part to stand alone, we spell out the definitions cleanly here.

### 6.7.1. Laplace–Beltrami operator and carré du champ

Let ((M,g)) be a smooth Riemannian manifold with volume measure (\mathrm{vol}_g). For a smooth function (f):

* the gradient (\nabla f) is defined by (g(\nabla f, X)=df(X)) for all vector fields (X);
* the divergence (\mathrm{div}(X)) is characterized by (\mathcal L_X \mathrm{vol}_g = (\mathrm{div}X),\mathrm{vol}_g);
* the Laplace–Beltrami operator is (\Delta_g f := \mathrm{div}(\nabla f)).

The basic integration-by-parts identity is:
[
\int_M f,\Delta_g h, d\mathrm{vol}_g = -\int_M \langle \nabla f,\nabla h\rangle, d\mathrm{vol}_g
\quad\text{(for }f,h\in C^\infty(M)\text{)}.
\tag{6.68}
]

This defines the Dirichlet form associated to (\Delta_g):
[
\mathcal E_0(f,h) := \int_M \langle \nabla f,\nabla h\rangle,d\mathrm{vol}_g.
\tag{6.69}
]

The carré du champ operator is (\Gamma(f,h)=\langle \nabla f,\nabla h\rangle).

### 6.7.2. Gibbs measures and Langevin generators on compact manifolds

Let (S:M\to\mathbb R) be a smooth function (“potential”). Define the Gibbs measure
[
d\mu := Z^{-1} e^{-S}, d\mathrm{vol}_g.
\tag{6.70}
]
Then the operator
[
L f := \Delta_g f - \langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle
\tag{6.71}
]
is symmetric in (L^2(\mu)). Indeed, for smooth (f,h),
[
\int f,Lh, d\mu
===============

# \int f(\Delta h - \langle\nabla S,\nabla h\rangle)e^{-S}d\mathrm{vol}

# -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla h\rangle e^{-S}d\mathrm{vol}

\int h,Lf,d\mu.
\tag{6.72}
]

So (L) is the generator of the reversible diffusion with invariant law (\mu). (On compact (M) this diffusion is non-explosive and defines a Markov semigroup (e^{tL}) on (L^2(\mu)).)

### 6.7.3. Application to (M_\Lambda)

Take (M=M_\Lambda), (g=g_\Lambda), and let (S_\Lambda) be the lattice action (e.g. the Wilson action). Then the natural Gibbs law is
[
d\mu_\Lambda := Z_\Lambda^{-1} e^{-S_\Lambda(U)}, d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U),
\tag{6.73}
]
and the reversible generator is exactly your (L_\Lambda):
[
L_\Lambda=\Delta_{g_\Lambda}-\langle \nabla S_\Lambda,\nabla\cdot\rangle.
\tag{6.74}
]


This operator (and its Dirichlet form (\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu_\Lambda)) is the analytic object whose spectral gap you later relate—via functional inequalities and then OS reconstruction—to a lattice mass gap.
