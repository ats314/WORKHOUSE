Part 5 — Abstract Interface to Gauge–Invariant Theories

Up to now we have built:

* A curvature-based analytic engine for finite-dimensional Gibbs measures ((M,g,S,\mu)): Bakry–Émery curvature ⇒ (CD(\rho,\infty)) ⇒ Poincaré and log–Sobolev inequalities with constants (1/\rho) and (2/\rho).
* A scalar lattice prototype where a uniform convexity bound on the potential yields a volume-independent spectral gap and LSI.

The goal of this part is to package these ideas in a form suitable for **gauge theories**—in particular lattice Yang–Mills—but in an **abstract, model-agnostic** way. We want a clean interface:

* Input: a family of finite-volume configuration manifolds ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda,S_\Lambda,\mu_\Lambda)) with a gauge group acting by isometries and leaving the Gibbs measures invariant, plus a curvature lower bound in **physical directions**.
* Output: Poincaré and log–Sobolev inequalities, and hence spectral gaps, for **gauge-invariant observables**, with constants **independent of (\Lambda)**.

This is the module that Part II will instantiate with actual lattice Yang–Mills geometry.

---

## 5.1. Abstract finite-volume configuration family

We fix a family of finite index sets (\Lambda) (typically finite subsets of (\mathbb{Z}^d) or spacetime slabs). For each (\Lambda) we assume:

1. A smooth, connected, finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)).
2. A potential (S_\Lambda\in C^2(M_\Lambda)), bounded below, such that
   [
   Z_\Lambda := \int_{M_\Lambda} e^{-S_\Lambda(x)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(x) < \infty.
   ]
3. The Gibbs measure
   [
   d\mu_\Lambda(x)
   = Z_\Lambda^{-1} e^{-S_\Lambda(x)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(x).
   ]

We define the usual diffusion generator, carré du champ and Dirichlet form:

* Generator on smooth (f):
  [
  L_\Lambda f
  = \Delta_{g_\Lambda} f - \langle \nabla S_\Lambda,\nabla f\rangle_{g_\Lambda}.
  ]
* Carré du champ:
  [
  \Gamma_\Lambda(f,g) = \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle_{g_\Lambda},\qquad
  \Gamma_\Lambda(f) = |\nabla f|*{g*\Lambda}^2.
  ]
* Dirichlet form:
  [
  \mathcal{E}*\Lambda(f,g)
  = \int*{M_\Lambda} \Gamma_\Lambda(f,g),d\mu_\Lambda.
  ]

The Bakry–Émery Ricci tensor is
[
\operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}
:= \operatorname{Ric}*{g*\Lambda} + \nabla^2 S_\Lambda,
]
viewed as a symmetric bilinear form on each tangent space (T_xM_\Lambda).

The (\Gamma_2)-operator is
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)
:= \frac12 L_\Lambda \Gamma_\Lambda(f) - \Gamma_\Lambda(f,L_\Lambda f),
]
and the Bochner–Bakry–Émery identity reads, pointwise,
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)
= |\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2

* \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(\nabla f,\nabla f).
  ]

For a scalar model (no gauge symmetry), a global lower bound
(
\operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda} \ge \rho,g_\Lambda
)
would immediately yield (CD(\rho,\infty)) and hence Poincaré/LSI. The twist now is that gauge theories have **degenerate directions**: moving along gauge orbits doesn’t change physical observables, so we only care about curvature in directions transverse to those orbits.

## 5.2. Gauge symmetry and physical subspaces

We now abstract the notion of a gauge symmetry and its associated decomposition of tangent spaces into “pure gauge” and “physical” directions.

### 5.2.1. Gauge group action

For each finite (\Lambda), we assume a compact Lie group (\mathcal{G}*\Lambda) acts smoothly on (M*\Lambda):
[
\mathcal{G}*\Lambda \times M*\Lambda \to M_\Lambda,\quad (g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x.
]

Hypotheses:

1. **Isometric action.** For every (g\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda), the map (x\mapsto g\cdot x) is an isometry of ((M*\Lambda,g_\Lambda)).
2. **Gauge-invariant potential.** For all (g\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda) and (x\in M*\Lambda),
   [
   S_\Lambda(g\cdot x) = S_\Lambda(x).
   ]

Consequences:

* The volume form (d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}) is invariant under the action.
* The Gibbs measure (\mu_\Lambda) is (\mathcal{G}_\Lambda)-invariant.
* The generator (L_\Lambda) commutes with the action: (L_\Lambda(f\circ g) = (L_\Lambda f)\circ g).

This abstractly encodes the basic lattice gauge symmetry: (\mathcal{G}_\Lambda) is the product of copies of the structure group (G) at each site, acting on link variables by endpoint conjugation. 

### 5.2.2. Vertical subspaces: gauge directions

For each (x\in M_\Lambda), the **gauge orbit** is
[
\mathcal{O}*x := {g\cdot x : g\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda}.
]

Its tangent space at (x) is the **vertical subspace**
[
V_x := T_x \mathcal{O}*x \subset T_xM*\Lambda.
]

Concretely, if (\mathfrak{g}*\Lambda) is the Lie algebra of (\mathcal{G}*\Lambda), then for each (X\in\mathfrak{g}*\Lambda) the one-parameter subgroup (\exp(tX)) generates a vector field
[
X^\sharp(x) := \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|*{t=0} \big(\exp(tX)\cdot x\big),
]
and
[
V_x = { X^\sharp(x) : X\in\mathfrak{g}_\Lambda}.
]

On a dense open set of “regular” points, (\dim V_x) is constant and the orbits form a smooth foliation.

Intuitively, (V_x) consists of **pure gauge variations**: moving along (V_x) changes the representative of the gauge-equivalence class but not the physical configuration.

### 5.2.3. Horizontal subspaces: physical directions

We choose a smooth assignment of **horizontal subspaces** (H_x\subset T_xM_\Lambda) such that
[
T_xM_\Lambda = H_x \oplus V_x
]
and (H_x) is the orthogonal complement of (V_x) with respect to (g_\Lambda):
[
H_x = V_x^\perp.
]

We regard (H_x) as the space of **physical directions** at (x). Properties:

* Since the action is isometric, the decomposition is (\mathcal{G}_\Lambda)-equivariant:
  [
  d(g\cdot)*x(H_x) = H*{g\cdot x},\qquad
  d(g\cdot)*x(V_x) = V*{g\cdot x}.
  ]
* In lattice YM, (H_x) is exactly the orthogonal complement to the infinitesimal gauge transformations in the product group metric.

### 5.2.4. Gauge-invariant observables and horizontal gradients

Let (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) be the algebra of smooth, gauge-invariant observables:
[
\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}
:= { f\in C^\infty(M_\Lambda) : f(g\cdot x) = f(x)\ \forall g\in\mathcal{G}*\Lambda,\ \forall x\in M*\Lambda}.
]

Typical examples in YM are Wilson loops and gauge-invariant polynomials in plaquette holonomies.

If (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) and (X\in\mathfrak{g}*\Lambda), then
[
f(\exp(tX)\cdot x) = f(x)\quad\forall t,
]
so differentiating at (t=0),
[
df_x(X^\sharp(x)) = 0 \quad \forall X.
]
Thus
[
df_x(v) = 0 \quad \forall v\in V_x,
]
and hence
[
g_\Lambda(x)\big(\nabla f(x),v\big) = 0\quad\forall v\in V_x.
]
Therefore:

> **Proposition 5.1 (Horizontal gradients of gauge-invariant observables).**
> For every (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) and every (x\in M*\Lambda),
> [
> \nabla f(x)\in H_x.
> ]

Equivalently, for gauge-invariant observables the carré du champ is purely horizontal:
[
\Gamma_\Lambda(f)(x) = |\nabla f(x)|*{g*\Lambda}^2
= |\nabla^H f(x)|*{g*\Lambda}^2,\qquad \nabla^H f := \text{projection of }\nabla f\text{ to }H.
]

This is the key structural point: **only the horizontal curvature matters** for gauge-invariant functions.

## 5.3. Horizontal Bakry–Émery curvature hypothesis

The Bochner–Bakry–Émery identity gives, for any smooth (f),
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)
= |\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2

* \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(\nabla f,\nabla f).
  ]

If (f) is gauge invariant, then (\nabla f(x)\in H_x). This motivates a curvature hypothesis restricted to (H_x).

### 5.3.1. Horizontal curvature lower bound

We formulate an **abstract** horizontal Bakry–Émery bound:

> **Hypothesis (H_{\mathrm{curv}}) (global horizontal curvature).**
> There exists a constant (\rho_* > 0), **independent of (\Lambda)**, such that for every finite (\Lambda), every (x\in M_\Lambda), and every (v\in H_x),
> [
> \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(x)(v,v)
> = \big(\operatorname{Ric}*{g*\Lambda} + \nabla^2 S_\Lambda\big)(x)(v,v)
> ;\ge; \rho_*,|v|*{g*\Lambda}^2.
> ]

Remarks:

* This is a curvature lower bound only in the physical directions (H_x); we do not require any control on the vertical directions (V_x).
* (\rho_*) must not deteriorate with (\Lambda); this is the “thermodynamic-limit-friendly” part.

In lattice YM, Part II constructs a **local** version of this bound (near the trivial configuration) by combining Haar (Ricci) curvature and the Wilson Hessian. 

### 5.3.2. (CD(\rho_*,\infty)) on gauge-invariant observables

Let (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}). At any (x\in M*\Lambda),
[
\nabla f(x)\in H_x,
]
so Hypothesis (H_{\mathrm{curv}}) implies
[
\operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(x)(\nabla f(x),\nabla f(x))
;\ge; \rho_*,|\nabla f(x)|*{g*\Lambda}^2
= \rho_*,\Gamma_\Lambda(f)(x).
]

Therefore
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)(x)
= |\nabla^2 f(x)|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2

* \operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(x)(\nabla f(x),\nabla f(x))
  ;\ge; \rho_*,\Gamma_\Lambda(f)(x).
  ]

In other words, for gauge-invariant observables we have the curvature–dimension condition
[
\Gamma_{2,\Lambda}(f)\ge \rho_*,\Gamma_\Lambda(f)
\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{A}_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}.
]

If we view (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) as the algebra of smooth functions on the **quotient manifold** (M*\Lambda/\mathcal{G}*\Lambda), then this is precisely a (CD(\rho**,\infty)) bound on the quotient geometry.

## 5.4. Product structure and uniformity in volume

One of the perks of Bakry–Émery curvature is its good behavior under products. That is essential if we want constants that do not blow up with the lattice volume.

### 5.4.1. Product curvature for ungauged systems

Suppose we have a “local degree of freedom” ((M_{\mathrm{loc}},g_{\mathrm{loc}},S_{\mathrm{loc}},\mu_{\mathrm{loc}})) with
[
\operatorname{Ric}*{\mu*{\mathrm{loc}}} \ge \rho_{\mathrm{loc}},g_{\mathrm{loc}}.
]

For a product of (n) independent copies,
[
M = M_{\mathrm{loc}}^n,\qquad g = \bigoplus_{i=1}^n g_{\mathrm{loc}}^{(i)},\qquad S = \sum_{i=1}^n S_{\mathrm{loc}}^{(i)},
]
the product curvature identity gives
[
\operatorname{Ric}*\mu \ge \rho*{\mathrm{loc}},g
]
with the **same** constant (\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}), independent of (n). This is standard and is exactly what we used in the scalar prototype: convexity plus positive Ricci at a single site gives a volume-independent spectral gap on the whole lattice. 

### 5.4.2. Gauge symmetry and quotient geometry

In a gauge theory, (M_\Lambda) is typically a product of groups (one copy per edge), but we then impose a huge redundancy via the gauge group (\mathcal{G}_\Lambda).

Analytically, you can think of two equivalent pictures:

1. Work on the full product manifold (M_\Lambda) with the product curvature bound and then **restrict to gauge-invariant observables**, which see only the horizontal directions; or
2. Work directly on the quotient manifold (or orbifold) (M_\Lambda/\mathcal{G}_\Lambda), where the vertical gauge directions have been modded out.

In either view, the key point is that **local curvature information** on a fixed number of neighboring degrees of freedom can be propagated uniformly in (|\Lambda|) thanks to the product structure. Horizontal curvature bounds at the level of a unit cell then feed into a **volume-uniform (\rho_*)** under Hypothesis (H_{\mathrm{curv}}).

In actual YM, Part II shows that:

* The product Haar metric has Ricci (\operatorname{Ric}*{g*\Lambda} = \kappa_G,g_\Lambda) independent of (\Lambda), and
* The Hessian of the Wilson + additional local terms is bounded below in the horizontal directions near the vacuum, giving a local horizontal (\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}>0) independent of (\Lambda).

The global (H_{\mathrm{curv}}) is then a **target**; in practice we will use a local version plus Lyapunov arguments (Part 3 of the 20-part plan) to get global functional inequalities.

## 5.5. Consequences for gauge-invariant observables

Assume Hypothesis (H_{\mathrm{curv}}) for some (\rho_* > 0) independent of (\Lambda). Then the restriction of (L_\Lambda) and (\Gamma_\Lambda) to (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) satisfies a bona fide (CD(\rho**,\infty)) condition.

Applying the results of Part 3 (CD ⇒ Poincaré/LSI) in this restricted setting, we immediately obtain:

### 5.5.1. Uniform Poincaré inequality in the gauge-invariant sector

For each finite (\Lambda) and every (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) in the form domain,
[
\operatorname{Var}*{\mu_\Lambda}(f)
\le \frac{1}{\rho_*} \int_{M_\Lambda} \Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
]

Equivalently, if we let (-L_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) be the self-adjoint operator generated by (\mathcal{E}*\Lambda) on the closed subspace (L^2(\mu*\Lambda))-closure of (\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}), its spectral gap satisfies
[
\lambda*{1}^{\mathrm{inv}}(\Lambda) \ge \rho_*,
]
with a lower bound **independent of (\Lambda)**.

In words: gauge-invariant observables decorrelate on a timescale (\mathcal{O}(1/\rho_*)) under the configuration-space diffusion, uniformly in lattice size.

### 5.5.2. Uniform log–Sobolev inequality in the gauge-invariant sector

Similarly, the log–Sobolev inequality holds for all smooth (f\in\mathcal{A}*\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}):
[
\operatorname{Ent}*{\mu_\Lambda}(f^2)
\le \frac{2}{\rho_*} \int_{M_\Lambda} \Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
]

This yields:

* Exponential decay of relative entropy for the Langevin semigroup restricted to gauge-invariant observables.
* Concentration inequalities for gauge-invariant functionals, with constants that do not depend on (|\Lambda|).

These are exactly the kinds of bounds one wants for thermodynamic-limit questions and for controlling correlation lengths.

## 5.6. Summary and role of Part 5 in the overall program

This part isolates the **abstract analytic interface** between curvature geometry and gauge-invariant observables:

* Data:

  * A family ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda,S_\Lambda,\mu_\Lambda)) of finite-volume Gibbs systems.
  * A gauge group (\mathcal{G}*\Lambda) acting isometrically and leaving (\mu*\Lambda) invariant.
  * The associated vertical subspaces (V_x) and horizontal (physical) subspaces (H_x=V_x^\perp).
  * The algebra (\mathcal{A}_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}) of gauge-invariant observables, with (\nabla f\in H_x).

* Hypothesis:

  * A **horizontal Bakry–Émery curvature lower bound** (H_{\mathrm{curv}}) with constant (\rho_*>0) independent of (\Lambda).

* Output:

  * Uniform Poincaré and log–Sobolev inequalities for gauge-invariant observables.
  * A spectral gap (\lambda_{1}^{\mathrm{inv}}(\Lambda)\ge\rho_*) for the configuration-space diffusion restricted to the gauge-invariant sector.

In the broader 20-part structure:

* **Part 2–3** developed the curvature ⇒ FI machinery in a general setting.
* **Part 4** gave a scalar example where (H_{\mathrm{curv}}) holds with (\rho_*) explicitly computable from the potential.
* **Part 5 (this part)** shows how to phrase the same mechanism in the presence of gauge symmetry.
* **Part 6–11** will show that lattice Yang–Mills provides concrete ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda,S_\Lambda)) and (\mathcal{G}*\Lambda) for which we can prove a **local** version of (H*{\mathrm{curv}}) (near the trivial connection), with constants independent of volume.

To go from this local curvature to global, volume-uniform Poincaré/LSI, we will need Lyapunov and local-to-global arguments, which are the subject of the next block of parts (12–15 in the 20-part outline, corresponding to your original Part III).
