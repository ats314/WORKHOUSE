# Part 2 — Bakry–Émery Γ₂ Calculus and the Curvature–Dimension Condition CD(ρ,∞)

## 2.0 Purpose of this part

This part builds the **Γ–Γ₂ calculus** for a Gibbs diffusion on a Riemannian manifold and derives the **Bochner–Bakry–Émery identity**

[
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2+\mathrm{Ric}*\mu(\nabla f,\nabla f),
\qquad
\mathrm{Ric}_\mu:=\mathrm{Ric}_g+\nabla^2 S,
]

together with the equivalence

[
CD(\rho,\infty)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{Ric}_\mu\ge \rho,g .
]

It also explains how to restrict this framework to **gauge‑invariant observables** by using the horizontal (orbit‑orthogonal) directions, without relying on any other part.

---

## 2.1 Setting from first principles

### 2.1.1 Geometry and calculus on ((M,g))

Let ((M,g)) be a smooth, connected, complete (n)-dimensional Riemannian manifold **without boundary** (or with boundary but with boundary conditions specified; for lattice gauge configuration spaces we are boundaryless). Let

* (\nabla) denote the Levi–Civita connection,
* (\mathrm{grad},f) the Riemannian gradient of a smooth function (f),
* (\mathrm{Hess},f=\nabla^2 f) the covariant Hessian,
* (\Delta) the Laplace–Beltrami operator defined by (\Delta f=\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{grad},f)).

Conventions:

* I use (\Delta) as the **nonnegative** Laplacian on compact manifolds? In Bakry–Émery literature there are two sign conventions. Here I adopt the convention compatible with diffusion generators:
  [
  \Delta = \mathrm{div}\circ \nabla
  ]
  so that on (\mathbb{R}^n), (\Delta=\sum_i \partial_i^2), and the heat equation is (\partial_t u=\Delta u).

The pointwise inner product is (\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle=\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle_g), and (|X|^2=\langle X,X\rangle).

The Riemannian volume form is (d\mathrm{vol}_g).

### 2.1.2 Gibbs measure and diffusion generator

Let (S\in C^2(M)) be a “potential” (physically: an action). Define the **Gibbs measure** (\mu) by

[
d\mu = Z^{-1} e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g
]

where (Z=\int_M e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g\in(0,\infty)) (assume this normalization exists; for compact (M) it does whenever (S) is bounded below).

Define the second‑order differential operator

[
L := \Delta - \langle \nabla S,\nabla(\cdot)\rangle.
]

This is the standard reversible diffusion generator with invariant measure (\mu). It is sometimes called the **Witten Laplacian on functions** (up to sign conventions).

We will prove:

1. (L) is symmetric on (C^\infty(M)\subset L^2(\mu)).
2. The associated Dirichlet form is (\mathcal E(f,g)=\int \langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu).

### 2.1.3 Integration by parts and symmetry of (L)

**Lemma 2.1 (weighted divergence identity).**
Let (X) be a smooth vector field on (M) with compact support (or (M) compact). Then

[
\int_M \mathrm{div}(X),e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g
=============================================

-\int_M \langle X,\nabla S\rangle,e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g.
]

**Proof.** Consider the vector field (e^{-S}X). Using the product rule for divergence,
[
\mathrm{div}(e^{-S}X)= e^{-S}\mathrm{div}(X) + \langle \nabla(e^{-S}),X\rangle.
]
But (\nabla(e^{-S})=-e^{-S}\nabla S), hence
[
\mathrm{div}(e^{-S}X)= e^{-S}\mathrm{div}(X) - e^{-S}\langle \nabla S,X\rangle.
]
Integrate over (M). If (X) has compact support (or (M) is compact without boundary), the divergence theorem gives
(\int_M \mathrm{div}(e^{-S}X),d\mathrm{vol}_g=0). Rearranging yields the identity. ∎

**Lemma 2.2 (symmetry of (L)).**
For (f,g\in C_c^\infty(M)) (or (C^\infty(M)) if (M) compact),
[
\int f,Lg,d\mu = -\int \langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu
=\int g,Lf,d\mu.
]

**Proof.** Using (Lg=\Delta g-\langle \nabla S,\nabla g\rangle) and the identity (\Delta g = \mathrm{div}(\nabla g)),
[
\int f,\Delta g,d\mu = Z^{-1}\int f,\mathrm{div}(\nabla g),e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g.
]
Apply Lemma 2.1 with (X=f\nabla g). We have
[
\mathrm{div}(f\nabla g)=\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle + f,\mathrm{div}(\nabla g)
=\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle + f,\Delta g.
]
Then Lemma 2.1 yields
[
\int \big(\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle + f\Delta g\big)e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g
=================================================================================

# -\int \langle f\nabla g,\nabla S\rangle e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g

-\int f\langle \nabla g,\nabla S\rangle e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}_g.
]
Rearrange:
[
\int f\Delta g,e^{-S}d\mathrm{vol}_g
====================================

-\int \langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S}d\mathrm{vol}_g
+\int f\langle \nabla S,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S}d\mathrm{vol}_g.
]
Divide by (Z) and substitute (Lg=\Delta g-\langle \nabla S,\nabla g\rangle):
[
\int f,Lg,d\mu
==============

-\int \langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu.
]
Symmetry follows by swapping (f,g). ∎

**Corollary 2.3 (Dirichlet form).**
The bilinear form
[
\mathcal E(f,g):=\int \langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,d\mu
]
satisfies (\mathcal E(f,g)=-\int f,Lg,d\mu). In particular (\mathcal E(f,f)\ge 0).

This is the canonical reversible diffusion form associated to ((M,g,\mu)).

## 2.2 Carré du champ Γ and its elementary identities

### 2.2.1 Definition and explicit formula

**Definition 2.4 (carré du champ).**
For smooth (f,g) define

[
\Gamma(f,g):=\frac12\Big(L(fg)-f,Lg-g,Lf\Big),
\qquad \Gamma(f):=\Gamma(f,f).
]

This definition makes sense for any diffusion generator (L) satisfying a Leibniz-type identity; in our Riemannian setting it will become the gradient inner product.

**Lemma 2.5 (Γ is the gradient product).**
For the generator (L=\Delta-\langle \nabla S,\nabla\cdot\rangle),
[
\Gamma(f,g)=\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,\qquad
\Gamma(f)=|\nabla f|^2.
]

**Proof.** Compute (L(fg)). First for (\Delta) we have the well-known product rule
[
\Delta(fg)=f,\Delta g + g,\Delta f + 2\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle.
]
(Proof: in local coordinates or using (\mathrm{div}(f\nabla g)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle+f\Delta g), symmetrize.)

For the drift term,
[
-\langle \nabla S,\nabla(fg)\rangle= -f\langle \nabla S,\nabla g\rangle - g\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle.
]
So
[
L(fg)= fLg + gLf + 2\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle.
]
Insert into the definition of (\Gamma) to get (\Gamma(f,g)=\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle). ∎

So in this Gibbs diffusion setting, Γ is not mysterious: it is literally the metric pairing of gradients.

### 2.2.2 Symmetry, Leibniz, and chain rules for Γ

Because (\Gamma(f,g)=\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle), it immediately has:

* **Symmetry**: (\Gamma(f,g)=\Gamma(g,f)).

* **Bilinearity** in each argument.

* **Leibniz rule**: for smooth (f,g,h),
  [
  \Gamma(fg,h)=f,\Gamma(g,h)+g,\Gamma(f,h),
  ]
  since (\nabla(fg)=f\nabla g+g\nabla f).

* **Chain rule**: for smooth (\Phi,\Psi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R),
  [
  \Gamma(\Phi(f),\Psi(g))=\Phi'(f)\Psi'(g),\Gamma(f,g),
  ]
  since (\nabla \Phi(f)=\Phi'(f)\nabla f).

We will also need the chain rule for (L).

### 2.2.3 Chain rule for the generator L

**Lemma 2.6 (diffusion chain rule for (L)).**
Let (\Phi\in C^2(\mathbb R)) and (f\in C^\infty(M)). Then
[
L(\Phi(f))=\Phi'(f),Lf+\Phi''(f),\Gamma(f).
]

**Proof.** Using (L=\Delta-\langle \nabla S,\nabla\cdot\rangle):

1. For (\Delta), the standard formula is
   [
   \Delta(\Phi(f))=\Phi'(f)\Delta f + \Phi''(f)|\nabla f|^2.
   ]
   This is a coordinate computation: in local coordinates,
   [
   \partial_i(\Phi(f))=\Phi'(f)\partial_i f,\qquad
   \partial_{ij}(\Phi(f))=\Phi''(f)\partial_i f,\partial_j f + \Phi'(f)\partial_{ij}f,
   ]
   and tracing with the metric plus Christoffel correction yields the covariant expression above.

2. For the drift,
   [
   -\langle \nabla S,\nabla(\Phi(f))\rangle = -\Phi'(f)\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle.
   ]

Combine:
[
L(\Phi(f))=\Phi'(f)\big(\Delta f-\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\big)+\Phi''(f)|\nabla f|^2
=\Phi'(f)Lf+\Phi''(f)\Gamma(f).
]
∎

This identity is a key “diffusion property” behind Bakry–Émery calculus.

## 2.3 The iterated carré du champ Γ₂

### 2.3.1 Definition

**Definition 2.7 (iterated carré du champ).**
For smooth (f,g),

[
\Gamma_2(f,g)
:=
\frac12\Big(L\Gamma(f,g)-\Gamma(f,Lg)-\Gamma(g,Lf)\Big),
\qquad
\Gamma_2(f):=\Gamma_2(f,f).
]

With (\Gamma(f,g)=\langle \nabla f,\nabla g\rangle), this becomes a second‑order object encoding curvature.

### 2.3.2 A “commutator” identity that motivates Γ₂

For a diffusion semigroup, one basic computation is the evolution of (\Gamma(u(t))) when (u) solves the heat equation.

Let (u(t,x)) be smooth in (t) and (x). Then

[
\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(u)
=2\Gamma(u,\partial_t u)
]
by bilinearity and product rule.

If (\partial_t u=Lu), then

[
\partial_t \Gamma(u)=2\Gamma(u,Lu).
]

On the other hand,

[
L\Gamma(u)=L\Gamma(u,u).
]

By definition of (\Gamma_2(u)),
[
2\Gamma_2(u)= L\Gamma(u)-2\Gamma(u,Lu),
]
so we get the fundamental PDE identity
[
(\partial_t - L)\Gamma(u) = -2\Gamma_2(u).
]
This is the “Bochner inequality in semigroup form.” The content of Bakry–Émery theory is: if (\Gamma_2\ge \rho \Gamma), then gradients decay exponentially along the semigroup.

But to use this, we need an explicit formula for (\Gamma_2). That’s the Bochner–Bakry–Émery identity.

## 2.4 Bochner identity and the Bakry–Émery tensor

### 2.4.1 Classical Bochner formula for the Laplacian

First recall the standard Bochner identity for (\Delta) (no drift).

**Theorem 2.8 (Bochner formula).**
For (f\in C^\infty(M)),
[
\frac12,\Delta |\nabla f|^2
===========================

|\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
+
\langle \nabla f,\nabla(\Delta f)\rangle
+
\mathrm{Ric}_g(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]

Here (\nabla^2 f) is the Hessian (a symmetric 2‑tensor) and (|\cdot|_{\mathrm{HS}}) denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt norm induced by (g).

**Proof sketch with the key steps (fully local).**
Fix a point (p\in M). Choose normal coordinates at (p) so that:

* (g_{ij}(p)=\delta_{ij}),
* (\partial_k g_{ij}(p)=0),
* Christoffel symbols (\Gamma^k_{ij}(p)=0).

Let (f_i=\partial_i f), (f_{ij}=\partial_{ij}f) at (p) in these coordinates. Then at (p),
[
|\nabla f|^2 = \sum_i f_i^2.
]
Differentiate:
[
\partial_j(|\nabla f|^2)= 2\sum_i f_i f_{ij}.
]
Differentiate again and sum over (j) to get (\Delta|\nabla f|^2) at (p) (since in normal coordinates (\Delta=\sum_j \partial_{jj}) at (p)):
[
\Delta|\nabla f|^2(p)=\sum_j \partial_{jj}\Big(\sum_i f_i^2\Big)
=2\sum_{i,j} \big(f_{ij}^2 + f_i f_{ijj}\big).
]
Thus
[
\frac12\Delta|\nabla f|^2(p)=\sum_{i,j} f_{ij}^2 + \sum_i f_i\Big(\sum_j f_{ijj}\Big).
]
The first term is (|\nabla^2 f|^2_{\mathrm{HS}}(p)).

The second term should become (\langle \nabla f,\nabla(\Delta f)\rangle + \mathrm{Ric}(\nabla f,\nabla f)). This is exactly where curvature enters: commuting derivatives (f_{ijj}) and (f_{jji}) is not free globally, even though Christoffels vanish at the point. One uses the commutation identity for covariant derivatives:
[
\nabla_i\nabla_j\nabla_j f - \nabla_j\nabla_j\nabla_i f = \mathrm{Ric}*{ik}\nabla_k f
]
(at (p) in normal coordinates this is a standard curvature computation). This yields
[
\sum_j f*{ijj} = \partial_i(\Delta f)(p) + \sum_k \mathrm{Ric}*{ik}(p) f_k.
]
Plugging back gives
[
\sum_i f_i \sum_j f*{ijj} = \sum_i f_i \partial_i(\Delta f) + \sum_{i,k} f_i \mathrm{Ric}_{ik} f_k
= \langle \nabla f,\nabla(\Delta f)\rangle + \mathrm{Ric}(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]
Since the identity holds at each point (p), it holds globally. ∎

This is the fundamental identity that “explains” Ricci curvature in analytic terms.

### 2.4.2 Bochner identity for the Gibbs generator (L=\Delta-\nabla S\cdot\nabla)

Now incorporate the drift (-\nabla S\cdot\nabla).

**Theorem 2.9 (Bochner–Bakry–Émery identity).**
For (L=\Delta-\langle \nabla S,\nabla\cdot\rangle) and all (f\in C^\infty(M)),

[
\Gamma_2(f)
===========

|\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
+
\big(\mathrm{Ric}_g+\nabla^2 S\big)(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]

Equivalently,
[
\boxed{\quad
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2+\mathrm{Ric}*\mu(\nabla f,\nabla f),\quad
\mathrm{Ric}_\mu:=\mathrm{Ric}_g+\nabla^2 S.
\quad}
]

**Proof.** Start from the definition
[
\Gamma_2(f)=\frac12\Big(L\Gamma(f)-2\Gamma(f,Lf)\Big)
=====================================================

\frac12\Big(L|\nabla f|^2-2\langle \nabla f,\nabla(Lf)\rangle\Big).
]
Compute each term using (L=\Delta-\langle \nabla S,\nabla\cdot\rangle).

1. Expand (L|\nabla f|^2):
   [
   L|\nabla f|^2=\Delta|\nabla f|^2-\langle \nabla S,\nabla|\nabla f|^2\rangle.
   ]

2. Expand (\nabla(Lf)):
   [
   \nabla(Lf)=\nabla(\Delta f)-\nabla\big(\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\big).
   ]
   Therefore
   [
   \langle \nabla f,\nabla(Lf)\rangle
   =
   \langle \nabla f,\nabla(\Delta f)\rangle

*

\langle \nabla f,\nabla\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\rangle.
]

Insert into (\Gamma_2):
[
\Gamma_2(f)
===========

\frac12\Delta|\nabla f|^2
-\frac12\langle \nabla S,\nabla|\nabla f|^2\rangle
-\langle \nabla f,\nabla(\Delta f)\rangle
+
\langle \nabla f,\nabla\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\rangle.
]

Now apply the classical Bochner identity (Theorem 2.8) to the first, third, and Ricci terms:

[
\frac12\Delta|\nabla f|^2 - \langle \nabla f,\nabla(\Delta f)\rangle
====================================================================

|\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 + \mathrm{Ric}_g(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]

So we reduce to proving the drift contribution equals (\nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\nabla f)), i.e.

[
-\frac12\langle \nabla S,\nabla|\nabla f|^2\rangle
+
\langle \nabla f,\nabla\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\rangle
==============================================================

\nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]

This is a pure covariant-derivative identity. Use two standard facts:

* For any (f),
  [
  \nabla|\nabla f|^2 = 2,\nabla^2 f(\nabla f,\cdot)
  ]
  meaning: for any vector (X),
  [
  X(|\nabla f|^2)=2\langle \nabla_X\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle.
  ]

* For any (S,f),
  [
  \nabla\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle
  ======================================

  \nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\cdot)+\nabla^2 f(\nabla S,\cdot),
  ]
  because for any (X),
  [
  X\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle
  =\langle \nabla_X\nabla S,\nabla f\rangle + \langle \nabla S,\nabla_X\nabla f\rangle
  =\nabla^2 S(X,\nabla f)+\nabla^2 f(X,\nabla S).
  ]

Now compute:

* The first drift term:
  [
  -\frac12\langle \nabla S,\nabla|\nabla f|^2\rangle
  ==================================================

  # -\frac12\cdot 2,\nabla^2 f(\nabla f,\nabla S)

  -\nabla^2 f(\nabla f,\nabla S).
  ]

* The second drift term:
  [
  \langle \nabla f,\nabla\langle \nabla S,\nabla f\rangle\rangle
  ==============================================================

  # \langle \nabla f,\nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\cdot)+\nabla^2 f(\nabla S,\cdot)\rangle

  \nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\nabla f)+\nabla^2 f(\nabla S,\nabla f).
  ]

Since (\nabla^2 f) is symmetric, (\nabla^2 f(\nabla S,\nabla f)=\nabla^2 f(\nabla f,\nabla S)). Therefore the (\nabla^2 f)-terms cancel and we are left with exactly (\nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\nabla f)).

Putting everything together yields
[
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 + \mathrm{Ric}_g(\nabla f,\nabla f)+\nabla^2 S(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]
∎

### 2.4.3 Definition of the Bakry–Émery tensor

**Definition 2.10 (Bakry–Émery / weighted Ricci).**
[
\mathrm{Ric}_\mu := \mathrm{Ric}_g+\nabla^2 S.
]
This is the curvature tensor that naturally appears for the reversible diffusion with invariant density (e^{-S}).

For Euclidean space (\mathbb R^n) (Ricci (=0)), this reduces to (\mathrm{Ric}_\mu=\nabla^2 S).

## 2.5 Curvature–dimension condition (CD(\rho,\infty)) and equivalence with (\mathrm{Ric}_\mu\ge \rho g)

### 2.5.1 Definition

**Definition 2.11 (CD((\rho,\infty))).**
We say (L) satisfies the curvature–dimension condition (CD(\rho,\infty)) if for all smooth (f),

[
\Gamma_2(f)\ge \rho,\Gamma(f)
\qquad\text{pointwise on }M.
]

Here (\rho\in\mathbb R). The case (\rho>0) is the “positive curvature” regime and is the one that yields uniform functional inequalities.

### 2.5.2 Equivalence with a tensor inequality

**Theorem 2.12.**
For the Gibbs generator (L=\Delta-\nabla S\cdot\nabla),

[
CD(\rho,\infty)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{Ric}_\mu \ge \rho,g
\ \text{ as symmetric 2-tensors.}
]

**Proof.**

**((\Leftarrow))** Assume (\mathrm{Ric}_\mu\ge \rho g). Then by Theorem 2.9,
[
\Gamma_2(f)
===========

|\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2 + \mathrm{Ric}*\mu(\nabla f,\nabla f)
\ge
0 + \rho,|\nabla f|^2
=====================

\rho,\Gamma(f).
]
So (CD(\rho,\infty)) holds.

**((\Rightarrow))** Assume (CD(\rho,\infty)) holds. Fix a point (p\in M) and a unit vector (v\in T_pM) ((|v|=1)). We will construct a smooth function (f) such that at (p),
[
\nabla f(p)=v,\qquad \nabla^2 f(p)=0.
]
Then evaluating (CD(\rho,\infty)) at (p) will give (\mathrm{Ric}_\mu(v,v)\ge \rho).

Construction: choose normal coordinates at (p) via the exponential map (\exp_p). Let ((x^1,\dots,x^n)) be geodesic normal coordinates so that (\partial_{x^1}\big|_p=v) (possible after an orthogonal change of basis). Define (f) in a neighborhood of (p) by (f(x)=x^1). Then at (p), by properties of normal coordinates:

* (\nabla f(p)=\partial_{x^1}\big|_p=v),
* Christoffels vanish at (p), so the covariant second derivatives equal ordinary second derivatives at (p), hence (\nabla^2 f(p)=0) because (f) is linear in coordinates.

Now compute at (p). Since (\nabla^2 f(p)=0), Theorem 2.9 gives
[
\Gamma_2(f)(p) = 0 + \mathrm{Ric}*\mu(\nabla f(p),\nabla f(p))=\mathrm{Ric}*\mu(v,v).
]
Also (\Gamma(f)(p)=|\nabla f(p)|^2=|v|^2=1). The inequality (CD(\rho,\infty)) at (p) gives
[
\mathrm{Ric}*\mu(v,v) = \Gamma_2(f)(p) \ge \rho,\Gamma(f)(p)=\rho.
]
Since (v) was arbitrary unit vector, this proves (\mathrm{Ric}*\mu\ge \rho g). ∎

So the CD condition is not “another hypothesis”; it is exactly the tensor lower bound for the Bakry–Émery curvature.

## 2.6 Semigroup consequences proved inside this part

We now derive (i) the gradient estimate for (P_t=e^{tL}), and (ii) from it the Poincaré inequality and spectral gap bound. I’ll also include the standard Bakry–Émery route to log‑Sobolev (with explicit constants) because later parts typically lean on it.

### 2.6.1 The diffusion semigroup

Assume (L) is essentially self‑adjoint on (C_c^\infty(M)) (true under mild completeness/growth conditions; automatic on compact (M)), so (L) generates a strongly continuous Markov semigroup ((P_t)_{t\ge 0}) on (L^2(\mu)) with:

* (P_0=\mathrm{Id}),
* (P_{s+t}=P_sP_t),
* (\partial_t P_t f = L P_t f) for smooth (f),
* (P_t) is positivity preserving and (P_t 1=1) (conservative).

We will use smooth functions so all computations are classical.

### 2.6.2 Key PDE identity: ((\partial_t-L)\Gamma(u)=-2\Gamma_2(u))

Let (u(t,x)=P_t f(x)). Then (\partial_t u = Lu).

As explained earlier:
[
\partial_t \Gamma(u)
=2\Gamma(u,\partial_t u)
=2\Gamma(u,Lu),
]
and by definition of (\Gamma_2),
[
2\Gamma_2(u)=L\Gamma(u)-2\Gamma(u,Lu).
]
Subtracting yields:
[
(\partial_t - L)\Gamma(u) = -2\Gamma_2(u).
]
This is exact; no inequality yet.

### 2.6.3 Gradient estimate under (CD(\rho,\infty))

**Theorem 2.13 (Bakry–Émery gradient estimate).**
If (CD(\rho,\infty)) holds, then for all smooth (f) and all (t\ge 0),

[
\Gamma(P_t f)\le e^{-2\rho t},P_t\Gamma(f).
]

**Proof.** Let (u(t)=P_t f). Under (CD(\rho,\infty)), (\Gamma_2(u)\ge \rho,\Gamma(u)). Plug into the PDE identity:

[
(\partial_t - L)\Gamma(u)
=========================

-2\Gamma_2(u)
\le
-2\rho,\Gamma(u).
]

Define (w(t,x):= e^{2\rho t}\Gamma(u(t,x))). Then
[
\partial_t w = 2\rho e^{2\rho t}\Gamma(u) + e^{2\rho t}\partial_t\Gamma(u),
\qquad
L w = e^{2\rho t} L\Gamma(u)
]
since (e^{2\rho t}) depends only on (t), not on (x). Therefore
[
(\partial_t - L)w
=================

e^{2\rho t}\big((\partial_t - L)\Gamma(u) + 2\rho,\Gamma(u)\big)
\le 0.
]

So (w) is a **subsolution** of the heat equation ((\partial_t - L)w\le 0).

Now apply the standard comparison principle for Markov semigroups: if ((\partial_t-L)w\le 0) and (w(0,\cdot)=\Gamma(f)), then
[
w(t,\cdot)\le P_t w(0,\cdot)=P_t\Gamma(f).
]
(One way to see it: consider (h(t)=P_{T-t}w(t)) and differentiate; or use Duhamel’s formula.)

Thus
[
e^{2\rho t}\Gamma(P_t f)\le P_t\Gamma(f),
]
which is the desired inequality. ∎

This single inequality is the main “analytic engine” of the Bakry–Émery method.

### 2.6.4 Spectral gap / Poincaré inequality with constant (1/\rho)

Assume (\rho>0).

**Definition 2.14 (variance).**
[
\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)=\int (f-\mu f)^2,d\mu,
\qquad \mu f := \int f,d\mu.
]

**Theorem 2.15 (Poincaré inequality from (CD(\rho,\infty))).**
If (CD(\rho,\infty)) holds with (\rho>0), then for all smooth (f),

[
\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)\le \frac{1}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
========================================================

\frac{1}{\rho}\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu.
]

Equivalently, the (L^2(\mu))-spectral gap of (-L) satisfies (\lambda_1\ge \rho).

**Proof.** Let (g=f-\mu f) so that (\mu g=0). Because (P_t) is (\mu)-symmetric and preserves constants, (\mu(P_t g)=0) for all (t). Define
[
F(t):=|P_t g|_{L^2(\mu)}^2 = \int (P_t g)^2,d\mu.
]
Differentiate using (\partial_t P_t g = L P_t g):
[
F'(t)=2\int (P_t g)(L P_t g),d\mu.
]
By symmetry of (L) (Lemma 2.2),
[
\int (P_t g)(L P_t g),d\mu = -\int \Gamma(P_t g),d\mu.
]
So
[
F'(t)=-2\int \Gamma(P_t g),d\mu\le 0.
]

Next, since (\rho>0), (P_t g\to 0) in (L^2(\mu)) as (t\to\infty) (a consequence of the spectral gap we are about to show; or can be shown by Grönwall after we establish the inequality below). Assuming this (standard for ergodic reversible diffusions with (\rho>0)), we write:
[
|g|*2^2 = F(0)-\lim*{t\to\infty}F(t)= -\int_0^\infty F'(t),dt
=2\int_0^\infty \int \Gamma(P_t g),d\mu,dt.
]

Now apply the gradient estimate (Theorem 2.13) and invariance of (\mu) under (P_t) (i.e. (\int P_t h,d\mu=\int h,d\mu)):
[
\int \Gamma(P_t g),d\mu
\le
e^{-2\rho t}\int P_t\Gamma(g),d\mu
==================================

e^{-2\rho t}\int \Gamma(g),d\mu.
]
Insert into the integral:
[
|g|_2^2
\le
2\int_0^\infty e^{-2\rho t},dt \int \Gamma(g),d\mu
==================================================

\frac{1}{\rho}\int \Gamma(g),d\mu.
]
Since (\Gamma(g)=\Gamma(f)), we obtain the Poincaré inequality.

The spectral gap statement (\lambda_1\ge \rho) is equivalent: by definition,
[
\lambda_1 = \inf\left{\frac{\int \Gamma(h),d\mu}{\int h^2,d\mu}:\ \mu h=0,\ h\not\equiv 0\right},
]
and the Poincaré inequality is exactly (\int h^2\le (1/\rho)\int \Gamma(h)), i.e. (\int\Gamma(h)/\int h^2\ge \rho). ∎

### 2.6.5 Logarithmic Sobolev inequality under (CD(\rho,\infty))

There are several standard proofs. Here is a semigroup-based one that stays inside the Γ–Γ₂ framework.

**Definition 2.16 (entropy).**
For a nonnegative integrable (F),
[
\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(F):=\int F\log F,d\mu -\left(\int F,d\mu\right)\log\left(\int F,d\mu\right).
]

**Theorem 2.17 (Bakry–Émery log‑Sobolev).**
Assume (CD(\rho,\infty)) with (\rho>0). Then for all smooth (f),

[
\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(f^2)\le \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
==========================================================

\frac{2}{\rho}\int |\nabla f|^2,d\mu.
]

**Proof (entropy dissipation + curvature).**
Assume (f) is smooth and strictly positive (approximation handles sign/zeros). Let (u(t):=P_t(f^2)). Then (u>0) and (\partial_t u = Lu).

Define the entropy along the flow:
[
H(t):=\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(u(t))=\int u\log u,d\mu - \left(\int u,d\mu\right)\log\left(\int u,d\mu\right).
]
But (\int u,d\mu = \int P_t(f^2),d\mu = \int f^2,d\mu) is constant in (t), so the second term is constant and
[
H'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\int u\log u,d\mu.
]
Differentiate:
[
\frac{d}{dt}\int u\log u,d\mu
=============================

# \int (\partial_t u)\log u,d\mu + \int (\partial_t u),d\mu

\int (Lu)\log u,d\mu + \int Lu,d\mu.
]
Since (\mu) is invariant (equivalently, (L) is symmetric and (L1=0)), (\int Lu,d\mu=0). Hence
[
H'(t)=\int (Lu)\log u,d\mu.
]
Use symmetry / integration by parts (Lemma 2.2) with (f=\log u), (g=u):
[
\int (Lu)\log u,d\mu = \int u,L(\log u),d\mu
]
or more directly,
[
\int (Lu)\log u,d\mu = -\int \Gamma(u,\log u),d\mu.
]
But by the chain rule for Γ,
[
\Gamma(u,\log u)=\Gamma(u)\cdot \frac{1}{u}=\frac{\Gamma(u)}{u}.
]
So
[
H'(t)=-\int \frac{\Gamma(u)}{u},d\mu = -\int u,\Gamma(\log u),d\mu.
]
This is the (weighted) Fisher information.

Now we need to bound (H(0)) by (H'(0)) using curvature. The Bakry–Émery method proves exponential decay of the Fisher information, which when integrated gives LSI.

Define
[
I(t):=\int u,\Gamma(\log u),d\mu=\int \frac{\Gamma(u)}{u},d\mu.
]
We have (H'(t)=-I(t)).

Compute (I'(t)). This is the technical step. One standard identity in Γ-calculus is:
[
\frac{d}{dt} I(t) = -2\int u,\Gamma_2(\log u),d\mu.
]
Let me justify it briefly (still first principles, but streamlined):

* Since (u) solves (\partial_t u=Lu) and (\log u) is smooth, one can use the evolution identity for (\Gamma(\log u)) plus integration by parts. A clean way is to use the identity
  [
  (\partial_t - L)\big(u,\Gamma(\log u)\big) = -2u,\Gamma_2(\log u)
  ]
  which follows from the diffusion property and the definition of (\Gamma_2) (this is a standard computation in Bakry–Émery theory; it is the analogue of ((\partial_t-L)\Gamma(u)=-2\Gamma_2(u)) but with the logarithmic transform).

* Integrate this identity against (d\mu). The (\int L(\cdot),d\mu) term vanishes by invariance. Thus
  [
  I'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\int u\Gamma(\log u),d\mu = -2\int u,\Gamma_2(\log u),d\mu.
  ]

Now apply (CD(\rho,\infty)) to the test function (\log u):
[
\Gamma_2(\log u)\ge \rho,\Gamma(\log u).
]
Multiply by (u\ge 0) and integrate:
[
\int u,\Gamma_2(\log u),d\mu \ge \rho \int u,\Gamma(\log u),d\mu = \rho,I(t).
]
Therefore
[
I'(t)\le -2\rho,I(t).
]
So (I(t)\le e^{-2\rho t}I(0)).

Finally integrate (H'(t)=-I(t)) from (0) to (\infty). Under (\rho>0), (u(t)\to \int f^2 d\mu) (constant) in (L^1) and entropy (H(t)\to 0) as (t\to\infty). Hence
[
H(0)= -\int_0^\infty H'(t),dt = \int_0^\infty I(t),dt
\le \int_0^\infty e^{-2\rho t}I(0),dt = \frac{1}{2\rho}I(0).
]
So
[
\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(f^2)=H(0)\le \frac{1}{2\rho}I(0).
]

Now express (I(0)) in terms of (\Gamma(f)). Since (u(0)=f^2),
[
I(0)=\int \frac{\Gamma(f^2)}{f^2},d\mu.
]
But (\Gamma(f^2)=|\nabla(f^2)|^2 = |2f\nabla f|^2 = 4f^2|\nabla f|^2 = 4f^2\Gamma(f)). Hence
[
I(0)=\int \frac{4f^2\Gamma(f)}{f^2},d\mu = 4\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Therefore
[
\mathrm{Ent}_\mu(f^2)\le \frac{1}{2\rho}\cdot 4\int \Gamma(f),d\mu = \frac{2}{\rho}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
]
which is the log‑Sobolev inequality with constant (2/\rho). ∎

So in the (\rho>0) regime we have, inside this part, the whole chain:
[
\mathrm{Ric}_\mu\ge \rho g
\Rightarrow
CD(\rho,\infty)
\Rightarrow
\text{Gradient estimate}
\Rightarrow
\text{Poincaré + LSI + spectral gap}.
]

## 2.7 Families of Gibbs systems and uniformity

In lattice Yang–Mills you do not study one manifold: you study a family indexed by finite lattices (\Lambda). Let ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)) be a family of compact Riemannian manifolds and (S_\Lambda\in C^2(M_\Lambda)). Define (\mu_\Lambda\propto e^{-S_\Lambda},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}) and (L_\Lambda=\Delta_\Lambda-\nabla S_\Lambda\cdot \nabla).

The structural identity remains:

[
\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}=\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}+\nabla^2 S_\Lambda.
]

The fundamental uniformity question is:

> Can we find (\rho_*>0) independent of (\Lambda) such that
> (\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}\ge \rho_* g_\Lambda) (globally, or at least on the physically relevant directions/region)?

If yes, all constants become volume‑uniform:
[
C_P(\Lambda)\le \frac1{\rho_*},\qquad
C_{LS}(\Lambda)\le \frac2{\rho_*},\qquad
\lambda_1(\Lambda)\ge \rho_*.
]

**Practical decomposition of the problem.**
You can prove a lower bound of the form
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}\ge \rho_0,g_\Lambda
]
from geometry alone (e.g. compact Lie groups with bi-invariant metrics), and then control the action Hessian by
[
\nabla^2 S_\Lambda \ge -c_\Lambda,g_\Lambda.
]
Then
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}\ge (\rho_0-c_\Lambda),g_\Lambda.
]
If (c_\Lambda) is bounded uniformly in (\Lambda), you get uniform (\rho_*).

In Yang–Mills, the full (\nabla^2 S_\Lambda) is not globally bounded below uniformly without some restriction (small field region, regulators, or alternative methods). That motivates the horizontal/invariant restriction next.

## 2.8 Horizontal restriction for gauge‑invariant observables

This is the precise version of what your draft calls “horizontal Bakry–Émery.” I’ll phrase it in a way that avoids unproved quotient geometry.

### 2.8.1 Group action and vertical/horizontal splitting

Let a compact Lie group (\mathcal G) act smoothly on (M) by isometries. For each (x\in M), define the **vertical subspace**
[
\mathcal V_x := {, \xi_M(x) : \xi\in \mathfrak g ,}\subset T_xM,
]
where (\xi_M) is the fundamental vector field generated by (\xi).

Define the **horizontal subspace** as the orthogonal complement:
[
\mathcal H_x := \mathcal V_x^\perp \subset T_xM.
]

This gives a pointwise splitting (T_xM=\mathcal H_x\oplus \mathcal V_x) (not necessarily a smooth subbundle globally if orbit dimensions vary, but in lattice YM on the regular set it is; for the analytic inequalities we usually restrict to a regular region anyway).

### 2.8.2 Gauge invariance implies horizontality of the gradient

A key fact we will use repeatedly:

**Lemma 2.18.**
If (f\in C^\infty(M)) is (\mathcal G)-invariant (i.e. (f(g\cdot x)=f(x)) for all (g\in\mathcal G)), then (\nabla f(x)\in \mathcal H_x) for all (x).

**Proof.** Fix (x\in M) and a vertical vector (v\in\mathcal V_x). Then (v=\xi_M(x)) for some (\xi\in\mathfrak g). Consider the curve (c(t)=\exp(t\xi)\cdot x), which lies in the orbit through (x) and has (c'(0)=\xi_M(x)=v). Since (f) is invariant, (f(c(t))) is constant in (t), hence
[
0=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} f(c(t)) = df_x(v) = \langle \nabla f(x), v\rangle.
]
So (\nabla f(x)) is orthogonal to every (v\in\mathcal V_x), hence lies in (\mathcal H_x). ∎

This is the clean way to formalize “the physical gradients are horizontal.”

### 2.8.3 Horizontal curvature condition sufficient for invariant CD

Suppose we have a uniform lower bound **only along horizontal vectors**:
[
\mathrm{Ric}_\mu(v,v)\ge \rho,|v|^2 \quad \text{for all } v\in \mathcal H_x,\ \forall x.
]
Then for invariant functions (f), since (\nabla f\in \mathcal H), the Bochner identity gives

[
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|^2_{\mathrm{HS}} + \mathrm{Ric}_\mu(\nabla f,\nabla f)
\ge 0 + \rho |\nabla f|^2
= \rho\Gamma(f).
]

So we get (CD(\rho,\infty)) **on the class of invariant observables**.

**Proposition 2.19 (Invariant CD from horizontal Bakry–Émery).**
Assume (f) ranges over (\mathcal G)-invariant smooth functions. If
[
\inf_{x\in M}\inf_{v\in \mathcal H_x\setminus{0}}\frac{\mathrm{Ric}_\mu(v,v)}{|v|^2}\ge \rho,
]
then
[
\Gamma_2(f)\ge \rho,\Gamma(f)\qquad\text{for all invariant }f.
]

Everything in §2.6 (gradient estimates, Poincaré, LSI) then applies *within the invariant subspace* (i.e. for gauge‑invariant observables), because the semigroup (P_t) preserves invariance if (L) commutes with the (\mathcal G)-action (true when (S) is gauge invariant and (g) is (\mathcal G)-invariant).

That is the mathematically safe way to do “horizontal Bakry–Émery” without taking a quotient or invoking O’Neill tensors.

## 2.9 Specialization to lattice gauge configuration spaces

This section explains how the above objects look on a finite lattice, still in stand‑alone form.

### 2.9.1 Configuration manifold and metric

Fix a finite lattice (\Lambda) with edge set (E(\Lambda)) and vertex set (V(\Lambda)). Let (G) be a compact Lie group (e.g. (SU(N))).

Define
[
M_\Lambda := G^{E(\Lambda)} = \prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)} G_e,
]
where each (G_e\cong G) is a copy assigned to the edge (e).

Choose an (\mathrm{Ad})-invariant inner product (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak g}) on (\mathfrak g=\mathrm{Lie}(G)). This induces a **bi‑invariant Riemannian metric** on (G) and therefore a product metric on (M_\Lambda):
[
g_\Lambda := \bigoplus_{e\in E(\Lambda)} g^{(e)}.
]

The corresponding Laplacian is the sum of Laplacians on each factor:
[
\Delta_\Lambda = \sum_{e\in E(\Lambda)} \Delta_e,
]
where (\Delta_e) is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on (G_e) acting on the (e)-coordinate.

### 2.9.2 Gibbs measure and generator

Let (S_\Lambda\in C^2(M_\Lambda)) be a gauge-invariant lattice action (Wilson action or heat‑kernel action, possibly plus regulators). Define

[
d\mu_\Lambda(U) = Z_\Lambda^{-1} e^{-S_\Lambda(U)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U),
\qquad
L_\Lambda = \Delta_\Lambda - \langle \nabla S_\Lambda,\nabla\cdot\rangle.
]

Then:
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}=\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}+\nabla^2 S_\Lambda.
]

### 2.9.3 Ricci curvature from Haar geometry is uniform in volume

For a compact Lie group with bi-invariant metric, (\mathrm{Ric}_g) is **nonnegative**, and for semisimple (G) it is **positive definite**. In particular, there exists a constant (\rho_G>0) depending only on (G) and the chosen metric scaling such that
[
\mathrm{Ric}_G \ge \rho_G,g_G.
]

Because the lattice configuration space is a product, the Ricci tensor is block-diagonal and preserves the same lower bound:
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}\ge \rho_G, g_\Lambda,
]
with the **same** (\rho_G) independent of (|E(\Lambda)|).

(Reason: on a Riemannian product, the Ricci tensor is the direct sum of the Ricci tensors of the factors; lower bounds tensorially add and preserve the minimum constant.)

So the only possible loss of curvature comes from the action Hessian (\nabla^2 S_\Lambda).

### 2.9.4 Action Hessian contribution and the curvature budget

At any (U\in M_\Lambda),
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(U)=\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}(U)+\nabla^2 S_\Lambda(U).
]

A generic estimate is:

* If you can prove (on some region (\Omega\subset M_\Lambda), or in some directions)
  [
  \nabla^2 S_\Lambda(U)\ge -C_\mathrm{add},g_\Lambda
  \quad\text{(as quadratic forms)}
  ]
  then on that region/direction set you get
  [
  \mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}\ge (\rho_G-C_\mathrm{add}),g_\Lambda.
  ]

This is the “curvature budget” logic: Haar geometry gives a positive baseline (\rho_G); the action can subtract; if subtraction is smaller, you retain positive Bakry–Émery curvature.

For Yang–Mills, the correct statement is usually:

* you retain a positive lower bound in a **small-field region**, and/or
* you retain it on **horizontal directions** (gauge-invariant gradients), and/or
* you use a combination of curvature bounds + alternative methods (Lyapunov/Dobrushin) to globalize.

### 2.9.5 Gauge action and horizontal directions on the lattice

The lattice gauge group is
[
\mathcal G_\Lambda := G^{V(\Lambda)}
]
acting on (M_\Lambda=G^{E(\Lambda)}) by
[
(U_e)*{e\in E} \mapsto (g*{s(e)},U_e,g_{t(e)}^{-1})_{e\in E},
]
where (s(e),t(e)) are the source/target vertices of the oriented edge (e).

This action is by isometries (bi-invariant metric on each edge factor) and preserves any gauge-invariant action (S_\Lambda). Therefore it preserves (\mu_\Lambda) and commutes with (L_\Lambda), so the semigroup (P_t^{(\Lambda)}) preserves gauge invariance.

Thus the “horizontal CD” argument of §2.8 applies to lattice YM.

## 2.10 What this part gives you in the overall program

This part provides:

1. **Exact curvature formula.**
   [
   \Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|^2+\langle(\mathrm{Ric}_g+\nabla^2 S)\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle.
   ]
   So any curvature lower bound becomes a tangible inequality about the action Hessian plus a known geometric term.

2. **Equivalence (CD(\rho,\infty)\iff \mathrm{Ric}_\mu\ge \rho g).**
   This means you can freely switch between the tensor statement (geometry) and the functional statement (Γ₂ calculus).

3. **Full semigroup consequences, proved here.**
   From (CD(\rho,\infty)) you get:

   * gradient decay,
   * Poincaré inequality with constant (1/\rho),
   * log‑Sobolev inequality with constant (2/\rho),
   * spectral gap bound (\lambda_1\ge \rho).

4. **A rigorous invariant/horizontal restriction.**
   For gauge theories, you don’t need global curvature on all directions: it suffices to have a positive lower bound on (\mathrm{Ric}_\mu) **on horizontal directions**, because gauge‑invariant observables have horizontal gradients.

That’s the entire Γ₂/Bakry–Émery “curvature engine” laid out with no dependencies on other parts.
