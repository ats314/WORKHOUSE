Introduction: The Unification of Symmetry and DynamicsThis report provides a comprehensive mathematical exposition of the geometric and probabilistic structures underpinning modern theoretical physics and geometric analysis. It expands upon the fundamental concepts of Lie group geometry, the spectral properties of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, the rigorous formulation of stochastic processes on manifolds, the algebraic machinery of Bakry-Émery $\Gamma$-calculus, and the preservation of gauge invariance in lattice field theories.The central theme unifying these diverse mathematical landscapes is the concept of invariance. In Lie group geometry, we investigate metrics invariant under group translations; in spectral geometry, we analyze operators invariant under isometries (the Laplacian and Casimir elements); in stochastic analysis, we construct diffusion processes that leave the Boltzmann-Gibbs measure invariant; and in lattice gauge theory, we demand that the discretized action remains invariant under local gauge transformations. The derivations presented herein are exhaustive, retracing the steps from foundational definitions to advanced theorems, supported by the referenced literature.The necessity of this deep dive arises from the complex interplay between the curvature of the state space and the behavior of dynamic systems evolving upon it. Whether one is studying the heat diffusion on a curved manifold, the convergence of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms for Bayesian inference, or the confinement of quarks in Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD), the underlying mathematical architecture remains remarkably consistent. This report elucidates these connections through detailed proofs and derivations.2. Geometry of Lie Groups: Curvature and The Levi-Civita ConnectionThe study of Lie groups equipped with Riemannian metrics serves as the foundational bedrock for understanding symmetries in physical systems. While the algebraic structure of a Lie group $G$ is determined by its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, the geometric structure—geodesics, curvature, and stability—depends on the choice of a Riemannian metric. We focus here on left-invariant metrics, which allow us to reduce global geometric problems on the manifold $G$ to algebraic problems on the tangent space at the identity, $\mathfrak{g} \cong T_e G$.2.1 Derivation of the Levi-Civita ConnectionLet $G$ be a Lie group of dimension $n$ and $\mathfrak{g}$ its Lie algebra of left-invariant vector fields. A Riemannian metric $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ on $G$ is said to be left-invariant if the left translation maps $L_g: G \to G$ are isometries for all $g \in G$. This implies that the inner product on $T_g G$ is determined essentially by the inner product on $T_e G$ via push-forward: $\langle u, v \rangle_g = \langle (L_{g^{-1}})_* u, (L_{g^{-1}})_* v \rangle_e$.The Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$ is the unique torsion-free, metric-compatible affine connection on $(G, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$. It is determined by the Koszul formula:$$2\langle \nabla_X Y, Z \rangle = X\langle Y, Z \rangle + Y\langle Z, X \rangle - Z\langle X, Y \rangle
- \langle Y, [X, Z] \rangle - \langle Z, \rangle + \langle X, \rangle$$For left-invariant vector fields $X, Y, Z \in \mathfrak{g}$, the scalar product $\langle Y, Z \rangle$ is a constant function on $G$. Consequently, the derivative terms $X\langle Y, Z \rangle$ vanish identically. The formula simplifies to a purely algebraic expression involving the Lie bracket and the metric 1:$$2\langle \nabla_X Y, Z \rangle = \langle, Z \rangle - \langle, X \rangle + \langle [Z, X], Y \rangle$$This equation allows us to compute the covariant derivative $\nabla_X Y$ solely in terms of the algebraic structure of $\mathfrak{g}$.2.1.1 Connection Coefficients in an Orthonormal BasisLet $\{e_1, \dots, e_n\}$ be an orthonormal basis of $\mathfrak{g}$ with respect to the metric $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$. The structure of the algebra is encoded in the structure constants $\alpha_{ijk}$, defined by:$$[e_i, e_j] = \sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_{ijk} e_k$$Note that by the antisymmetry of the Lie bracket, $\alpha_{ijk} = -\alpha_{jik}$. However, no symmetry is assumed for the third index $k$ in the general case. We wish to find the coefficients $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ such that $\nabla_{e_i} e_j = \sum_k \Gamma^k_{ij} e_k$. Substituting the basis vectors into the simplified Koszul formula:$$2\langle \nabla_{e_i} e_j, e_k \rangle = \langle [e_i, e_j], e_k \rangle - \langle [e_j, e_k], e_i \rangle + \langle [e_k, e_i], e_j \rangle$$Using the orthonormality condition $\langle e_a, e_b \rangle = \delta_{ab}$, the inner product terms simplify directly to the structure constants:$\langle [e_i, e_j], e_k \rangle = \langle \sum_m \alpha_{ijm} e_m, e_k \rangle = \alpha_{ijk}$$\langle [e_j, e_k], e_i \rangle = \alpha_{jki}$$\langle [e_k, e_i], e_j \rangle = \alpha_{kij}$Thus, we obtain the explicit formula for the connection coefficients derived by Milnor 1:$$\Gamma^k_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} (\alpha_{ijk} - \alpha_{jki} + \alpha_{kij})$$This result is fundamental. It demonstrates that for a left-invariant metric, the "forces" (represented by the connection coefficients) experienced by a particle moving along a geodesic are linear functions of the velocity components, determined entirely by the Lie algebra structure constants.2.1.2 The Bi-Invariant CaseA metric is bi-invariant if it is invariant under both left and right translations. This imposes a strong constraint on the structure constants. In a bi-invariant metric, the adjoint map $\text{ad}_X$ is skew-symmetric with respect to the inner product: $\langle, Z \rangle = -\langle Y, [X, Z] \rangle$.In terms of structure constants, this implies total antisymmetry:$$\alpha_{ijk} = \langle [e_i, e_j], e_k \rangle = -\langle e_j, [e_i, e_k] \rangle = -\alpha_{ikj}$$Combined with the standard antisymmetry $\alpha_{ijk} = -\alpha_{jik}$, the structure constants become totally antisymmetric in all three indices. Substituting this into the connection formula:$$\Gamma^k_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} (\alpha_{ijk} - \alpha_{jki} + \alpha_{kij})$$Since indices can be cyclically permuted in a totally antisymmetric tensor, $\alpha_{jki} = \alpha_{ijk}$ and $\alpha_{kij} = \alpha_{ijk}$. Thus:$$\Gamma^k_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} (\alpha_{ijk} - \alpha_{ijk} + \alpha_{ijk}) = \frac{1}{2} \alpha_{ijk}$$Reassembling the vector, we find:$$\nabla_{e_i} e_j = \frac{1}{2} \sum_k \alpha_{ijk} e_k = \frac{1}{2} [e_i, e_j]$$Or, in coordinate-free notation 1:$$\nabla_X Y = \frac{1}{2}$$This confirms that for bi-invariant metrics, the geodesics through the identity are exactly the one-parameter subgroups $\gamma(t) = \exp(tX)$, since $\nabla_X X = \frac{1}{2}[X, X] = 0$.2.2 Curvature Tensor Derivations and Milnor's TheoremThe stability of geodesics and the topological constraints on the group are determined by the Riemann curvature tensor $R$.2.2.1 The Riemann Curvature TensorThe operator is defined as $R(X, Y) = \nabla_X \nabla_Y - \nabla_Y \nabla_X - \nabla_{}$. For left-invariant vector fields on a Lie group with a general left-invariant metric, using the connection derived above, a lengthy but algebraic calculation yields the curvature.For the orthonormal basis $\{e_i\}$, the sectional curvature of the plane spanned by distinct $e_i, e_j$ is $K_{ij} = \langle R(e_i, e_j)e_j, e_i \rangle$. Milnor 1 provided a simplified diagonalization to analyze this. He assumed an orthogonal basis where the bracket $[e_i, e_j]$ is essentially diagonalized (which is possible for many 3D groups). However, the general expression in terms of $\alpha_{ijk}$ is:$$\langle R(e_i, e_j)e_j, e_i \rangle = \sum_k \left( \Gamma^k_{jj} \Gamma^i_{ik} - (\Gamma^k_{ij})^2 \right) + \dots$$If we define specific structure constants $c_{ij}^k$ such that $[e_i, e_j] = c_{ij}^k e_k$, and assume the simplified case where cross-terms vanish (as in Milnor's "special" basis for 3D unimodular groups where $[e_1, e_2] = \lambda_3 e_3$, etc.), the sectional curvatures take the elegant form:$$K(e_1, e_2) = \frac{1}{2} (\lambda_1 \lambda_2 \lambda_3 - \lambda_1^2 \lambda_2 \lambda_3 \dots) \quad \text{(Milnor's simplified forms)}$$More precisely, let $\mu_i$ be constants related to the structure constants in a diagonalized basis. Milnor proved that the sign of the curvature is dictated by the signs of these structure constants.Theorem (Milnor on Curvature Signs) 1:Let $G$ be a 3-dimensional Lie group with a left-invariant metric. The Ricci principal directions align with the basis diagonalizing the Lie bracket. The signature of the Ricci curvature (number of positive, negative, and zero eigenvalues) classifies the group structure. For example:If $[e_1, e_2] = e_3, [e_2, e_3] = e_1, [e_3, e_1] = e_2$ (forming $\mathfrak{su}(2)$), all sectional curvatures are positive (specifically $K=1/4$ for the bi-invariant metric).If the group is non-unimodular (e.g., solvable groups), negative curvature arises naturally.2.2.2 Ricci and Scalar CurvatureThe Ricci curvature $Ric(X, Y) = \text{Tr}(Z \to R(Z, X)Y)$ is derived by contracting the Riemann tensor. For a unimodular Lie group, the scalar curvature $S$ satisfies:$$S = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i,j,k} \alpha_{ijk}^2 - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} (\text{Tr}(\text{ad}_{e_i}))^2 \quad \text{(general trace form)}$$For unimodular groups, $\text{Tr}(\text{ad}_X) = 0$, so the scalar curvature is determined purely by the sum of squares of the structure constants. If the metric is bi-invariant, $S = \frac{1}{4} \sum \alpha_{ijk}^2$, which is strictly non-negative. This leads to the topological constraint that compact Lie groups with finite fundamental groups admit metrics of positive curvature, while others may not.2.3 The Adjoint Representation and Semisimple GroupsFor a semisimple Lie group, the Killing form $B(X, Y) = \text{Tr}(\text{ad}_X \circ \text{ad}_Y)$ is non-degenerate. The Laplace-Beltrami Operator as Sum of SquaresThe correspondence between the geometric Laplace-Beltrami operator and the algebraic Casimir element is a pivot point connecting analysis and representation theory.3.1 Definition and Coordinate DerivationOn a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$, the Laplace-Beltrami operator $\Delta$ acts on functions $f \in C^\infty(M)$ as the divergence of the gradient 4:$$\Delta f = \text{div}(\text{grad} f) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}} \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i} \left( \sqrt{\det g} \, g^{ij} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j} \right)$$This operator is the unique second-order differential operator that is invariant under isometries and self-adjoint with respect to the $L^2$ inner product $(u, v) = \int_M u v \, d\text{vol}_g$.3.2 Left-Invariant Vector Fields and DivergenceConsider a Lie group $G$ with a left-invariant metric. Let $\{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$ be an orthonormal basis of left-invariant vector fields. We can express the gradient and divergence in this frame.The gradient is simply $\text{grad} f = \sum_i (X_i f) X_i$.The divergence of a vector field $V = \sum_i v_i X_i$ is:$$\text{div} V = \sum_i X_i(v_i) + v_i \text{div}(X_i)$$To compute $\text{div}(X_i)$, recall that the divergence is the trace of the operator $Y \mapsto \nabla_Y X_i$.$$\text{div}(X_i) = \sum_j \langle \nabla_{X_j} X_i, X_j \rangle$$Using the Koszul formula for the connection coefficients:$$\langle \nabla_{X_j} X_i, X_j \rangle = \frac{1}{2} (\langle [X_j, X_i], X_j \rangle - \langle [X_i, X_j], X_j \rangle + \langle [X_j, X_j], X_i \rangle)$$The last term is zero. The first two terms involve $\langle [X_j, X_i], X_j \rangle$.If the group is unimodular, the trace of the adjoint map $\text{ad}_{X_i}$ is zero. It can be shown that for a left-invariant metric on a unimodular group, $\text{div}(X_i) = 0$ for all basis vectors.6Result:Substituting $\text{div}(X_i) = 0$ into the Laplacian definition:$$\Delta f = \text{div}(\sum_i (X_i f) X_i) = \sum_i X_i(X_i f) + (X_i f) \text{div}(X_i) = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 f$$This proves that on a unimodular Lie group (which includes all compact, semisimple, and nilpotent groups), the Laplace-Beltrami operator is exactly the sum of squares of the orthonormal generators.3.3 The Casimir Element CorrespondenceThe Casimir element $\Omega$ resides in the center of the universal enveloping algebra $U(\mathfrak{g})$. For a semisimple algebra with Killing form $B$, let $\{e_i\}$ be a basis and $\{e^i\}$ the dual basis ($B(e_i, e^j) = \delta_{ij}$). The quadratic Casimir is $\Omega = \sum e_i e^i$.7If we equip $G$ with the bi-invariant metric induced by $-B$ (for compact $G$), an orthonormal basis $\{X_i\}$ is self-dual. Thus, identifying the Lie algebra elements with left-invariant differential operators:$$\Omega = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2$$Proof of Identity:Geometric Operator: We established in 3.2 that $\Delta_{LB} = \sum X_i^2$ for a unimodular group with left-invariant metric.Algebraic Operator: The image of the Casimir $\Omega$ under the left-regular representation on $C^\infty(G)$ is $\sum X_i^2$.Uniqueness: Since the metric is bi-invariant, the operator must commute with both left and right translations. The Casimir is the unique second-order operator (up to scaling) in the center of $U(\mathfrak{g})$, ensuring bi-invariance.Conclusion: $\Delta_{LB} \equiv \Omega$.This identity allows us to compute the spectrum of the Laplacian using representation theory. For an irreducible representation $\pi_\lambda$ with highest weight $\lambda$, the Laplacian acts as a scalar $c(\lambda)$:$$\Delta f = -(\langle \lambda, \lambda + 2\rho \rangle) f$$where $\rho$ is the Weyl vector (half sum of positive roots). This formula 8 is the analytic engine behind harmonic analysis on spheres $S^n \cong SO(n+1)/SO(n)$ and other symmetric spaces.3.4 Laplacian on p-formsThe correspondence extends to differential forms. The Laplacian on forms is $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$. On a group manifold, this can be expressed via the Weitzenböck formula:$$\Delta \omega = -\text{Tr}(\nabla^2 \omega) + \text{Ric}(\omega)$$For a bi-invariant metric, algebraic derivations 9 show that the Laplacian on forms decomposes into left and right Casimir operators:$$\Delta^{(p)} = \frac{1}{2} (C_L + C_R)$$where $C_L$ and $C_R$ are the Casimir operators acting on the left and right regular representations, respectively. This generalizes the scalar case (where $C_L = C_R$ on central functions) to the full exterior algebra.4. Stochastic Analysis on Manifolds: Diffusion GeneratorsStochastic analysis generalizes Newton's laws to environments dominated by thermal fluctuations. On a manifold, the "force" of noise must respect the curvature. The primary object of study is the diffusion generator, which encodes the local infinitesimal behavior of the process.4.1 Derivation of the Diffusion GeneratorLet $X_t$ be a continuous Markov process on a manifold $M$. The generator $\mathcal{L}$ is defined by its action on test functions $f$:$$\mathcal{L} f(x) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X_t)|X_0=x] - f(x)}{t}$$Geometrically, we construct Brownian motion on $M$ via the Eells-Elworthy-Malliavin construction.10 We lift the process to the orthonormal frame bundle $O(M)$. Let $U_t \in O(M)$ be a frame at $X_t$. The horizontal lift of Euclidean Brownian motion $dB_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is defined by the Stratonovich SDE:$$dU_t = \sum_{i=1}^n H_i(U_t) \circ dB^i_t$$where $H_i$ are the canonical horizontal vector fields on $O(M)$ associated with the Levi-Civita connection. The projection $X_t = \pi(U_t)$ is Riemannian Brownian motion.Using Itô's formula for the projection, the generator is found to be 10:$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n E_i^2 = \frac{1}{2} \Delta_M$$where $\Delta_M$ is the Laplace-Beltrami operator. (Note: Factors of 1/2 vary by convention; in geometric analysis, $\Delta$ often denotes the geometer's Laplacian which is negative definite, while probabilists use $\frac{1}{2}\Delta$ positive).4.2 Langevin Dynamics and the Weighted LaplacianIn sampling applications (e.g., computational physics, Bayesian statistics), we construct a diffusion process $X_t$ that converges to a target Gibbs measure $\mu(dx) = e^{-V(x)} \text{vol}_g(dx)$, where $V$ is a potential energy.The process is described by the Overdamped Langevin SDE on the manifold 11:$$dX_t = -\text{grad} V(X_t) dt + \sqrt{2} dB_t^M$$where $dB_t^M$ is the Riemannian Brownian motion.Derivation of the Generator:We seek the operator $L_V$ associated with this SDE. The generator consists of the drift term and the diffusion term.Diffusion Term: The noise $\sqrt{2} dB_t$ generates the operator $\frac{(\sqrt{2})^2}{2} \Delta = \Delta$.Drift Term: The deterministic drift $-\text{grad} V$ generates the first-order operator $-\langle \text{grad} V, \nabla \cdot \rangle$.Combining these, the generator is:$$L_V f = \Delta f - \langle \nabla V, \nabla f \rangle$$This operator is known as the Weighted Laplacian or Witten Laplacian.13Proof of Invariance (Integration by Parts):To prove that $e^{-V}$ is the invariant density, we show that $L_V$ is symmetric (self-adjoint) with respect to the weighted inner product $\langle f, g \rangle_V = \int f g e^{-V} d\text{vol}$.Using the divergence theorem on the manifold (assuming no boundary or rapid decay):$$\begin{aligned}
\int_M (\Delta f) g e^{-V} d\text{vol} &= \int_M \text{div}(\nabla f) g e^{-V} d\text{vol} \\
&= -\int_M \langle \nabla f, \nabla (g e^{-V}) \rangle d\text{vol} \\
&= -\int_M \langle \nabla f, (\nabla g) e^{-V} - g e^{-V} \nabla V \rangle d\text{vol} \\
&= -\int_M \langle \nabla f, \nabla g \rangle e^{-V} d\text{vol} + \int_M \langle \nabla f, \nabla V \rangle g e^{-V} d\text{vol}
\end{aligned}
$$Now consider the term from the drift:$$
\int_M (-\langle \nabla V, \nabla f \rangle) g e^{-V} d\text{vol}
$$Adding the two parts of $L_V$:$$
\int_M (L_V f) g e^{-V} d\text{vol} = -\int_M \langle \nabla f, \nabla g \rangle e^{-V} d\text{vol}$$This expression is symmetric in $f$ and $g$. Thus, $L_V$ is self-adjoint, and the Dirichlet form is $\mathcal{E}(f,g) = \int \langle \nabla f, \nabla g \rangle d\mu$. The constant function $f=1$ is in the kernel ($L_V 1 = 0$), implying stationarity of the measure $\mu$.134.3 Second-Order Insight: Geometry Accelerates SamplingThe derivation $L_V = \Delta - \nabla V \cdot \nabla$ reveals why Riemannian Manifold Langevin Dynamics (RMLA) outperforms Euclidean sampling. In Euclidean space, curvature is zero, and $\Delta = \sum \partial_i^2$. On a manifold (or a probability simplex), the metric $g_{ij}$ preconditions the gradient descent. The term $\Delta$ contains the Christoffel symbols which effectively correct the trajectory for the curvature of the space.Specifically, if we choose the metric $g$ to be the Fisher Information metric (making the manifold the statistical manifold of the likelihood), the diffusion adapts naturally to the "geometry of information," taking larger steps in flat directions and smaller steps in highly curved directions. This geometric preconditioning is proven to reduce the mixing time of the Markov chain from exponential to polynomial in the dimension for certain non-convex distributions.115. Bakry-Émery $\Gamma$-Calculus and Bochner's FormulaBakry and Émery abstracted the geometric notions of curvature into the algebraic properties of diffusion operators. This allows the derivation of functional inequalities (like Poincaré and Log-Sobolev) in settings where a Riemannian manifold may not formally exist (e.g., infinite-dimensional spaces or discrete graphs).5.1 The Carré du Champ OperatorsGiven a generator $L$ (satisfying the diffusion property, i.e., chain rule), we define the Carré du Champ operator $\Gamma$ ("square of the field") 15:$$\Gamma(f, g) = \frac{1}{2} \left( L(fg) - f Lg - g Lf \right)$$Derivation: For the Laplace-Beltrami operator $L=\Delta$:$$\Delta(fg) = (\Delta f)g + f(\Delta g) + 2\langle \nabla f, \nabla g \rangle$$Substituting this into the definition immediately yields:$$\Gamma(f, g) = \langle \nabla f, \nabla g \rangle$$Thus, $\Gamma$ recovers the Riemannian metric from the operator.We iterate this procedure to define the second-order operator $\Gamma_2$:$$\Gamma_2(f, g) = \frac{1}{2} \left( L \Gamma(f, g) - \Gamma(f, Lg) - \Gamma(g, Lf) \right)$$5.2 Derivation of the Bochner-Weitzenböck Formula via $\Gamma_2$The significance of $\Gamma_2$ is revealed by the Bochner formula from Riemannian geometry.17 For a smooth function $f$:$$\frac{1}{2} \Delta |\nabla f|^2 = |\text{Hess} f|^2 + \langle \nabla f, \nabla (\Delta f) \rangle + \text{Ric}(\nabla f, \nabla f)$$Let us map this to the $\Gamma$ calculus notation:$|\nabla f|^2 = \Gamma(f, f)$.$\frac{1}{2} \Delta |\nabla f|^2 = \frac{1}{2} L \Gamma(f, f)$.$\langle \nabla f, \nabla (\Delta f) \rangle = \Gamma(f, Lf)$.Substituting these into the Bochner formula:$$\frac{1}{2} L \Gamma(f, f) - \Gamma(f, Lf) = |\text{Hess} f|^2 + \text{Ric}(\nabla f, \nabla f)$$Comparing this with the definition of $\Gamma_2(f, f) = \frac{1}{2} L \Gamma(f, f) - \Gamma(f, Lf)$, we obtain the fundamental identity:$$\Gamma_2(f, f) = |\text{Hess} f|^2 + \text{Ric}(\nabla f, \nabla f)$$5.3 Curvature-Dimension Condition $CD(K, N)$This identity allows us to define lower bounds on Ricci curvature purely through the operator $L$. We say the operator satisfies the Curvature-Dimension condition $CD(K, N)$ if 19:$$\Gamma_2(f, f) \ge K \Gamma(f, f) + \frac{1}{N} (Lf)^2$$For the case of diffusion on a weighted manifold $(M, g, e^{-V} d\text{vol})$ with generator $L_V = \Delta - \nabla V \cdot \nabla$, we must re-evaluate $\Gamma_2$.The algebraic computation yields the Bakry-Émery Ricci tensor:$$\Gamma_2(f, f) = |\text{Hess} f|^2 + (\text{Ric} + \text{Hess} V)(\nabla f, \nabla f)$$This result proves that the "effective curvature" seen by the diffusion is the sum of the intrinsic geometric curvature and the Hessian of the potential.Implication: A space might have negative Ricci curvature ($\text{Ric} < 0$), but if the potential $V$ is strongly convex ($\text{Hess} V \gg 0$), the sum can be positive. This explains why Langevin dynamics converges exponentially fast for log-concave distributions even in flat or negatively curved spaces—the potential creates an "effective" positive curvature that confines the process.136. Gauge Invariance in Lattice Gauge TheoryLattice Gauge Theory (LGT) provides a non-perturbative regularization of Quantum Field Theories. The central challenge is to discretize spacetime without breaking the gauge symmetry, which is essential for the physical consistency of the theory (e.g., current conservation, masslessness of gluons).6.1 Geometric Setup: Links, Plaquettes, and Parallel TransportIn the continuum, the gauge field $A_\mu(x)$ takes values in the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ of the gauge group $G$ (e.g., $SU(3)$). On the lattice $\Lambda = a\mathbb{Z}^4$, we work with link variables $U_\mu(n) \in G$, representing the parallel transport along the edge from site $n$ to $n+\hat{\mu}$:$$U_\mu(n) \approx \mathcal{P} \exp \left( i g \int_n^{n+\hat{\mu}} A_\mu(x) dx \right)$$6.2 Gauge Transformations and Action InvarianceA gauge transformation is defined by assigning a group element $\Omega(n) \in G$ to each lattice site. The link variables transform as parallel transporters 21:$$U_\mu(n) \to U'_\mu(n) = \Omega(n) U_\mu(n) \Omega^{-1}(n+\hat{\mu})$$We construct the Wilson Action $S_W$ from the trace of plaquettes (minimal loops $1 \times 1$). The plaquette variable $U_{\mu\nu}(n)$ is the ordered product of links around a square:$$U_{\mu\nu}(n) = U_\mu(n) U_\nu(n+\hat{\mu}) U_\mu^{-1}(n+\hat{\nu}) U_\nu^{-1}(n)$$Proof of Invariance:Under the transformation, intermediate $\Omega$ terms cancel telescopically:$$\begin{aligned}
U'_{\mu\nu} &= [\Omega(n) U_\mu \Omega^{-1}(n+\hat{\mu})][\Omega(n+\hat{\mu}) U_\nu \Omega^{-1}(n+\hat{\mu}+\hat{\nu})] \dots \\
&= \Omega(n) U_{\mu} U_{\nu} U_{\mu}^{-1} U_{\nu}^{-1} \Omega^{-1}(n) \\
&= \Omega(n) U_{\mu\nu}(n) \Omega^{-1}(n)
\end{aligned}$$The trace is cyclic: $\text{Tr}(U'_{\mu\nu}) = \text{Tr}(\Omega U_{\mu\nu} \Omega^{-1}) = \text{Tr}(U_{\mu\nu})$.Thus, the action $S_W = \sum \text{Re} \text{Tr}(1 - U_{\mu\nu})$ is gauge invariant.216.3 Invariance of the Haar MeasureThe path integral is defined as $Z = \int \mathcal{D}U e^{-S_W}$. The measure $\mathcal{D}U = \prod_{n,\mu} dU_\mu(n)$ is the product of Haar measures on the group links.Crucially, we must prove that this measure is invariant under gauge transformations: $\mathcal{D}U' = \mathcal{D}U$.Proof using Jacobians:Consider the transformation of a single link $U' = \Omega_L U \Omega_R^{-1}$, where $\Omega_L = \Omega(n)$ and $\Omega_R = \Omega(n+\hat{\mu})$ are fixed elements for the integration over $U_\mu(n)$.This is a composition of a left translation by $\Omega_L$ and a right translation by $\Omega_R^{-1}$.The Haar measure $dU$ is defined to be both left and right invariant for compact Lie groups (which are unimodular).$$d(\Omega_L U) = dU \quad \text{and} \quad d(U \Omega_R^{-1}) = dU$$Since the gauge transformation is simply a combination of left and right translations on the group manifold at each link, the Jacobian of the transformation is unity:$$\det \left( \frac{\partial U'}{\partial U} \right) = 1$$Therefore, $\mathcal{D}U' = \mathcal{D}U$. This derivation 24 guarantees that the vacuum expectation values $\langle \mathcal{O} \rangle = \frac{1}{Z} \int \mathcal{D}U \mathcal{O} e^{-S}$ are gauge invariant if the observable $\mathcal{O}$ is invariant.Comparison with Continuum: In the continuum, the measure $\mathcal{D}A$ is flat. Gauge transformations $A \to A + D\alpha$ have unit Jacobian formally, but require gauge fixing (Faddeev-Popov) to avoid integrating over the infinite volume of the gauge orbit. On the lattice, the gauge group is compact ($SU(N)$), so the volume of the gauge orbit is finite ($\text{Vol}(G)^V$). We can integrate without gauge fixing (for gauge invariant observables), effectively summing over all gauge copies, which just contributes a finite constant factor canceling in the ratio of expectation values.6.4 The Continuum Limit DerivationWe finally expand the lattice action to recover the Yang-Mills action.Let $U_\mu(n) = e^{i a g A_\mu(n)}$. Using the BCH formula for the plaquette product around the loop 21:$$U_{\mu\nu} = e^{i a g A_\mu(n)} e^{i a g A_\nu(n+\hat{\mu})} e^{-i a g A_\mu(n+\hat{\nu})} e^{-i a g A_\nu(n)}$$Using Taylor expansions $A_\nu(n+\hat{\mu}) \approx A_\nu + a \partial_\mu A_\nu$, the exponent collects to:$$i a^2 g (\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu + i g [A_\mu, A_\nu]) + O(a^3) = i a^2 g F_{\mu\nu}$$The trace part:$$\text{Tr}(1 - e^{i a^2 g F_{\mu\nu}}) \approx \text{Tr}\left( 1 - (1 + i a^2 g F - \frac{1}{2} a^4 g^2 F^2) \right) = \frac{1}{2} a^4 g^2 \text{Tr}(F_{\mu\nu}^2)$$Summing over the lattice $\sum_n \to \int \frac{d^4x}{a^4}$ cancels the $a^4$ factor, yielding the continuum action $\frac{1}{4} \int \text{Tr}(F^2) d^4x$.7.
