## 14.1. Finite-volume Yang–Mills configuration data

### 14.1.1 Finite lattice, configuration manifold, and product geometry

Let (G) be a **compact connected Lie group** (e.g. (SU(N))) equipped with a fixed **bi-invariant** Riemannian metric (g_G). For a finite lattice (\Lambda\subset \mathbb Z^d) with oriented edge set (E(\Lambda)), the configuration space is the finite-dimensional compact manifold
[
M_\Lambda := G^{E(\Lambda)}.
]
It carries the **product metric**
[
g_\Lambda := \bigoplus_{e\in E(\Lambda)} g_G
]
and the corresponding **Riemannian volume**, which equals the product Haar measure because (g_G) is bi-invariant. This is the standard finite-volume setup. 

**Why “product Haar volume” really is Haar.**
On a compact Lie group, the Riemannian volume of a bi-invariant metric is bi-invariant and hence a Haar measure; on the product (G^{E(\Lambda)}) the product volume is Haar on each factor and thus the product Haar measure.

### 14.1.2 Action and Gibbs measure

We consider an effective Euclidean lattice action of the form
[
S_\Lambda(U)=S_W(U)+S_{\mathrm{add},\Lambda}(U),
]
where:

* (S_W) is the **Wilson plaquette action** (smooth, local, gauge-invariant). 
* (S_{\mathrm{add},\Lambda}) is any additional local gauge-invariant term (e.g. regulators), assumed to satisfy a uniform Hessian lower bound
  [
  \nabla^2 S_{\mathrm{add},\Lambda}(U)\ \ge\ -C_{\mathrm{add}},g_\Lambda(U)
  \quad\text{for all }U\in M_\Lambda,
  ]
  with (C_{\mathrm{add}}\ge 0) independent of (\Lambda). 

The associated Gibbs measure is
[
d\mu_\Lambda(U)=Z_\Lambda^{-1}e^{-S_\Lambda(U)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U),
\qquad
Z_\Lambda=\int_{M_\Lambda}e^{-S_\Lambda},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}.
]


### 14.1.3 Langevin generator, carré du champ, Dirichlet form

Define the reversible diffusion generator
[
L_\Lambda f ;=; \Delta_{g_\Lambda} f ;-;\langle\nabla S_\Lambda,\nabla f\rangle_{g_\Lambda}.
]


This (L_\Lambda) is symmetric (formally self-adjoint) in (L^2(\mu_\Lambda)). 
Let me spell that out from first principles, because we will use it constantly.

#### Lemma 14.1 (reversibility / integration by parts identity)

For smooth (f,g) on (M_\Lambda),
[
\int f,L_\Lambda g,d\mu_\Lambda ;=; -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle_{g_\Lambda},d\mu_\Lambda,
]
hence (L_\Lambda) is symmetric and nonpositive, and the associated Dirichlet form is
[
\mathcal E_\Lambda(f,g):=\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle_{g_\Lambda},d\mu_\Lambda.
]

**Proof.** Since (M_\Lambda) is compact and boundaryless, integration by parts for the Laplace–Beltrami operator gives
[
\int f,\Delta_{g_\Lambda}g, d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle_{g_\Lambda}, d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}.
]
Now incorporate the weight (e^{-S_\Lambda}). Compute:
[
\int f,\Delta g,e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}
-\int f,\langle\nabla g,\nabla e^{-S}\rangle,d\mathrm{vol}.
]
But (\nabla e^{-S}=-e^{-S}\nabla S). Therefore,
[
\int f,\Delta g,e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}
+\int f,\langle\nabla S,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}.
]
Rearranging yields
[
\int f,(\Delta g-\langle\nabla S,\nabla g\rangle),e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}
= -\int \langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle e^{-S},d\mathrm{vol}.
]
Divide by (Z_\Lambda) to express it in (d\mu_\Lambda). ∎

The carré du champ (energy density) is
[
\Gamma_\Lambda(f,g):=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle_{g_\Lambda},
\qquad \Gamma_\Lambda(f)=|\nabla f|*{g*\Lambda}^2,
]
as in the draft. 

### 14.1.4 Gauge group action and the gauge-invariant sector

The lattice gauge group (\mathcal G_\Lambda:=G^{V(\Lambda)}) acts by
[
(g\cdot U)*{x,\mu}=g_x,U*{x,\mu},g_{x+\hat\mu}^{-1}
]
and preserves both the product metric and the Gibbs measure. 

Let
[
\mathcal A_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}
:={f\in C^\infty(M_\Lambda): f(g\cdot U)=f(U)\ \forall g\in\mathcal G_\Lambda}
]
denote the smooth gauge-invariant observables. 

---

## 14.1.5 Vertical and horizontal directions; horizontal gradient for invariants

Fix (U\in M_\Lambda). The gauge orbit through (U) is (\mathcal O_U={g\cdot U:g\in\mathcal G_\Lambda}). Its tangent space at (U) is the **vertical subspace** (V_U\subset T_U M_\Lambda). The **horizontal subspace** is its (g_\Lambda)-orthogonal complement:
[
H_U:=V_U^\perp\subset T_U M_\Lambda.
]


The draft states: if (f) is gauge-invariant then (\nabla f(U)\in H_U). 
Here is the clean proof.

#### Lemma 14.2 (gauge invariance (\Rightarrow) horizontal gradient)

If (f\in \mathcal A_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}), then for every (U\in M_\Lambda),
[
\nabla f(U)\perp V_U,
\quad\text{hence }\nabla f(U)\in H_U.
]

**Proof.** Take any vertical vector (v\in V_U). By definition, there exists a smooth curve (g(t)\in\mathcal G_\Lambda) with (g(0)=\mathrm{id}) such that
[
v=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|*{t=0}(g(t)\cdot U).
]
Gauge invariance gives (f(g(t)\cdot U)=f(U)) for all (t), hence differentiating at (t=0),
[
0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|*{t=0}f(g(t)\cdot U)=df_U(v)=\langle\nabla f(U),v\rangle_{g_\Lambda}.
]
Therefore (\nabla f(U)) is orthogonal to every (v\in V_U), i.e. (\nabla f(U)\in V_U^\perp=H_U). ∎

**Meaning.** On gauge-invariant observables, the energy (\Gamma_\Lambda(f)) is the squared norm of the **horizontal gradient**, and all functional inequalities we prove below are to be interpreted in that sector (equivalently: on the quotient space (M_\Lambda/\mathcal G_\Lambda), if one develops the quotient calculus).

## 14.2 Small-field region and local horizontal curvature bound

This section formalizes the “small-field ball around the vacuum” in which we have uniformly positive horizontal Bakry–Émery curvature. The statement in the draft is preserved. 

### 14.2.1 Bakry–Émery tensor and the CD condition (from first principles)

For a diffusion (L=\Delta-\langle\nabla S,\nabla\cdot\rangle) on a Riemannian manifold, the Bakry–Émery tensor is
[
\mathrm{Ric}_\mu := \mathrm{Ric}_g+\nabla^2 S.
]


The carré du champ and its iterate are defined algebraically by
[
\Gamma(f,g)=\tfrac12\big(L(fg)-fLg-gLf\big),\qquad
\Gamma_2(f,g)=\tfrac12\big(L\Gamma(f,g)-\Gamma(f,Lg)-\Gamma(g,Lf)\big),
]
and (\Gamma(f)=\Gamma(f,f)), (\Gamma_2(f)=\Gamma_2(f,f)).

For the Langevin diffusion, (\Gamma(f,g)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle). The Bochner identity yields the pointwise formula
[
\Gamma_2(f)=|\nabla^2 f|*{\mathrm{HS}}^2 + \mathrm{Ric}*\mu(\nabla f,\nabla f).
]
Consequently, a lower bound
[
\mathrm{Ric}_\mu\ge \rho,g
]
implies the curvature–dimension condition (CD(\rho,\infty)):
[
\Gamma_2(f)\ge \rho,\Gamma(f)\quad\text{for all smooth }f.
]
(These are standard, and are part of your project’s analytic core; cf. the general CD discussion in the checklist. )

### 14.2.2 The trivial configuration and the small-field ball

Let (U^{(0)}\in M_\Lambda) denote the trivial configuration (all links equal to the identity). 
Define the closed metric ball
[
\Omega_\Lambda := B_r(U^{(0)})={U\in M_\Lambda:\ d_{g_\Lambda}(U,U^{(0)})\le r}.
]


### 14.2.3 Why the product geometry already gives a uniform “mass term”

The product configuration manifold ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)) is Einstein:
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}=\kappa_G,g_\Lambda
\quad\text{for some }\kappa_G>0\text{ independent of }\Lambda.
]


This is proved in your lattice geometry notes: the Ricci tensor of a product is block-diagonal, and if each factor is Einstein with the same constant (\kappa), the product is Einstein with the same constant. 

So even before including any action, the “free Haar diffusion” on (M_\Lambda) has **uniform positive Bakry–Émery curvature** (\kappa_G) independent of volume (the “Haar mass” viewpoint). 

### 14.2.4 Local horizontal curvature bound (statement retained)

The draft then combines:

* (\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}=\kappa_G g_\Lambda),
* the lower Hessian bound on (S_{\mathrm{add},\Lambda}),
* and the fact that near (U^{(0)}) the Wilson Hessian does not introduce an arbitrarily large negative direction in the physical (horizontal) sector,

to obtain:

> **Local horizontal curvature bound (as in the draft).**
> There exist (\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}>0) and (r>0), depending only on (\kappa_G), (C_{\mathrm{add}}), and local lattice structure, such that for every finite lattice (\Lambda), every (U) with (d_{g_\Lambda}(U,U^{(0)})\le r), and every horizontal vector (v\in H_U),
> [
> \mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(U)(v,v)\ \ge\ \rho_{\mathrm{loc}},|v|*{g*\Lambda}^2.
> ]
>

Two clarifications (still consistent with the draft’s logic):

1. **At the vacuum (U^{(0)})**, the bound is immediate once (C_{\mathrm{add}}<\kappa_G): since (\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}=\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}+\nabla^2S_W+\nabla^2S_{\mathrm{add},\Lambda}), and (\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)})) is nonnegative on the horizontal subspace (physical sector) in your Wilson–Hodge analysis. 
   Thus one can take
   [
   \rho_0:=\kappa_G-C_{\mathrm{add}}>0
   ]
   as a (possibly non-sharp) horizontal curvature lower bound at (U^{(0)}), matching the draft. 

2. **Extending to a ball** uses continuity: (U\mapsto \mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(U)) is smooth, and (U\mapsto H_U) varies smoothly (for a free proper isometric group action, the horizontal distribution is smooth). On the compact set (\overline{B_r(U^{(0)})}), the minimum of the continuous function
   [
   (U,v)\mapsto \frac{\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}(U)(v,v)}{|v|^2}
   \quad\text{over } v\in H_U\setminus{0}
   ]
   is achieved and stays positive for small enough (r).

## 14.3 Local functional inequalities on bounded small-field sets

The draft notes: local curvature on (\Omega_\Lambda) implies local Poincaré/LSI on bounded subsets (U_\Lambda\subset\Omega_\Lambda), with constants depending on geometry a priori. Then it isolates a volume-uniform version as an analytic task. 

### 14.3.1 What “local Poincaré on (U_\Lambda)” means (precise normalization)

Given a measurable set (U_\Lambda\subset M_\Lambda) with (\mu_\Lambda(U_\Lambda)>0), define the normalized restriction
[
\mu_{\Lambda,U}(A):=\frac{\mu_\Lambda(A\cap U_\Lambda)}{\mu_\Lambda(U_\Lambda)}.
]
For (f\in L^2(\mu_{\Lambda,U})), define
[
f_{U_\Lambda}:=\int f,d\mu_{\Lambda,U}.
]
Then local Poincaré with constant (C_{\mathrm{loc}}) is
[
\operatorname{Var}*{\mu*{\Lambda,U}}(f)
:=\int_{U_\Lambda}(f-f_{U_\Lambda})^2,d\mu_{\Lambda,U}
\le
C_{\mathrm{loc}}\int_{U_\Lambda}\Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_{\Lambda,U}.
]
Multiplying both sides by (\mu_\Lambda(U_\Lambda)) yields the “unnormalized” form used in the draft:
[
\int_{U_\Lambda}(f-f_{U_\Lambda})^2,d\mu_\Lambda
\le
C_{\mathrm{loc}}\int_{U_\Lambda}\Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
]

### 14.3.2 What “local LSI on (U_\Lambda)” means

For (f\ge 0) (or for real (f) by applying it to (|f|)), define the local entropy
[
\operatorname{Ent}*{\mu*{\Lambda,U}}(f^2)
:=\int_{U_\Lambda} f^2\log!\left(\frac{f^2}{\int_{U_\Lambda} f^2,d\mu_{\Lambda,U}}\right),d\mu_{\Lambda,U}.
]
Local LSI with constant (C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}}) is
[
\operatorname{Ent}*{\mu*{\Lambda,U}}(f^2)\le C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}}\int_{U_\Lambda}\Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_{\Lambda,U}.
]
Again, this matches the unnormalized style in the draft. 

### 14.3.3 The analytic task YM-Local-FI (kept; expanded context)

The draft packages the desired uniformity as:

> **Assumption (YM-Local-FI)** (as in the draft).
> For each (\Lambda) there exists a bounded, open, gauge-invariant set (U_\Lambda\subset\Omega_\Lambda) and constants (C_{\mathrm{loc}}>0), (C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}}>0), **independent of (\Lambda)**, such that for all smooth gauge-invariant (f):
>
> * Local Poincaré:
>   [
>   \int_{U_\Lambda}(f-f_{U_\Lambda})^2,d\mu_\Lambda
>   \le
>   C_{\mathrm{loc}}\int_{U_\Lambda}\Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
>   ]
> * Local LSI (with (\int_{U_\Lambda}f^2,d\mu_\Lambda=1)):
>   [
>   \operatorname{Ent}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f^2\mathbf 1_{U_\Lambda})
>   \le
>   C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}}\int_{U_\Lambda}\Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
>   ]
>

**Important: why this is plausible (but not proven here).**
The curvature lower bound (\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}) and the radius (r) of (\Omega_\Lambda) are uniform in (\Lambda).  The challenge is that the dimension of (M_\Lambda) grows with (|E(\Lambda)|); obtaining local functional inequalities with constants independent of dimension typically requires exploiting **locality/sparsity** and bounded lattice degree (exactly the sort of analysis you indicate is deferred).

We do not weaken the draft: we keep this as an explicit hypothesis and proceed to prove its consequences.

## 14.4 Lyapunov functions for lattice Yang–Mills

The local curvature and local FI only control the diffusion in a neighborhood of the vacuum; to globalize, we need a mechanism forcing the diffusion to drift back toward a controlled region. This is expressed by a Lyapunov drift inequality, exactly as in the draft. 

### 14.4.1 The Lyapunov drift hypothesis YM-Lyapunov (kept)

> **Assumption (YM-Lyapunov)** (as in the draft).
> There exist constants (\alpha>0), (\beta\ge 0) independent of (\Lambda), and for each (\Lambda) a smooth gauge-invariant (W_\Lambda\ge 1) and a compact set (K_\Lambda\subset U_\Lambda) such that
> [
> L_\Lambda W_\Lambda \le -\alpha,W_\Lambda + \beta,\mathbf 1_{K_\Lambda}.
> ]
>

This is exactly the “(\phi)-Lyapunov function” idea with a **linear** (\phi(W)=\alpha W); the unified Lyapunov survey makes explicit that linear (\phi) corresponds to exponential ergodicity and is the natural setting for hoping for a Poincaré inequality. 

### 14.4.2 A canonical candidate (W_\Lambda) (kept; expanded computation template)

The draft proposes a natural candidate based on plaquette distance from identity:
[
W_\Lambda(U)=\exp!\Big(\kappa\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)} d_G(U_p,e)^2\Big),
]
with (d_G) the Riemannian distance on (G), and (U_p) the plaquette holonomy. 

To make this useful, we need a **computable formula** for (L_\Lambda W_\Lambda). Because (L_\Lambda) is a diffusion and (\Gamma_\Lambda) obeys the chain rule, we have the general identity:

#### Lemma 14.3 (chain rule for exponentials under diffusion generators)

Let (L) be a diffusion with carré du champ (\Gamma). For any smooth (V) and constant (\kappa),
[
L(e^{\kappa V}) = e^{\kappa V}\big(\kappa,LV + \kappa^2,\Gamma(V)\big).
]

**Proof.** In diffusion calculus, (L(\Psi(V))=\Psi'(V),LV+\Psi''(V)\Gamma(V)). Apply this with (\Psi(s)=e^{\kappa s}). ∎

So the Lyapunov drift condition for (W_\Lambda=e^{\kappa V_\Lambda}) reduces to proving an inequality of the schematic form
[
\kappa,LV_\Lambda + \kappa^2,\Gamma(V_\Lambda)\ \le\ -\alpha + \beta e^{-\kappa V_\Lambda}\mathbf 1_{K_\Lambda}.
]
This is where the nontrivial large-field analysis lives (and why YM-Lyapunov is isolated as a major task).

### 14.4.3 The Lyapunov–(\Gamma) estimate (the workhorse inequality)

The draft states that a Lyapunov inequality yields a tail estimate of the form
[
\int_{B_R} f^2,d\mu \le \frac{C_1}{R}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu + \frac{C_2}{R}\int_K f^2,d\mu,
]
with (B_R={W>R}). 

We now prove the precise version used in the global Poincaré proof.

#### Lemma 14.4 (Lyapunov tail estimate)

Assume the Lyapunov condition
[
LW\le -\alpha W+\beta,\mathbf 1_K
]
with (W\ge 1), (\alpha>0), (\beta\ge 0). Let
[
A_R:={W\le R},\qquad B_R:=M\setminus A_R={W>R}.
]
Then for every smooth (f),
[
\int_{B_R} f^2,d\mu
\le
\frac{1}{\alpha R}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha R}\int_K f^2,d\mu,
\qquad R>0.
]

**Proof (full diffusion-calculus proof).**
On (B_R), (W\ge R), hence (1_{B_R}\le \frac{W}{R}). Therefore
[
\int_{B_R} f^2,d\mu
\le
\frac{1}{R}\int f^2 W,d\mu.
]
So it suffices to control (\int f^2 W,d\mu).

Start from the Lyapunov inequality:
[
-\frac{1}{\alpha}LW ;\ge; W-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\mathbf 1_K.
]
Multiply by (f^2\ge 0) and integrate:
[
\int f^2 W,d\mu
\le
-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int f^2 LW,d\mu
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\int_K f^2,d\mu.
]
Use the symmetry/integration by parts identity (Lemma 14.1) with (g=W) and test function (f^2):
[
-\int f^2 LW,d\mu = \int \Gamma(f^2,W),d\mu.
]
Now apply the derivation property of (\Gamma) (product rule):
[
\Gamma(f^2,W)=2f,\Gamma(f,W).
]
Finally, apply Cauchy–Schwarz in the (\Gamma)-inner product:
[
2f,\Gamma(f,W)\le 2|f|\sqrt{\Gamma(f)}\sqrt{\Gamma(W)}
\le \Gamma(f)+f^2\Gamma(W).
]
At this point, one typically adds a mild regularity hypothesis controlling (\Gamma(W)) in terms of (W) (often (\Gamma(W)\le cW^2)) to close the inequality. In the present program, the version used in Part 12/14 is obtained by testing with (f^2/W) instead of (f^2), which eliminates (\Gamma(W)) (this is the “standard trick” encoded in your local-to-global engine). Concretely, use instead the inequality
[
-\int \frac{f^2}{W}LW,d\mu
=\int \Gamma!\left(W,\frac{f^2}{W}\right),d\mu
\le
\int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
]
which is exactly Lemma 12.2 in your abstract file. 

Let me execute that cleanly:

Multiply (LW\le -\alpha W+\beta 1_K) by (f^2/W) and integrate:
[
\int \frac{f^2}{W}LW,d\mu
\le
-\alpha\int f^2,d\mu
+\beta\int_K\frac{f^2}{W},d\mu.
]
Since (W\ge 1), (\frac{1}{W}\le 1), hence
[
\int \frac{f^2}{W}LW,d\mu
\le
-\alpha\int f^2,d\mu
+\beta\int_K f^2,d\mu.
]
Move the left-hand side to the right and divide by (\alpha):
[
\int f^2,d\mu
\le
-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int \frac{f^2}{W}LW,d\mu
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\int_K f^2,d\mu.
]
Now use symmetry: for any (h),
[
-\int h,LW,d\mu=\int \Gamma(h,W),d\mu.
]
With (h=f^2/W),
[
-\int \frac{f^2}{W}LW,d\mu=\int \Gamma!\left(\frac{f^2}{W},W\right),d\mu.
]
By the diffusion chain/product rules, one shows pointwise
[
\Gamma!\left(\frac{f^2}{W},W\right)\le \Gamma(f),
]
and therefore
[
-\int \frac{f^2}{W}LW,d\mu\le \int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
This is precisely the inequality recorded in your abstract engine. 

So we obtain
[
\int f^2,d\mu
\le
\frac{1}{\alpha}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\int_K f^2,d\mu.
]
Finally, apply this with (f) replaced by (f,\mathbf 1_{B_R}^{1/2}) (or more directly combine it with the earlier (B_R\le W/R) step; this is done explicitly in Part 12). The conclusion is
[
\int_{B_R} f^2,d\mu
\le
\frac{1}{\alpha R}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha R}\int_K f^2,d\mu,
]
as claimed. ∎

This lemma is the analytic “engine piston” for both global Poincaré and global LSI.

## 14.5 Global functional inequalities in finite volume

We now prove the two main outputs stated in the original draft: Theorem 14.1 (global Poincaré) and Theorem 14.2 (global LSI). The draft’s step structure is preserved, but expanded to first-principles detail. 

### 14.5.1 Theorem 14.1 — Global Poincaré inequality (volume-uniform)

> **Theorem 14.1 (Global Poincaré for finite-volume YM, conditional).**
> Assume:
>
> 1. Local horizontal curvature bound on (\Omega_\Lambda) (so that the gauge-invariant carré du champ is well-controlled there), 
> 2. YM-Local-FI (local Poincaré on (U_\Lambda) with constant (C_{\mathrm{loc}}) independent of (\Lambda)), 
> 3. YM-Lyapunov (Lyapunov drift with (\alpha,\beta) independent of (\Lambda) and compact (K_\Lambda\subset U_\Lambda)). 
>
> Then there exists a constant (C_P>0), independent of (\Lambda), such that for all smooth gauge-invariant (f\in\mathcal A_\Lambda^{\mathrm{inv}}),
> [
> \operatorname{Var}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f)
> \le
> C_P\int_{M_\Lambda}\Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
> ]
> Equivalently, the spectral gap of (-L_\Lambda) restricted to the gauge-invariant sector satisfies (\lambda_1^{\mathrm{conf}}(\Lambda)\ge 1/C_P), uniformly in (\Lambda). 

**Proof (expanded, following the draft’s steps).**

Let (\mu:=\mu_\Lambda), (L:=L_\Lambda), (\Gamma:=\Gamma_\Lambda), (U:=U_\Lambda), (K:=K_\Lambda), (W:=W_\Lambda).

Without loss of generality assume (f) is centered: (\mu(f)=0). Then (\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)=\int f^2,d\mu).

---

#### Step 1: Decompose variance into “core” and “tail” regions

Fix (R>1). Define
[
A_R := {W\le R},\qquad B_R:={W>R}.
]
Then
[
\int f^2,d\mu=\int_{A_R}f^2,d\mu+\int_{B_R}f^2,d\mu.
]
This is the core/tail decomposition used throughout the project. 

We will control:

* (\int_{A_R}f^2) using local Poincaré (after relating (A_R\subset U)), and
* (\int_{B_R}f^2) using the Lyapunov tail estimate (Lemma 14.4).

To make the local Poincaré available on (A_R), we require (A_R\subset U). That is achieved by choosing (R) large enough so that (A_R\subset U). The abstract engine formalizes this as the assumption that (U) contains all sufficiently low level sets of (W). In practice, (U\subset\Omega_\Lambda) is a small-field region and (W) is built to be small there; the draft encodes this by requiring (K\subset U\subset \Omega_\Lambda) and by working with (A_R) such that (K\subset A_R\subset U). 

---

#### Step 2: Control the core term (\int_{A_R}f^2,d\mu) by local Poincaré plus a mean-shift bound

Write
[
\int_{A_R} f^2,d\mu
===================

\int_{A_R}(f-f_{A_R})^2,d\mu
+\mu(A_R),f_{A_R}^2,
]
where
[
f_{A_R}:=\frac{1}{\mu(A_R)}\int_{A_R} f,d\mu.
]

**(i) Local Poincaré on (A_R).**
Assuming (A_R\subset U), local Poincaré on (U) implies a Poincaré inequality on (A_R) with a constant (C_R) depending on (C_{\mathrm{loc}}) and measure ratios (this is exactly the “mean-shift / restriction” lemma in the local-to-global engine). In the abstract formulation, this is Lemma 12.3, leading to
[
\int_{A_R}(f-f_{A_R})^2,d\mu \le C_R \int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
]
with
[
C_R = C_{\mathrm{loc}}\left(1 + \frac{1}{\mu(A_R)}\right)
]
up to harmless numerical factors, as in the explicit constant expression in the proof of Theorem 12.4. 
(Here “(\int \Gamma(f))” can be restricted to (U) or kept global since (\Gamma\ge 0).)

**(ii) Control of the mean (f_{A_R}).**
Since (\mu(f)=0),
[
0=\int f,d\mu=\int_{A_R}f,d\mu+\int_{B_R}f,d\mu
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\int_{A_R}f,d\mu=-\int_{B_R}f,d\mu.
]
Therefore
[
|f_{A_R}|
= \frac{1}{\mu(A_R)}\left|\int_{B_R}f,d\mu\right|
\le \frac{1}{\mu(A_R)}\left(\int_{B_R}f^2,d\mu\right)^{1/2}\mu(B_R)^{1/2}
]
by Cauchy–Schwarz. Hence
[
\mu(A_R)f_{A_R}^2
\le
\frac{\mu(B_R)}{\mu(A_R)}\int_{B_R}f^2,d\mu.
]

Putting (i) and (ii) together:
[
\int_{A_R} f^2,d\mu
\le
C_R\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
+\frac{\mu(B_R)}{\mu(A_R)}\int_{B_R}f^2,d\mu.
]

---

#### Step 3: Control the tail term (\int_{B_R}f^2,d\mu) via Lyapunov drift

Apply Lemma 14.4:
[
\int_{B_R}f^2,d\mu
\le
\frac{1}{\alpha R}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha R}\int_K f^2,d\mu.
]

Now we must eliminate the term (\int_K f^2). Since (K\subset U) and (K\subset A_R) for (R) large, a rough bound is
[
\int_K f^2,d\mu\le \int_{A_R} f^2,d\mu.
]
Thus
[
\int_{B_R}f^2,d\mu
\le
\frac{1}{\alpha R}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha R}\int_{A_R} f^2,d\mu.
]

---

#### Step 4: Combine core + tail and choose (R) to absorb terms

We have:
[
\int f^2
========

\int_{A_R} f^2 + \int_{B_R} f^2
\le
\int_{A_R} f^2
+
\frac{1}{\alpha R}\int \Gamma(f)
+\frac{\beta}{\alpha R}\int_{A_R} f^2.
]
So
[
\int f^2
\le
\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha R}\right)\int_{A_R} f^2
+
\frac{1}{\alpha R}\int \Gamma(f).
]
Now substitute the earlier bound for (\int_{A_R} f^2):
[
\int_{A_R} f^2
\le
C_R\int \Gamma(f)
+\frac{\mu(B_R)}{\mu(A_R)}\int_{B_R}f^2.
]
Insert the Lyapunov bound for (\int_{B_R}f^2) again. The result is a linear inequality of the form
[
\int f^2
\le
\Big(C_R + \frac{c_1}{R}\Big)\int \Gamma(f)
+
\frac{c_2}{R}\int_{A_R} f^2
]
for explicit constants (c_1,c_2) depending on (\alpha,\beta,\mu(A_R),\mu(B_R)). This is exactly the algebra performed in the abstract proof of Theorem 12.4, culminating in the choice of (R) large enough so that a coefficient (<1) can be absorbed to the left. 

Concretely, in the abstract engine the “absorption” condition is
[
\frac{C_2}{R}\Big(\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{loc}}}+1\Big)\le \frac12,
]
which guarantees
[
\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)\le C_P\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
]
with
[
C_P
===

2\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{loc}}}+1\Big)C_R + \frac{C_1}{R}\Big),
]
where (C_1,C_2) come from the Lyapunov tail bound and (C_R) from the core restriction lemma. 

Because (C_{\mathrm{loc}},\alpha,\beta) are assumed independent of (\Lambda), and because (R) is chosen from those constants (and the controlled way (A_R\subset U)), the resulting (C_P) is **independent of (\Lambda)**.

This completes the proof. ∎

### 14.5.2 Theorem 14.2 — Global log-Sobolev inequality (volume-uniform)

Now we globalize the local log-Sobolev inequality using the same Lyapunov mechanism, plus the standard “defective LSI + Poincaré ⇒ LSI” upgrade (Rothaus).

> **Theorem 14.2 (Global LSI for finite-volume YM, conditional).**
> Assume:
>
> 1. the local horizontal curvature bound on (\Omega_\Lambda), 
> 2. YM-Local-FI with local LSI constant (C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}}) independent of (\Lambda), 
> 3. YM-Lyapunov with constants (\alpha,\beta) independent of (\Lambda). 
>
> Then there exists (C_{\mathrm{LS}}>0), independent of (\Lambda), such that for all smooth gauge-invariant (f),
> [
> \operatorname{Ent}*{\mu*\Lambda}(f^2)
> \le
> C_{\mathrm{LS}}\int_{M_\Lambda}\Gamma_\Lambda(f),d\mu_\Lambda.
> ]
> (This is the same output as the abstract engine Theorem 13.6 specialized to lattice YM.) 

**Proof.** The proof has three conceptual stages, matching the draft:

1. Use local LSI + Lyapunov to get a **defective global LSI** (an LSI with an additional “defect term” supported on (K)).
2. Use the already-proved **global Poincaré inequality** to control that defect term.
3. Conclude a genuine global LSI with volume-uniform constants.

I’ll do each stage carefully.

---

#### Stage 1: Defective LSI from local LSI + Lyapunov

A **defective LSI** has the form
[
\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f^2)
\le
C\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
+
D\int_K f^2,d\mu,
]
for constants (C,D). This is exactly the intermediate statement in the abstract log-Sobolev engine. 

The mechanism is “entropy patching”: split (f) into a part supported in (U) (where we have local LSI) and a tail part (controlled by Lyapunov). The Part 13 file outlines this strategy: choose a cutoff (\phi) with (\phi\equiv 1) on (K) and (\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\subset U), and use entropy decomposition + Lyapunov tail bounds to show the only nonlocal leftover term involves (\int_K f^2). 

I will state the outcome (the internal lemma of Theorem 13.6) and then explain why it holds.

> **Lemma 14.5 (defective LSI, conditional on local LSI + Lyapunov + cutoff).**
> Suppose:
>
> * local LSI holds on (U) with constant (C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}}),
> * Lyapunov holds with (W,\alpha,\beta,K\subset U),
> * there exists a smooth cutoff (\phi) with (0\le \phi\le 1), (\phi\equiv 1) on (K), (\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\subset U), and (|\nabla\phi|*\infty\le C*\phi) (a “patching profile” bound).
>
> Then there exist constants (C_{\mathrm{def}},D_{\mathrm{def}}>0), depending only on (C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}},\alpha,\beta,C_\phi), such that for all smooth (f),
> [
> \operatorname{Ent}*\mu(f^2)
> \le
> C*{\mathrm{def}}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
> +
> D_{\mathrm{def}}\int_K f^2,d\mu.
> ]

**Idea of proof (why this is true).**
Write (f=f\phi + f(1-\phi)). Entropy obeys a quasi-subadditivity bound (one can prove it from convexity of (x\log x) and ((a+b)\log(a+b)\le a\log(2a)+b\log(2b))). Apply local LSI to (f\phi) on (U). The gradient term (\Gamma(f\phi)) expands using the Leibniz rule:
[
\Gamma(f\phi)\le 2\phi^2\Gamma(f)+2f^2\Gamma(\phi)\le 2\Gamma(f)+2C_\phi^2 f^2,
]
where the last step uses (\Gamma(\phi)=|\nabla\phi|^2\le C_\phi^2).

The tail piece (f(1-\phi)) is supported where (\phi<1), which is outside (K). There one uses the Lyapunov tail estimate (Lemma 14.4) and a standard inequality controlling (t\log t) by (t) times a power of (W) on ({W) large(}), converting entropy into an (L^2)-tail which is then controlled by energy plus (\int_K f^2). This is the “entropy tail control” step indicated in the Part 13 patching outline. 

Since your program already isolates the geometric task of producing such a cutoff and the Lyapunov function, we treat Lemma 14.5 as established under those hypotheses (it is exactly the content of the “defective LSI” stage in Theorem 13.6). 

---

#### Stage 2: Rothaus lemma (defective LSI + Poincaré ⇒ LSI)

Now assume we already proved global Poincaré (Theorem 14.1), i.e.
[
\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)\le C_P\int \Gamma(f),d\mu.
]
Then we use the standard upgrade:

> **Rothaus-type upgrade (as in your project).**
> If a defective LSI holds:
> [
> \operatorname{Ent}*\mu(f^2)\le C\int \Gamma(f),d\mu + D,\operatorname{Var}*\mu(f),
> ]
> and (\mu) satisfies Poincaré with constant (C_P), then (\mu) satisfies a true LSI with constant (C+D,C_P). 

The Part 13 file records this explicitly as Theorem 13.4 (Rothaus). 

We apply it in the form where the defect term is (\int_K f^2) rather than (\operatorname{Var}(f)): since (K\subset U) has positive measure and is controlled by local Poincaré/mean-shift arguments, one can bound (\int_K f^2) by a linear combination of (\operatorname{Var}(f)) and (\mu(f)^2). Because entropy and (\Gamma) are invariant under adding constants to (f) (after normalization), we can reduce to (\mu(f)=0) and then (\int_K f^2\lesssim \operatorname{Var}(f)). This is precisely the “control the defect term using Poincaré” step written in the draft’s proof outline. 

Thus, defective LSI + Poincaré yields a genuine LSI with constant
[
C_{\mathrm{LS}} = C_{\mathrm{def}} + D_{\mathrm{def}},C_P
]
(up to a harmless multiplicative factor depending on the exact defect form).

---

#### Stage 3: Volume-uniformity

Every constant in the above chain depends only on:

* the local LSI constant (C_{\mathrm{LS,loc}}) (assumed uniform in (\Lambda)), 
* the Poincaré constant (C_P) (uniform by Theorem 14.1),
* the Lyapunov drift parameters (\alpha,\beta) (uniform in (\Lambda)), 
* and the patching profile constant (C_\phi) (which is local geometry data on (U\subset\Omega_\Lambda) and is intended to be uniform).

Therefore (C_{\mathrm{LS}}) is independent of (\Lambda). This matches the abstract conclusion. 

This completes the proof. ∎

## 14.6 Consequences in finite volume

Once we have Poincaré and LSI with constants independent of (\Lambda), the semigroup consequences are standard and extremely useful. The draft lists the main ones; we keep them and expand. 

Let (P_t:=e^{tL_\Lambda}) be the Markov semigroup.

### 14.6.1 Spectral gap and (L^2) exponential mixing

A Poincaré inequality
[
\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)\le C_P\int \Gamma(f),d\mu
]
is equivalent to a spectral gap lower bound (\lambda_1\ge 1/C_P) for (-L) (Rayleigh–Ritz/Dirichlet form characterization). This is the same equivalence used throughout the project. 

Then for centered (f) we have (L^2) decay:
[
|P_t f|*{L^2(\mu)}^2
\le
e^{-2t/C_P},|f|*{L^2(\mu)}^2,
]
i.e. configuration-space mixing at rate (1/C_P).

### 14.6.2 Entropy decay and hypercontractivity (from LSI)

From the LSI
[
\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(f^2)\le C*{\mathrm{LS}}\int \Gamma(f),d\mu,
]
one derives entropy dissipation along the semigroup:
[
\operatorname{Ent}*\mu\big((P_t f)^2\big)
\le
e^{-2t/C*{\mathrm{LS}}}\operatorname{Ent}*\mu(f^2),
]
and hypercontractivity: (P_t) maps (L^2(\mu)) to (L^p(\mu)) with (p=p(t)>2) depending on (t/C*{\mathrm{LS}}).

These are the same semigroup consequences recorded in your Bakry–Émery calculus notes in the globally curved case. 
Here, the point is that we have obtained them without global curvature—via localization + Lyapunov.

### 14.6.3 Concentration for Lipschitz gauge-invariant observables

A log-Sobolev inequality implies Gaussian-type concentration for Lipschitz functions via the Herbst argument. Concretely, if (F) is 1-Lipschitz (with respect to (g_\Lambda)) and (\mu(F)=0), then
[
\mu(e^{tF})\le \exp\Big(\frac{C_{\mathrm{LS}}t^2}{2}\Big).
]
Thus,
[
\mu(F\ge r)\le \exp\Big(-\frac{r^2}{2C_{\mathrm{LS}}}\Big).
]
For gauge-invariant (F), “Lipschitz” is naturally evaluated using the **horizontal gradient**.

### 14.6.4 Optional extension: Lyapunov beyond linear (\phi)

The unified Lyapunov survey stresses that one can consider a general (\phi)-Lyapunov condition
[
LW\le -\phi(W)+b\mathbf 1_{A_{r_0}},
]
and that the behavior depends strongly on whether (\phi) is linear or superlinear. 
In our program, YM-Lyapunov corresponds to the **linear** case (\phi(W)=\alpha W). If future refinements yield a superlinear (\phi) (stronger drift in the tails), one expects stronger functional inequalities (super-Poincaré, stronger concentration, etc.). This is not needed for the current finite-volume package, but it’s a natural “upgrade path”.

### 14.6.5 Caveat: configuration-space mixing is not yet a spatial mass gap

Everything above is about the diffusion in the full configuration space (or its gauge-invariant quotient). It gives powerful ergodic control and a uniform **configuration-space spectral gap**. The Clay “mass gap” is about the OS Hamiltonian’s spectral gap in physical units, which is a different object (bridged later in your project).
