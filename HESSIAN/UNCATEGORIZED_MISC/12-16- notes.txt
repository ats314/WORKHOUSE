11.3.1 Setup: the infinite-volume Euclidean state, reflection, and time translations

Fix a>0 (lattice spacing) and a Euclidean time direction (the 0-th coordinate). Let \Omega denote the infinite-volume configuration space and \mu an infinite-volume Euclidean Gibbs measure at fixed a. The relevant symmetries are:
	•	Time translations \tau_n by n\hat e_0 acting on observables by pullback, (\tau_n F)(U)=F(\tau_{-n}U).  
	•	Time reflection \Theta across a hyperplane between two time slices, inducing the antilinear involution on observables
(\theta F)(U):=\overline{F(\Theta U)}.

	•	The positive-time algebra \mathcal A_+: bounded cylinder functions depending only on finitely many links in the nonnegative-time half-space.  

We assume the standard OS hypotheses on \mu: time-translation invariance (H1), reflection invariance (H2), and reflection positivity (H3).  

In our chain, these properties persist to the infinite-volume limit at fixed a (cf. Part 11.1–11.2), and §11.2 supplies time-direction exponential decay of covariances with exponent \eta(a) (in lattice steps).

11.3.2 External input: lattice OS reconstruction and the transfer matrix identity

We now invoke OS reconstruction in the form that produces (i) an OS Hilbert space \mathcal H_{\mathrm{OS}}, (ii) a vacuum vector \Omega, (iii) a positive self-adjoint contraction T implementing one time-step, and (iv) a self-adjoint Hamiltonian H\ge 0 with T=e^{-aH}.    

Concretely, OS starts from the sesquilinear form on \mathcal A_+,
\langle F,G\rangle_{\mathrm{OS}}:=\mu\big((\theta F),\,G\big),
quotients by the null space and completes to get \mathcal H_{\mathrm{OS}}, with \Omega=[1] cyclic.

This is the single identity we will actually use from the reconstruction theorem.

11.3.3 A spectral-measure gap criterion from decay at discrete times

The second ingredient is a basic “Laplace-transform support” lemma: decay of the function n\mapsto \langle\psi,e^{-naH}\psi\rangle forces the spectral measure of H associated to \psi to have no mass below the corresponding rate.

Let H\ge 0 be self-adjoint on a Hilbert space \mathcal H and let \nu_\psi be the spectral measure of H associated to \psi, characterized by
\langle \psi, e^{-tH}\psi\rangle
=
\int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-t\lambda}\,d\nu_\psi(\lambda)
\qquad (t\ge 0).


Lemma 11.1 (spectral-measure gap from discrete-time decay).
Fix a>0 and m>0. If there exists C_\psi<\infty such that for all integers n\ge 0,
\langle \psi, e^{-naH}\psi\rangle \le C_\psi\,e^{-mna},
\tag{11.2}
then \nu_\psi([0,m))=0, equivalently E_H([0,m))\psi=0.  

Proof. Suppose \nu_\psi([0,m))>0. Then there exists \varepsilon\in(0,m) with \delta:=\nu_\psi([0,m-\varepsilon])>0.  
For each n\ge 0,
\langle \psi,e^{-naH}\psi\rangle
=
\int e^{-na\lambda}\,d\nu_\psi(\lambda)

\int_{[0,m-\varepsilon]} e^{-na\lambda}\,d\nu_\psi(\lambda)
\ \ge\
\delta\,e^{-(m-\varepsilon)na}.
But \delta e^{-(m-\varepsilon)na} eventually dominates C_\psi e^{-mna} as n\to\infty (since e^{\varepsilon na}\to\infty), contradicting (11.2). Hence \nu_\psi([0,m))=0. \square

Throughout, we are in the fixed–cutoff infinite-volume OS setting of §11.3: the OS reconstruction provides a Hilbert space \mathcal H_{\mathrm{OS}}, vacuum \Omega, a positive self-adjoint contraction T implementing one Euclidean time step, and a Hamiltonian H\ge 0 such that

\tag{11.11}
with lattice spacing a>0. The orthogonal complement \mathcal H_{\mathrm{OS}}\ominus \mathbb C\Omega is the “excited sector.”

11.4.1 The OS one-step Dirichlet form and a discrete-time Poincaré inequality
Define the one-step OS dissipation (Dirichlet) form
\mathcal E_{\mathrm{OS}}(\psi)
\;:=\;
\big\langle \psi,\,(I-T)\psi\big\rangle_{\mathrm{OS}},
\qquad \psi\in\mathcal H_{\mathrm{OS}}.
\tag{11.12}
Since 0\le T\le I, we have \mathcal E_{\mathrm{OS}}(\psi)\ge 0, with equality on \ker(I-T)\supseteq \mathbb C\Omega.

Also define the OS variance (projection away from the vacuum)
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{OS}}(\psi)
\;:=\;
\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{OS}}^2-|\langle \psi,\Omega\rangle_{\mathrm{OS}}|^2
\;=\;
\big\|\psi-\langle \psi,\Omega\rangle_{\mathrm{OS}}\,\Omega\big\|_{\mathrm{OS}}^2.
\tag{11.13}

The next lemma is the discrete-time analog of “Poincaré \Leftrightarrow spectral gap.”

Lemma 11.3 (one-step dissipation gap on \Omega^\perp).
Let T be a positive self-adjoint contraction on a Hilbert space \mathcal H with a fixed unit vector \Omega satisfying T\Omega=\Omega. Define
\delta_T
\;:=\;
\inf_{\substack{\psi\perp \Omega\\ \psi\neq 0}}
\frac{\langle \psi,(I-T)\psi\rangle}{\|\psi\|^2}.
\tag{11.14}
Then \delta_T = 1-\|T\|_{\Omega^\perp}, and the following are equivalent:
	1.	\delta_T>0.
	2.	The discrete-time Poincaré inequality
\mathrm{Var}(\psi)\ \le\ \frac{1}{\delta_T}\,\langle \psi,(I-T)\psi\rangle
\qquad \forall \psi\in\mathcal H
\tag{11.15}
holds (with \mathrm{Var}(\psi)=\|\psi-\langle\psi,\Omega\rangle\Omega\|^2).
	3.	The spectrum of T on \Omega^\perp is contained in [0,1-\delta_T].

Proof. Restrict to \Omega^\perp. On \Omega^\perp, T is self-adjoint with spectrum in [0,1]. Let \lambda_\ast:=\sup\sigma(T|_{\Omega^\perp})=\|T\|_{\Omega^\perp}. Then for \psi\in\Omega^\perp,
\langle \psi,(I-T)\psi\rangle
=
\int_{[0,1]} (1-\lambda)\,d\nu_\psi(\lambda)
\;\ge\;
(1-\lambda_\ast)\,\|\psi\|^2.
Taking the infimum over \psi\neq 0 gives \delta_T\ge 1-\lambda_\ast. Conversely, approximate \lambda_\ast by spectral vectors to get equality. The equivalence with (11.15) is just rewriting \psi\mapsto \psi-\langle\psi,\Omega\rangle\Omega and the fact that I-T annihilates \Omega. \square

So: a uniform lower bound on the quadratic form of I-T on \Omega^\perp is exactly a one-step spectral gap for the transfer matrix.

⸻

11.4.2 From a one-step transfer gap to a Hamiltonian mass gap
Because T=e^{-aH}, a gap for T is equivalent to a gap for H via the spectral map \lambda=e^{-aE}.

Proposition 11.4 (one-step dissipation \Rightarrow Hamiltonian gap).
Assume T=e^{-aH} with H\ge 0, a>0, and T\Omega=\Omega. Let \delta_T be as in (11.14). Then on \Omega^\perp,
\inf \sigma(H|_{\Omega^\perp})
\ \ge\
\frac{1}{a}\,\big(-\log(1-\delta_T)\big)
\ \ge\
\frac{\delta_T}{a}.
\tag{11.16}
In particular, if \ker(H)=\mathbb C\Omega, then \mathrm{gap}(H)\ge \frac{1}{a}(-\log(1-\delta_T)).

Proof. On \Omega^\perp, \sigma(T)\subset[0,1-\delta_T] by Lemma 11.3, so
\sigma(H|_{\Omega^\perp})
=
-\frac{1}{a}\log \sigma(T|_{\Omega^\perp})
\ \subset\
\left[\frac{1}{a}\big(-\log(1-\delta_T)\big),\ \infty\right).
Finally, -\log(1-x)\ge x for x\in(0,1), yielding the second inequality in (11.16). \square

This is the key “alternative bridge” statement: if you can prove a uniform lower bound on (I-T) in the OS Hilbert space, you get a mass gap with the correct 1/a scaling.

11.4.3 Euclidean representation of the one-step dissipation

\langle [F],\,T[F]\rangle_{\mathrm{OS}}

\mathcal E_{\mathrm{OS}}([F])

12.1 Reflection positivity permanence under reflection-equivariant coarse graining and under projective limits on cylinder observables

The fixed–cutoff story (Parts 9–11) produces, at each lattice spacing a>0, an infinite-volume Euclidean state \mu_a that is time-translation invariant and reflection positive, and hence (by OS reconstruction) an OS Hamiltonian H_a whose gap is controlled by Euclidean time decay. The continuum step now forces us to compare different cutoffs a\downarrow 0. In particular, we need reflection positivity to survive whatever “RG / coarse-graining / projective” architecture is used to connect scales, because OS reconstruction is built on reflection positivity.

The unified outline isolates this as the first continuum-level structural input: reflection positivity must persist under reflection-equivariant coarse graining and (at least) on cylinder observables in projective limits.  

This subsection records two permanence principles:
	1.	Pushforward permanence. If \mu is reflection positive and P is a coarse-graining map commuting with reflection and preserving positive-time measurability, then the pushforward P_\#\mu is also reflection positive.  
	2.	Projective-limit permanence (cylinder level). If (\mu_a) is a consistent projective family of reflection-positive measures with reflection-equivariant projections, then the projective limit measure is reflection positive on cylinder functions.  

Both statements are purely measure-theoretic: they do not use any special feature of the Wilson action beyond reflection positivity at the starting scale.

12.1.1 Abstract reflected probability spaces and reflection positivity

We work in the standard “reflected measurable space” framework.

Definition 12.1 (reflected probability space).
A reflected probability space is a quintuple
(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu,\theta;\mathcal F_+)
where (\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu) is a probability space, \theta:\Omega\to\Omega is a measurable involution (\theta^2=\mathrm{id}), and \mathcal F_+\subset\mathcal F is a distinguished sub-\sigma-algebra (“positive-time information”). In lattice gauge theory, \theta is induced by OS time reflection \Theta and complex conjugation on observables; we suppress that distinction here and treat \theta as the involution acting on configurations.

Definition 12.2 (reflection positivity).
A reflected probability space (\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu,\theta;\mathcal F_+) is reflection positive if for every bounded \mathcal F_+-measurable complex-valued function F,
\int_\Omega \overline{F(\omega)}\,F(\theta\omega)\,d\mu(\omega)\ \ge\ 0.
\tag{12.1}
Equivalently: for every finite family F_1,\dots,F_n\in L^\infty(\mathcal F_+), the Gram matrix
\Big(\ \langle F_i,\theta F_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}\ \Big)_{i,j=1}^n
\quad\text{with}\quad
\langle F,\theta G\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}:=\int \overline{F(\omega)}\,G(\theta\omega)\,d\mu(\omega)
\tag{12.2}
is positive semidefinite.  

This is the abstract form of the OS reflection-positivity axiom from Part 4.

⸻

12.1.2 Coarse graining that commutes with reflection

We now formalize the minimal compatibility conditions a coarse-graining map must satisfy in order to preserve reflection positivity.

Let (\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu,\theta;\mathcal F_+) be a reflected probability space at a “fine” scale, and let (\Omega',\mathcal F',\theta';\mathcal F'_+) be a reflected measurable space at a “coarse” scale.

A coarse-graining map P:\Omega\to\Omega' should satisfy:
	1.	Reflection equivariance
P\circ\theta=\theta'\circ P,
\tag{12.3}
i.e. coarse graining commutes with reflection.  
	2.	Positive-time preservation
P^{-1}(\mathcal F'_+)\subset \mathcal F_+,
\tag{12.4}
i.e. a positive-time observable at the coarse scale pulls back to a positive-time observable at the fine scale.  
	3.	Pushforward relation of measures
\mu':=P_\#\mu,
\tag{12.5}
so \mu' is the induced (“renormalized”) measure at the coarse scale.

These are exactly the compatibility conditions stated for the cutoff maps P_{a\to a'} in the continuum-limit scaffolding.  

Lemma 12.3 (reflection positivity survives reflection-equivariant pushforward).
Assume (\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu,\theta;\mathcal F_+) is reflection positive and P:\Omega\to\Omega' satisfies (12.3)–(12.5). Then the pushed-forward reflected probability space (\Omega',\mathcal F',\mu',\theta';\mathcal F'_+) is reflection positive.

Proof.
Take any bounded \mathcal F'_+-measurable G_1,\dots,G_n. Set F_i:=G_i\circ P. By (12.4), each F_i is \mathcal F_+-measurable. Then compute the Gram entries under \mu':
\langle G_i,\theta'G_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu')}
=\int_{\Omega'} \overline{G_i(\omega')}\,G_j(\theta'\omega')\,d\mu'(\omega').
Using \mu'=P_\#\mu this equals
\int_{\Omega}\overline{G_i(P\omega)}\,G_j(\theta'(P\omega))\,d\mu(\omega).
By reflection equivariance (12.3), \theta'(P\omega)=P(\theta\omega), so the last integral becomes
\int_{\Omega}\overline{F_i(\omega)}\,F_j(\theta\omega)\,d\mu(\omega)
=\langle F_i,\theta F_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}.
Thus the Gram matrix (\langle G_i,\theta'G_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu')}) equals the Gram matrix (\langle F_i,\theta F_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}), which is positive semidefinite by reflection positivity of \mu. ∎  

Remark 12.4 (what this means for RG).
This lemma packages the “no new RP work” message: once reflection positivity is established at a scale, any RG/block map that commutes with reflection and does not leak negative-time dependence into positive-time observables preserves RP at the coarser scale.

12.1.3 Projective limits: cylinder-level permanence of reflection positivity

Coarse graining typically comes as a family of compatible maps. The cleanest abstraction is the inverse-limit (projective) framework.

Let \mathcal A be an index set for cutoffs (e.g. a discrete sequence a_n\downarrow 0). For each a\in\mathcal A, assume a reflected probability space
(\Omega_a,\mathcal F_a,\mu_a,\theta_a;\mathcal F_{a,+}).

Assume we have measurable maps P_{a\to a'}:\Omega_a\to\Omega_{a'} whenever a is “finer” than a' (e.g. a<a'), satisfying:
	•	Projective compatibility
P_{a'\to a''}\circ P_{a\to a'}=P_{a\to a''},
\tag{12.6}
	•	Reflection equivariance
P_{a\to a'}\circ\theta_a=\theta_{a'}\circ P_{a\to a'},
\tag{12.7}
	•	Positive-time preservation
P_{a\to a'}^{-1}(\mathcal F_{a',+})\subset \mathcal F_{a,+},
\tag{12.8}
	•	Consistency of measures
(P_{a\to a'})_\#\mu_a=\mu_{a'}.
\tag{12.9}

These are exactly the “RG/projective architecture” requirements that the outline isolates as a continuum-level input.  

Let \Omega denote the inverse-limit space with canonical projections \pi_a:\Omega\to\Omega_a. Cylinder functions on \Omega are those of the form F=\widetilde F\circ \pi_a for some a and some bounded \widetilde F on \Omega_a.

By Kolmogorov extension (under standard consistency assumptions), there exists a projective limit measure \mu on \Omega such that
\int_\Omega (\widetilde\Psi\circ \pi_a)\,d\mu
=
\int_{\Omega_a}\widetilde\Psi\,d\mu_a
\quad\text{for all bounded measurable }\widetilde\Psi\text{ on }\Omega_a.
\tag{12.10}
Moreover, the reflection equivariance (12.7) forces a unique involution \theta on \Omega with
\pi_a\circ\theta=\theta_a\circ\pi_a
\qquad\text{for every }a.
\tag{12.11}

Lemma 12.5 (RP passes to the inverse limit, cylinder version).
Assume each \mu_a is reflection positive. Let \mu be the projective limit measure and \theta the induced reflection on \Omega. Then \mu is reflection positive on cylinder functions: for any fixed a and any bounded \mathcal F_{a,+}-measurable \widetilde F_1,\dots,\widetilde F_n, defining F_i:=\widetilde F_i\circ\pi_a yields a positive semidefinite Gram matrix
\Big(\ \langle F_i,\theta F_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}\ \Big)_{i,j=1}^n \succeq 0.
\tag{12.12}

Proof.
Let F_i=\widetilde F_i\circ\pi_a. Using the cylinder-measure identity (12.10) and the intertwining (12.11),
\langle F_i,\theta F_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}
=
\int_\Omega \overline{\widetilde F_i(\pi_a\omega)}\ \widetilde F_j(\theta_a(\pi_a\omega))\,d\mu(\omega)
=
\int_{\Omega_a}\overline{\widetilde F_i(u)}\ \widetilde F_j(\theta_a u)\,d\mu_a(u)
=
\langle \widetilde F_i,\theta_a\widetilde F_j\rangle_{L^2(\mu_a)}.
Thus the cylinder Gram matrix under \mu equals the Gram matrix under \mu_a, which is PSD because \mu_a is reflection positive. ∎  

Remark 12.6 (why “cylinder level” is exactly the right target).
OS reconstruction (as used in Part 11) is formulated on local observables—i.e. cylinder functions supported in a positive-time half-space. So cylinder-level reflection positivity is the minimal permanence statement that is genuinely needed downstream.

12.1 Reflection positivity permanence under reflection-equivariant coarse graining and under projective limits on cylinder observables

Part 11 completes the fixed–cutoff story: at each lattice spacing a>0, time-direction exponential decay of Euclidean covariances yields a strictly positive OS Hamiltonian gap of size \eta(a)/a (Theorem 11.2). Part 12 explains how to pass this information to the continuum, and it begins with the one structural feature that must not get broken en route: reflection positivity.

Two operations occur in any plausible continuum passage:
	1.	Coarse graining / renormalization: map a fine lattice configuration to a coarser one (block maps, decimation, flowed variables, etc.), producing an effective measure.
	2.	Taking a limit: as a\downarrow 0, build a limiting object on cylinder observables (a projective limit) and then reconstruct a continuum OS Hilbert space/Hamiltonian.

This subsection records the elementary permanence statements for reflection positivity under these two operations, isolating exactly what must be checked about the coarse-graining maps.

⸻

12.1.1 Reflection positivity on a general configuration space
Let (\Omega,\mathcal F) be a measurable configuration space equipped with:
	•	a time reflection \Theta:\Omega\to\Omega (an involution),
	•	a time translation (\tau_n)_{n\in\mathbb Z} commuting with \Theta in the usual way (we will only need compatibility on the positive-time algebra),
	•	a distinguished sub-*-algebra \mathcal A_+\subset L^\infty(\Omega,\mathcal F) of positive-time observables (cylinder functions depending only on finitely many degrees of freedom in the nonnegative-time half-space).

Define the antilinear involution \theta on observables by
(\theta F)(\omega)\ :=\ \overline{F(\Theta\omega)}.
\tag{12.1}
A probability measure \mu on (\Omega,\mathcal F) is reflection positive (with respect to (\Theta,\mathcal A_+)) if
\mu\big((\theta F)\,F\big)\ \ge\ 0
\qquad\text{for all }F\in\mathcal A_+.
\tag{12.2}

In the lattice gauge theory setting, \Omega will be M_\Lambda (or an infinite-volume limit), \Theta is the OS reflection, and \mathcal A_+ is the algebra of bounded cylinder functions supported in nonnegative times.

⸻

12.1.2 Pushforward under a reflection-equivariant coarse-graining map
We now formalize the minimal structure needed from a coarse graining (or RG) transformation.

Let (\Omega_f,\mathcal F_f) be the fine configuration space with reflection \Theta_f and positive-time algebra \mathcal A_{f,+}. Let (\Omega_c,\mathcal F_c) be the coarse configuration space with reflection \Theta_c and positive-time algebra \mathcal A_{c,+}.

Let \pi:\Omega_f\to\Omega_c be a measurable map (“coarse graining”).

Assumption 12.1 (reflection-equivariance and positive-time compatibility).
We assume:
	1.	(Reflection equivariance) \pi\circ\Theta_f=\Theta_c\circ\pi.
	2.	(Positive-time pullback) For every F\in\mathcal A_{c,+}, the pullback F\circ\pi lies in \mathcal A_{f,+}.

\tag{12.3}

Assume \pi satisfies Assumption 12.1.
	1.	If \mu_f is reflection invariant, i.e. \mu_f(H)=\mu_f(H\circ\Theta_f) for all bounded measurable H, then \mu_c is reflection invariant with respect to \Theta_c.
	2.	If \mu_f is reflection positive on (\Omega_f,\Theta_f,\mathcal A_{f,+}), then \mu_c is reflection positive on (\Omega_c,\Theta_c,\mathcal A_{c,+}).

Proof.
(1) Let H be bounded measurable on \Omega_c. Then by definition and equivariance,
\mu_c(H\circ\Theta_c)
=\mu_f\big((H\circ\Theta_c)\circ\pi\big)
=\mu_f\big(H\circ(\Theta_c\circ\pi)\big)
=\mu_f\big(H\circ(\pi\circ\Theta_f)\big)
=\mu_f\big((H\circ\pi)\circ\Theta_f\big).
If \mu_f is reflection invariant, the last expression equals \mu_f(H\circ\pi)=\mu_c(H).

=\overline{F(\Theta_c(\pi(\cdot)))}
=\overline{F(\pi(\Theta_f(\cdot)))}
=\big(\theta_f(F\circ\pi)\big).
Therefore,
\mu_c\big((\theta_c F)F\big)

\ge 0,
by reflection positivity of \mu_f. \square

## 12.4. Lyapunov drift: the mechanism that replaces global curvature

The idea is to prove that **outside a controlled region** (call it (K)), the diffusion generated by (L) has a **strict negative drift** for some coercive “weight” function (W). This drift plays the role that a **global** Bakry–Émery lower bound would play in the easy case: it prevents the dynamics/measure from spending too much time in “large-field” regions and lets us bootstrap **local** functional inequalities on (K) into **global** ones.

Concretely, we will use Lyapunov drift to manufacture inequalities of the form
[
\int_{M\setminus K} f^2,d\mu \ \lesssim\ \int \Gamma(f),d\mu \ +\ \int_{K} f^2,d\mu,
]
which is exactly the type of estimate needed in the next section to globalize a local Poincaré inequality.

---

### 12.4.1. Lyapunov condition

**Definition 12.7 (Linear Foster–Lyapunov drift).**
A function (W\in C^2(M)) is a Lyapunov function (for our purposes) if (W\ge 1) and there exist constants (\lambda>0), (b\ge 0), and a measurable set (K\subset M) (typically compact, “the safe region”) such that
[
LW \ \le\ -\lambda W \ +\ b,\mathbf 1_K
\quad\text{pointwise on }M.
\tag{12.2}
]
Equivalently, on (K^c) one has (LW\le -\lambda W): outside (K), (W) is strictly (L)-superharmonic, encoding drift back toward (K).

**Remarks (why this form is exactly what we need).**

1. The normalization (W\ge 1) is not cosmetic: it lets us divide by (W) safely and replace (\mathbf 1_K/W) by (\mathbf 1_K) whenever we only need upper bounds.

2. The “linear” form (-\lambda W) is the Foster–Lyapunov condition that naturally corresponds to exponential ergodicity and (in reversible settings) to Poincaré-type coercivity. Stronger (superlinear) drifts are possible, but linear drift is the right baseline for a global spectral gap argument.

---

### 12.4.2. The key Lyapunov energy lemma (do not skip this; it is the engine)

The point of this lemma is that it converts the **ratio** (-Lh/h) into a **quadratic form controlled by the Dirichlet energy**. It is the clean algebraic device that lets drift inequalities feed into Poincaré inequalities.

**Lemma 12.8 (Basic Lyapunov inequality).**
Let (h\in C^2(M)) satisfy (h>0). Then for any smooth (f) in the form domain,
[
\int_M \left(-\frac{Lh}{h}\right) f^2,d\mu \ \le\ \int_M \Gamma(f),d\mu.
\tag{12.3}
]

**Proof.**
By Lemma 12.1 (the reversibility / Dirichlet form identity),
[
\int_M \left(-\frac{Lh}{h}\right) f^2,d\mu
= -\int_M \frac{f^2}{h},Lh,d\mu
= \int_M \Gamma!\left(\frac{f^2}{h},h\right),d\mu
= \int_M \Gamma!\left(h,\frac{f^2}{h}\right),d\mu.
]
Using (\Gamma(u,v)=\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle), compute
[
\nabla!\left(\frac{f^2}{h}\right)
= \frac{2f\nabla f}{h} \ -\ \frac{f^2\nabla h}{h^2}.
]
Therefore
[
\Gamma!\left(h,\frac{f^2}{h}\right)
= \left\langle \nabla h,\ \frac{2f\nabla f}{h} - \frac{f^2\nabla h}{h^2}\right\rangle
= \frac{2f}{h},\Gamma(f,h) \ -\ \frac{f^2}{h^2}\Gamma(h).
]
Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to (\Gamma(f,h)=\langle\nabla f,\nabla h\rangle):
[
\frac{2f}{h}\Gamma(f,h)
\le \frac{2|f|}{h}|\nabla f||\nabla h|
\le |\nabla f|^2 + \frac{f^2}{h^2}|\nabla h|^2
= \Gamma(f) + \frac{f^2}{h^2}\Gamma(h).
]
Substitute into the previous line; the (\frac{f^2}{h^2}\Gamma(h)) term cancels against the negative term, yielding the pointwise bound
[
\Gamma!\left(h,\frac{f^2}{h}\right)\le \Gamma(f).
]
Integrating against (\mu) gives (12.3). (\square)

**Immediate consequence (how drift becomes an (L^2)-control).**
If (W\ge 1) satisfies the Lyapunov drift (12.2), then dividing by (\lambda W) gives
[
1 \ \le\ -\frac{LW}{\lambda W} \ +\ \frac{b}{\lambda},\frac{\mathbf 1_K}{W}
\ \le\ -\frac{LW}{\lambda W} \ +\ \frac{b}{\lambda},\mathbf 1_K.
]
Multiplying by (f^2) and integrating, and then applying Lemma 12.8 with (h=W), produces exactly the kind of “outside-(K) is controlled by energy + inside-(K)” inequality that will drive the local-to-global Poincaré theorem in §12.5.
