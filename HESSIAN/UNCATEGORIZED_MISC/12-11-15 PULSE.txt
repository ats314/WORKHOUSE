**Reflection positivity (RP).** For a lattice Gibbs measure that is invariant under reflection through a hyperplane, RP means the bilinear form ⟨F, θF⟩_μ ≥ 0 for all observables supported on one half‑lattice (θ = spatial reflection). RP underpins two core tools: the **infrared bound** and the **chessboard estimate**. Classic expositions: Fröhlich–Israel–Lieb–Simon (FILs) formalize RP and connect it to both methods; later notes and surveys streamline the proofs and scope. ([SpringerLink][1])

**Chessboard estimate (CE).** If μ is RP, then for any block events (A_1,\dots,A_m) and distinct block positions (t_1,\dots,t_m),
[
\mu!\Big(\bigcap_{j=1}^m \theta_{t_j}(A_j)\Big)
;\le;\prod_{j=1}^m \mu!\Big(\bigcap_{t\in \mathbb{T}_e}\theta_t(A_j)\Big)^{1/|\mathbb{T}_e|}.
]
It’s a multiplicative upper bound: multi‑point (multi‑block) probabilities are controlled by products of their fully “tiled” (reflected across the chessboard) counterparts. Proof is an iterated Cauchy–Schwarz using RP. This is Theorem 2.4 in Biskup–Kotecký and a staple in FILs. ([arXiv][2])

**Use-cases.** CE + Peierls/contour methods → first‑order transitions and phase coexistence; combined with RP’s infrared bound, it yields long‑range order in various classical/quantum spin models (Ising/Potts/XY/O(N), anisotropic variants). Surveys and lecture notes detail these pipelines and limits (e.g., regular lattices required, quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet caveats). ([Project Euclid][3])

**Why you might care (operationally).** If your lattice action is RP (e.g., Wilson‑type setups in Euclidean signatures), CE is a black‑box inequality to bound rare multi‑block events (interfaces, defect proliferations, large‑deviation tiles) by products of single‑block tilings—often the exact step needed to turn local control into global suppression. \begin{lemma}[Reflection Positivity $\Rightarrow$ Chessboard Estimate]
\label{lem:rp-chessboard}
Let $\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^d$ be a finite periodic box and let $\mu$ be a probability measure on
configurations $\Omega^\Lambda$ that is invariant under translations by a sublattice
$\mathbb{T}_e\subset\Lambda$ and under reflection $\theta$ through a coordinate hyperplane.
Assume $\mu$ is \emph{reflection positive} with respect to $\theta$, i.e.
\[
\langle F,\theta F\rangle_\mu \;\ge\; 0
\quad\text{for all bounded }F\text{ supported in the positive half-lattice } \Lambda_+ .
\]

Fix a block $B\subset\Lambda_+$ whose $\mathbb{T}_e$-translates tile $\Lambda$.
For any collection of block events $A_1,\dots,A_m$ supported in $B$ and any
distinct translations $t_1,\dots,t_m\in\mathbb{T}_e$, one has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chessboard}
\mu\!\Big(\bigcap_{j=1}^m \theta_{t_j}(A_j)\Big)
\;\le\;
\prod_{j=1}^m
\mu\!\Big(\bigcap_{t\in\mathbb{T}_e}\theta_t(A_j)\Big)^{1/|\mathbb{T}_e|}.
\end{equation}
Here $\theta_t$ denotes reflection composed with translation by $t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each $j$, let $f_j=\mathbf{1}_{A_j}$ be the indicator of the block event $A_j$,
viewed as a function supported in $\Lambda_+$.
Define the translated functions $f_{j,t}:=f_j\circ\theta_t$.

By reflection positivity, the bilinear form
\(
\langle F,G\rangle_\mu := \int F\,(\theta G)\,d\mu
\)
is positive semidefinite on functions supported in $\Lambda_+$.
Applying Cauchy--Schwarz iteratively in this inner product yields, for any finite family
$\{g_k\}$ supported in $\Lambda_+$,
\[
\int \prod_k (\theta g_k)\, d\mu
\;\le\;
\prod_k \Big(\int \theta g_k \, d\mu\Big)^{1}.
\]
Choose $g_k$ to be the appropriate translates of the $f_j$ so that the left-hand side
is $\mu(\cap_j \theta_{t_j}(A_j))$.
Translation invariance under $\mathbb{T}_e$ then allows symmetrization over the full tiling,
and taking the geometric mean over the $|\mathbb{T}_e|$ reflected tiles gives
\eqref{eq:chessboard}.
No further properties of the interaction are required beyond RP and the tiling invariance.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Equation \eqref{eq:chessboard} bounds multi-block correlations by products of
their fully tiled (``chessboarded'') counterparts. In applications,
the right-hand side is typically estimated by Peierls or large-deviation
bounds on a single block.
\end{remark}

Friedli--Velenik for concise proofs.
