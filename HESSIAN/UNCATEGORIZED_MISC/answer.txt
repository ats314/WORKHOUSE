1. Abstract

We consider 4D Euclidean SU(N) Yang–Mills theory and the “anomaly source” operator
S_{\mathrm{anom}} = \nabla^2 J_t appearing in a Projected Bochner–Hessian flow for the Hessian of the effective action.
Here J_t is the forcing term generated by the running of the gauge coupling under the RG flow, and at one loop it is determined by the Yang–Mills beta function.

Using the background field method, a quadratic expansion around the vacuum, and Landau gauge, we compute S_{\mathrm{anom}} in momentum space and show that, in the perturbative UV regime (small coupling), it is positive definite on the transverse gauge-field fluctuations. Its spectrum has a strictly positive minimal eigenvalue, bounded from below by
\sigma_A(k) \;\ge\; c\, g^2(k)\, k^2, \qquad
c = \frac{11 N}{24\pi^2} > 0,
for each momentum scale k in the perturbative domain. This establishes the desired perturbative anomaly source bound required for Prong B of the Yang–Mills mass gap strategy.

⸻

2. Theorem (Perturbative Anomaly Source Bound)

Theorem.
Consider pure SU(N) Yang–Mills theory in four-dimensional Euclidean space. Let g(k) be the renormalized running coupling at a momentum scale k, in a scheme where the one‑loop beta function is
\beta(g) = -\beta_0 g^3 + O(g^5), \qquad
\beta_0 = \frac{11N}{48\pi^2} > 0.
Let J_t[A] be the RG forcing term arising from the running of g, and define the anomaly source operator
S_{\mathrm{anom}} := \nabla^2 J_t
as the Hessian of J_t with respect to the gauge field, evaluated at the trivial background A_\mu = 0, in Landau gauge.

Then, for each fixed UV momentum scale k where g(k) is sufficiently small (perturbative regime), the operator S_{\mathrm{anom}}(k) acting on transverse gluon modes is positive definite and has minimal eigenvalue
\sigma_A(k) = 2 \beta_0\, g^2(k)\, k^2,
so in particular
\sigma_A(k) \;\ge\; c\, g^2(k)\, k^2 > 0,
\qquad
c := 2\beta_0 = \frac{11N}{24\pi^2}.

3. Derivation

3.1 Setup: Euclidean Yang–Mills and RG forcing

We work with pure SU(N) Yang–Mills theory on \mathbb{R}^4 in Euclidean signature.
Let T^a be generators of \mathfrak{su}(N) in the fundamental representation, normalized by
\mathrm{tr}(T^a T^b) = \frac{1}{2}\,\delta^{ab}, \qquad a,b = 1,\dots, N^2-1.

The gauge field is A_\mu = A^a_\mu T^a. The field strength is
F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu
+ i g [A_\mu, A_\nu],
or in components
F^a_{\mu\nu}
= \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu
+ g f^{abc} A^b_\mu A^c_\nu.

With this normalization,
\mathrm{Tr}(F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu})
= \mathrm{tr}(F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu})
= \tfrac{1}{2} F^a_{\mu\nu} F^a_{\mu\nu}.

The (canonically normalized) classical action is
S_{\mathrm{YM}}[A]
= \frac{1}{4 g^2(\mu)} \int d^4x\, F^a_{\mu\nu} F^a_{\mu\nu},
where g(\mu) is the renormalized coupling at reference scale \mu.

The one‑loop beta function for pure SU(N) Yang–Mills is 
\beta(g) := \mu \frac{dg}{d\mu}
= -\beta_0 g^3 + O(g^5),
\qquad \beta_0 = \frac{11N}{48\pi^2} > 0.

We introduce an RG scale k (e.g. the IR cutoff of an effective average action \Gamma_k or a Wilsonian action S_t), and write t = \log(k/\mu). The coupling runs as g = g(k), determined by the beta function.

The PBH flow you wrote involves a forcing term J_t in the “time” t. From standard RG arguments (in particular background-field methods, where the running of the gauge coupling is encoded in the coefficient of F^2 in the effective action ), the leading effect of the running coupling on the action (in a derivative expansion) has the structure
J_t[A] \;=\; \beta_0\, g^2(k) \int d^4x\,\mathrm{Tr}\big(F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu}\big) + O(g^4),
i.e.
J_t[A] \;=\; c_0\, g^2(k) \int d^4x\, F^a_{\mu\nu} F^a_{\mu\nu} + O(g^4),
\qquad c_0 = \frac{\beta_0}{2}.
(We justify the c_0 value from the log form below.)

This is the local, instantaneous forcing obtained by differentiating the RG–improved log term
J[A] = -\frac{\beta_0 g^2}{4}
\int d^4x\,\mathrm{Tr}\big(F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\big)\,
\log\!\left(\frac{F^2}{\mu^4}\right)
with respect to t = \log\mu: indeed
\frac{\partial J[A]}{\partial t}
= -\frac{\beta_0 g^2}{4}
\int \mathrm{Tr}(F^2)\,
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
\log\!\left(\frac{F^2}{\mu^4}\right)
= -\frac{\beta_0 g^2}{4}
\int \mathrm{Tr}(F^2)\,(-4)
= \beta_0 g^2 \int \mathrm{Tr}(F^2),
and \mathrm{Tr}(F^2) = \tfrac{1}{2}F^a_{\mu\nu}F^a_{\mu\nu} gives c_0 = \beta_0/2.

The nonlocality and nonanalyticity are in the integrated functional J[A]; the forcing J_t that enters the PBH flow is local and analytic in A near A=0, and this is what we actually need to differentiate to get S_{\mathrm{anom}}.

So from now on we work with
J_t[A] = c_0\, g^2(k) \int d^4x\, F^a_{\mu\nu} F^a_{\mu\nu},
\quad c_0 = \frac{\beta_0}{2} > 0.
\tag{3.1}

3.2 Background-field split and quadratic expansion around the vacuum

We use the background field method: write
A_\mu = \bar{A}_\mu + a_\mu,
where \bar{A}_\mu is a classical background field and a_\mu is the quantum fluctuation. The background field effective action \Gamma[\bar{A}] is gauge invariant under background gauge transformations. 

For the perturbative UV analysis of the mass gap, it is sufficient (and technically simplest) to evaluate the Hessian around the trivial background
\bar{A}_\mu = 0,
in which case A_\mu = a_\mu. This is justified because in the UV the effective potential is dominated by small fluctuations around the perturbative vacuum; any nontrivial background field configuration is suppressed for g(k) \ll 1.

Expand the field strength in powers of a:
F^a_{\mu\nu}(a)
= \partial_\mu a^a_\nu - \partial_\nu a^a_\mu
+ g f^{abc} a^b_\mu a^c_\nu.
To obtain the Hessian (quadratic kernel) we only need the expansion up to second order in a, so in F^2 we keep only the Abelian part
f^a_{\mu\nu}(a) := \partial_\mu a^a_\nu - \partial_\nu a^a_\mu,
and
F^a_{\mu\nu} F^a_{\mu\nu}
= f^a_{\mu\nu} f^a_{\mu\nu} + O(g a^3).
Thus, to quadratic order,
J_t[A] \;\equiv\; J_t^{(2)}[a] + O(a^3),
with
J_t^{(2)}[a]
= c_0 g^2(k) \int d^4x\, f^a_{\mu\nu}(a) f^a_{\mu\nu}(a).
\tag{3.2}

We now express this explicitly as a quadratic form in a_\mu^a.

Using integration by parts, the standard identity in Euclidean space is (for each colour index a) 
\int d^4x\, f^a_{\mu\nu} f^a_{\mu\nu}
= 2 \int d^4x\, a^a_\mu(x)\, K^{\mu\nu}\, a^a_\nu(x),
\tag{3.3}
where
K^{\mu\nu} := -\partial^2 \delta^{\mu\nu} + \partial^\mu \partial^\nu.
\tag{3.4}

Substituting (3.3) into (3.2) gives
J_t^{(2)}[a]
= 2 c_0 g^2(k) \int d^4x\, a^a_\mu(x)\, K^{\mu\nu}\, a^a_\nu(x).
\tag{3.5}

3.3 Gauge choice and definition of the Hessian

To define the Hessian operator unambiguously we must fix gauge, since K^{\mu\nu} has zero modes along pure gauge directions. We choose Landau gauge
\partial^\mu a^a_\mu = 0,
\tag{3.6}
which coincides with background-field Landau gauge at \bar{A}=0.

Let \mathcal{H}_T denote the space of transverse fluctuations:
\mathcal{H}_T := \{ a_\mu^a \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^4) \mid \partial^\mu a^a_\mu = 0\}.
The Faddeev–Popov ghosts ensure that longitudinal modes do not contribute to gauge-invariant observables, so for positivity of the gluonic quadratic form we may restrict to \mathcal{H}_T.

Write the quadratic functional in the usual Hessian form:
J_t^{(2)}[a]
= \frac{1}{2} \int d^4x\, d^4y\,
a^a_\mu(x) \, (S_{\mathrm{anom}})^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(x,y)\, a^b_\nu(y),
\tag{3.7}
where the kernel S_{\mathrm{anom}} is the second functional derivative of J_t,
(S_{\mathrm{anom}})^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(x,y)
:= \frac{\delta^2 J_t[A]}{\delta A^a_\mu(x)\, \delta A^b_\nu(y)}\bigg|_{A=0}.
\tag{3.8}
Comparing (3.5) and (3.7), and using translational invariance, we identify
(S_{\mathrm{anom}})^{\mu\nu}_{ab}
= 4\, c_0\, g^2(k)\, K^{\mu\nu}\, \delta_{ab}
\quad \text{on } \mathcal{H}_T.
\tag{3.9}

Thus, up to an overall positive constant, the anomaly source operator is just the standard Euclidean kinetic operator projected to transverse modes.

3.4 Momentum-space representation

Fourier transform the field:
a^a_\mu(x)
= \int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\, e^{i k\cdot x}\, \tilde{a}^a_\mu(k),
and correspondingly the operator:
K^{\mu\nu}(k)
= k^2 \delta^{\mu\nu} - k^\mu k^\nu.
\tag{3.10}

Define the transverse projector
P_T^{\mu\nu}(k)
:= \delta^{\mu\nu} - \frac{k^\mu k^\nu}{k^2},
\tag{3.11}
so that
K^{\mu\nu}(k) = k^2 P_T^{\mu\nu}(k).
\tag{3.12}

The Landau gauge condition \partial^\mu a^a_\mu=0 becomes
k^\mu \tilde{a}^a_\mu(k) = 0,
\tag{3.13}
so \tilde{a}^a_\mu(k) lives in the transverse subspace on which P_T^{\mu\nu} acts as the identity:
P_T^{\mu\nu}(k)\, \tilde{a}^a_\nu(k) = \tilde{a}^a_\mu(k).

In momentum space, (3.5) becomes
\begin{aligned}
J_t^{(2)}[a]
&= 2 c_0 g^2(k)
\int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\,
\tilde{a}^a_\mu(-k)\, K^{\mu\nu}(k)\, \tilde{a}^a_\nu(k) \\
&= 2 c_0 g^2(k)
\int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\,
\tilde{a}^a_\mu(-k)\, k^2 P_T^{\mu\nu}(k)\, \tilde{a}^a_\nu(k).
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.14}

Writing again in the Hessian form
J_t^{(2)}[a]
= \frac{1}{2} \int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\,
\tilde{a}^a_\mu(-k)\, (S_{\mathrm{anom}}(k))^{\mu\nu}_{ab}\, \tilde{a}^b_\nu(k),
\tag{3.15}
we read off
(S_{\mathrm{anom}}(k))^{\mu\nu}_{ab}
= 4 c_0\, g^2(k)\, k^2\, P_T^{\mu\nu}(k)\, \delta_{ab}.
\tag{3.16}

3.5 Diagonalisation and eigenvalues

For each momentum k\neq 0, choose an orthonormal basis of transverse polarization vectors \{\varepsilon_r^\mu(k)\}_{r=1}^{d_T} with
k_\mu \varepsilon_r^\mu(k) = 0, \qquad
\varepsilon_r^\mu(k) \varepsilon_{s\mu}(k) = \delta_{rs}.
In d=4 Euclidean dimensions, the transverse subspace has dimension d_T = 3.

Decompose
\tilde{a}^a_\mu(k) = \sum_{r=1}^{d_T} \varepsilon_r^\mu(k)\, \tilde{a}^a_r(k).

Using P_T^{\mu\nu}(k)\varepsilon_{r\nu}(k)=\varepsilon_r^\mu(k), (3.14) becomes
J_t^{(2)}[a]
= 2 c_0 g^2(k)
\int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\, k^2
\sum_{a=1}^{N^2-1}\sum_{r=1}^{d_T}
|\tilde{a}^a_r(k)|^2.
\tag{3.17}

Comparing with (3.15), the quadratic form is diagonal in the colour and polarization indices with eigenvalues
\lambda(k) = 4 c_0 g^2(k) k^2
if one defines S_{\mathrm{anom}}(k) via (3.16), but note that in the explicit quadratic form (3.17) the factor is actually
J_t^{(2)}[a]
= \frac{1}{2} \int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}
\sum_{a,r} (4 c_0 g^2 k^2) |\tilde{a}^a_r(k)|^2.
So the eigenvalue for each transverse polarization and colour is
\lambda(k) = 4 c_0\, g^2(k)\, k^2.
\tag{3.18}

However, recall that our definition of c_0 above was via
J_t[A] = c_0 g^2(k) \int F^a_{\mu\nu} F^a_{\mu\nu},
and we fixed it by matching to the log functional:
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
\left(
-\frac{\beta_0 g^2}{4} \int \mathrm{Tr}(F^2)\,\log(F^2/\mu^4)
\right)
= \beta_0 g^2 \int \mathrm{Tr}(F^2)
= \frac{\beta_0 g^2}{2} \int F^a_{\mu\nu} F^a_{\mu\nu}.
So
c_0 = \frac{\beta_0}{2}.
Substitute into (3.18):
\lambda(k)
= 4 \left(\frac{\beta_0}{2}\right) g^2(k) k^2
= 2 \beta_0\, g^2(k) k^2.
\tag{3.19}

Thus the spectrum of S_{\mathrm{anom}}(k) on the physical (transverse) fluctuation space is
\mathrm{spec}(S_{\mathrm{anom}}(k))
= \big\{ 2 \beta_0\, g^2(k)\, k^2 \big\},
with multiplicity (N^2-1) d_T.

Longitudinal modes (for which P_T has eigenvalue 0) are eliminated by the Landau gauge condition, so they are not in the domain of the physical Hessian.

3.6 Positivity and lower bound

We now identify the minimal eigenvalue \sigma_A(k) and prove the bound.

From (3.19), for each fixed momentum scale k,
\sigma_A(k) = 2 \beta_0\, g^2(k)\, k^2.
\tag{3.20}

Using the explicit one-loop value for the pure Yang–Mills beta-function coefficient 
\beta_0 = \frac{11 N}{48\pi^2} > 0,
we have
\sigma_A(k)
= 2 \frac{11 N}{48\pi^2} g^2(k)\, k^2
= \frac{11N}{24\pi^2} g^2(k)\, k^2.
\tag{3.21}

Define
c := \frac{11N}{24\pi^2} > 0.
\tag{3.22}
Then
\sigma_A(k) = c\, g^2(k)\, k^2.
\tag{3.23}

For any transverse fluctuation a \in \mathcal{H}_T, we can write (in momentum space)
\int d^4x\,d^4y\,
a^a_\mu(x)\,(S_{\mathrm{anom}})^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(x,y)\,a^b_\nu(y)
= \int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\sum_{a,r} \lambda(k) |\tilde{a}^a_r(k)|^2.
Using \lambda(k)\ge \sigma_A(k) as the unique eigenvalue in the transverse sector, we have
\int d^4x\,d^4y\,
a^a_\mu(x)\,(S_{\mathrm{anom}})^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(x,y)\,a^b_\nu(y)
\;\ge\;
\sigma_A(k)
\int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\sum_{a,r} |\tilde{a}^a_r(k)|^2.
Thus S_{\mathrm{anom}}(k) is positive definite on the transverse fluctuation space for all k in the perturbative regime, and its minimal eigenvalue satisfies the bound
\sigma_A(k) \;\ge\; c\, g^2(k)\, k^2 > 0.

This is exactly the inequality required for the anomaly source term in the PBH flow: it gives a strictly positive curvature contribution proportional to g^2 k^2, with a computable N-dependent constant.

3.7 Relation to the original log term and UV regime

Your effective action contribution
J[A]
= -\frac{\beta_0}{4} \int d^4x\, \mathrm{Tr}(F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu})
\log\!\left(\frac{F^2}{\mu^4}\right)
is a standard RG–improved one-loop form in a slowly varying background field: the \log(F^2/\mu^4) encodes the running of the coupling as a function of the background field strength. 

For the Hessian entering the PBH flow, the relevant object is the scale derivative J_t = \partial_t J, not J itself. That derivative eliminates the troublesome \log and yields a local operator proportional to \int F^2, which is analytic around A=0. We have shown that the Hessian of this local operator is positive definite in the UV (where perturbation theory is valid), with the explicit lower bound (3.23).

The UV regime is characterized by k \gg \Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}, so that g^2(k)\ll 1 and the one-loop approximation is reliable. In this domain the RG‑induced anomaly source gives a robust, strictly positive curvature contribution of order g^2 k^2 in the field-space Hessian.

4. Conclusion

Starting from the RG forcing term generated by the one-loop beta function in 4D Euclidean SU(N) Yang–Mills, we:
	1.	Expressed the forcing functional J_t[A] to leading order as a local \int F^2 operator with a positive coefficient proportional to \beta_0 g^2(k).
	2.	Expanded around the trivial background A_\mu=0, keeping terms quadratic in the fluctuation a_\mu.
	3.	Fixed Landau gauge to remove gauge zero-modes and restricted to transverse fluctuations.
	4.	Computed the Hessian operator S_{\mathrm{anom}} = \nabla^2 J_t explicitly in momentum space.
	5.	Diagonalized the operator on the transverse polarization subspace and obtained the eigenvalues
\lambda(k) = 2 \beta_0\, g^2(k)\, k^2.
	6.	Used the known value \beta_0 = 11N/(48\pi^2) to obtain the bound
\sigma_A(k) = c\, g^2(k)\, k^2, \qquad c = 11N/(24\pi^2)>0.

This proves that, in the perturbative UV regime, the anomaly source operator is strictly positive on the physical fluctuation space, with a controlled spectral gap of order g^2 k^2. In the PBH flow framework, this provides the needed quantitative control (Prong B) that the anomaly term can dominate geometric corrections in the UV, supporting the persistence of the mass gap along the flow, provided the nonperturbative IR behaviour does not destroy this positivity.

\;=\; \frac{11N}{24\pi^2}\, g^2(k)\, k^2
\;\ge\; c\, g^2(k)\, k^2 \;>\; 0,\quad c = \frac{11N}{24\pi^2}.
