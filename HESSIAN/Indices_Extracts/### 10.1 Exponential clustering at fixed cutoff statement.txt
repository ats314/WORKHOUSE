### 10.1 Exponential clustering at fixed cutoff: statement 

Parts 6–9 provide all of the analytic ingredients needed for fixed-cutoff clustering:

* on a “good” event (K), Helffer–Sjöstrand plus the matrix hinge gives a **matrix Brascamp–Lieb bound** for covariances in terms of (M_H^{-1});
* Part 9 gives **exponential off-diagonal decay** for the kernel of (M_H^{-1});
* Part 8 upgrades a bound under (\mu(\cdot\mid K)) to one under (\mu) with an explicit error proportional to (\mu(K^c)).

This subsection records the resulting **clustering statement** in a form designed to be uniform in the volume and compatible with OS reconstruction later.

---

#### 10.1.1 Setup and notation

Let (\Lambda) be the finite periodic (4)-dimensional lattice from §2.1, with configuration manifold
[
M_\Lambda = G^{E(\Lambda)}
]
equipped with the product bi-invariant metric (g_\Lambda). Let (\mu=\mu_{\Lambda,\beta}) be the Wilson Gibbs measure
[
d\mu(U) ;=; Z_{\Lambda,\beta}^{-1},e^{-S_W(U)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U).
]

For an observable (F:M_\Lambda\to\mathbb R), we use the link-support notion from §6.5.4:
[
\mathrm{supp}_E(F)\subseteq E(\Lambda),
\qquad
b\notin\mathrm{supp}_E(F)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \nabla_b F\equiv 0.
]
We measure separation using the link adjacency graph distance (\mathrm{dist}_E) (Definition 6.19), and for link sets (A,B\subseteq E(\Lambda)),
[
\mathrm{dist}_E(A,B):=\min{\mathrm{dist}_E(b,b'):\ b\in A,\ b'\in B}.
]

We also fix the “gradient size” seminorm
[
|\nabla F|*{\infty,2}
;:=;
\sup*{U\in M_\Lambda}\ |\nabla F(U)|*{g*\Lambda}
================================================

\sup_{U\in M_\Lambda}\Big(\sum_{b\in E(\Lambda)}|\nabla_bF(U)|_{\mathfrak g}^2\Big)^{1/2}.
\tag{10.1}
]
For local (F), this is finite and depends only on finitely many link-derivative components.

---

#### 10.1.2 The “good set” input and the massive Maxwell decay input

Let (K\subset M_\Lambda) be a measurable event with (0<\mu(K)<1), and write (\mu_K:=\mu(\cdot\mid K)).

Assume that on (K) the conditional HS machinery yields the matrix covariance bound in terms of the massive Maxwell inverse on horizontals (as in §6.4–§6.5): there exists a fixed self-adjoint operator (M_H\succ0) on (H^{(0)}=\ker(d_0^*)\subset \mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)) such that for all (C^1) observables (F,G) with (\mu_K(G)=0),
[
\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu_K}(F,G)
;\le;
\int_K \big\langle \nabla_H F,\ M_H^{-1}\nabla_H G\big\rangle*{\mathcal C^1},d\mu_K.
\tag{10.2}
]
(When (F,G) are gauge invariant, (\nabla F,\nabla G) are horizontal, so (\nabla_H) may be replaced by (\nabla) without change.)

Assume further that Part 9 provides an exponential kernel bound for (M_H^{-1}) in the link distance:
[
\big|\big(M_H^{-1}\big)*{b,b'}\big|*{\mathrm{op}}
\ \le
C_M,e^{-\eta_M,\mathrm{dist}*E(b,b')},
\qquad b,b'\in E(\Lambda),
\tag{10.3}
]
with constants (C_M,\eta_M>0) depending only on the parameters ((m^2,\alpha)) (equivalently on ((c_H,\beta,n,\lambda*\rho))) and the dimension, but **not** on (|\Lambda|).

---

#### 10.1.3 Fixed-cutoff clustering with explicit localization error

The first statement keeps the localization error visible; it is the version that is most robust to whatever typicality mechanism is eventually used in §8.3.

**Theorem 10.1 (fixed-cutoff clustering with an explicit good-set error).**
Assume (10.2) and (10.3) hold for some event (K) and constants (C_M,\eta_M). Then for all bounded (C^1) observables (F,G),
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu}(F,G)\big|
\ \le
C*{\mathrm{good}}(F,G),e^{-\eta_M,\mathrm{dist}*E(\mathrm{supp}*E(F),\mathrm{supp}*E(G))}
;+;
8,|F|*\infty,|G|*\infty,\mu(K^c),
\tag{10.4}
]
where one may take
[
C*{\mathrm{good}}(F,G)
;:=;
C_M,|\nabla F|*{\infty,2},|\nabla G|*{\infty,2},
\sqrt{|\mathrm{supp}_E(F)|,|\mathrm{supp}_E(G)|}.
\tag{10.5}
]

*Remarks.*

1. The key feature is that (C_M,\eta_M) are **volume-uniform**: the only volume sensitivity in (10.4) sits in (\mu(K^c)), which is exactly what §8.3 is tasked to control.
2. For fixed local observables (F,G), the support sizes (|\mathrm{supp}_E(F)|,|\mathrm{supp}*E(G)|) are (O(1)), so (C*{\mathrm{good}}(F,G)) is (O(1)) as (|\Lambda|\to\infty).

---

#### 10.1.4 Pure exponential clustering under a typicality hypothesis

To turn (10.4) into the canonical “single exponential” clustering statement, we assume a typicality bound ensuring (\mu(K^c)) is superexponentially small in the graph diameter, or at least exponentially small in (|P(\Lambda)|) (which is more than enough on a bounded-degree periodic lattice).

**Assumption 10.2 (typicality at volume scale).**
There exist constants (c_{\mathrm{typ}}>0) and (\Lambda_0) such that for all periodic (\Lambda) with (|\Lambda|\ge |\Lambda_0|),
[
\mu(K^c)\ \le\ e^{-c_{\mathrm{typ}},|P(\Lambda)|}.
\tag{10.6}
]
(In the intended application, (K=K_\Lambda(\varepsilon)) from §8.2, and (10.6) is the “missing lemma” isolated in §8.3.)

Since (\mathrm{dist}*E(\cdot,\cdot)) is a graph distance on the finite set (E(\Lambda)), we always have
[
\mathrm{dist}*E(A,B)\ \le\ |E(\Lambda)|\ \le\ C*{E/P},|P(\Lambda)|
\tag{10.7}
]
for a dimension-dependent constant (C*{E/P}<\infty) (uniform in (\Lambda)). Hence (10.6) implies
[
\mu(K^c)\ \le\ \exp!\Big(-\frac{c_{\mathrm{typ}}}{C_{E/P}}\ \mathrm{dist}_E(A,B)\Big)
\qquad\text{for all }A,B\subseteq E(\Lambda)\text{ with }\mathrm{dist}_E(A,B)\ge 1.
\tag{10.8}
]

Combining (10.4) with (10.8) yields the clean clustering statement.

**Corollary 10.3 (pure exponential clustering at fixed cutoff, conditional on typicality).**
Assume (10.2)–(10.3) and Assumption 10.2. Then there exist constants (\eta>0) and (C(F,G)<\infty) such that for all sufficiently large (\Lambda),
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{\Lambda,\beta}}(F,G)\big|
\ \le
C(F,G),\exp!\Big(-\eta,\mathrm{dist}*E(\mathrm{supp}*E(F),\mathrm{supp}*E(G))\Big),
\tag{10.9}
]
with
[
\eta
:=\min\Big{\eta_M,\ \frac{c*{\mathrm{typ}}}{C*{E/P}}\Big},
\qquad
C(F,G):=2C*{\mathrm{good}}(F,G)+16|F|*\infty|G|*\infty.
\tag{10.10}
]
All constants are uniform in the volume.

---

This completes the fixed-cutoff clustering statement. In §10.2 we prove Theorem 10.1 by inserting the Part 9 kernel decay into the HS bound on (K) and then applying the covariance decomposition from §8.1; §10.3 records the dependence of (\eta) on (\beta) (and on the lattice spacing (a), when we restore physical units).




## 10.2 Haar mass and group Ricci 

This section is where the phrase **“Haar mass”** gets cashed out into an actual geometric statement: **positive Ricci curvature of the compact gauge group** (with a bi-invariant metric) produces a **universal quadratic confinement near the identity**, and that confinement shows up either as a **Ricci term** in Bakry–Émery *or* as a **strictly convex Jacobian potential** in exponential coordinates.

---

### 10.2.1 Two equivalent viewpoints

There are two ways to see the same effect.

**(A) Riemannian reference-measure picture.**
You keep the reference measure as the product Riemannian/Haar volume:
[
d\mu_\Lambda(U)=Z_\Lambda^{-1}e^{-S_\Lambda(U)},d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U).
]
Then the Bakry–Émery tensor is
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{\mu*\Lambda}=\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}+\nabla^2 S_\Lambda,
]
and the “Haar mass” contribution is literally (\mathrm{Ric}*{g*\Lambda}).

**(B) Flat-coordinate picture near the identity.**
You write each link variable as
[
U_\ell=\exp(X_\ell),
]
pull back the volume form to Lebesgue measure (dX), and the **Jacobian** becomes an extra potential:
[
d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U)=\Big(\prod_{\ell}J_G(X_\ell)\Big),dX
=\exp!\Big(-\sum_{\ell}S_H(X_\ell)\Big),dX,
\quad S_H:=-\log J_G.
]
Then the “Haar mass” is the **quadratic term in (S_H)** near (0).

Both pictures encode the same geometry; you just choose whichever makes the later estimate cleaner.

---

### 10.2.2 Ricci curvature of a compact Lie group with bi-invariant metric

#### When do you get a *strictly positive* lower bound?

A bi-invariant metric always gives (\mathrm{Ric}_G\ge 0). But to get a *uniform positive* constant
[
\mathrm{Ric}_G \ge \kappa_G, g_G\quad\text{with }\kappa_G>0,
]
you must exclude flat directions—equivalently: **no torus factor in the identity component** (e.g. compact semisimple (G)). So we assume:

**Assumption 10.2 (Positive Ricci group).**
[
\mathrm{Ric}_G \ge \kappa_G, g_G\quad\text{for some }\kappa_G>0.
]

For compact simple (G) with the usual normalization (metric proportional to (-)Killing form), you even get the stronger “Einstein” identity (\mathrm{Ric}_G=\kappa_G g_G).

#### Levi–Civita connection for bi-invariant metrics

Let (\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle) be the inner product on (\mathfrak g=T_eG) induced by (g_G), and let (X^L) denote the left-invariant vector field from (X\in\mathfrak g).

For a bi-invariant metric, the Levi–Civita connection satisfies:
[
\nabla_{X^L}Y^L=\tfrac12 [X,Y]^L.
]

**Idea of proof:** use the Koszul formula, note that metric pairings of left-invariant fields are constant (so derivative terms vanish), and use Ad-invariance to simplify the bracket terms.

#### Curvature and Ricci formula

Define curvature by
[
R(A,B)C = \nabla_A\nabla_B C - \nabla_B\nabla_A C - \nabla_{[A,B]}C.
]
With the connection above, one gets:
[
R(X^L,Y^L)Z^L = -\tfrac14 [[X,Y],Z]^L.
]

Now take an orthonormal basis ({e_i}) of (\mathfrak g). Then:
[
\mathrm{Ric}_G(X,X)
=\sum_i \langle R(X^L,e_i^L)e_i^L, X^L\rangle
= \tfrac14\sum_i |[X,e_i]|^2 \ge 0.
]

That formula makes the “no flat directions” condition totally transparent:

* If (X) commutes with everything (an abelian direction), then ([X,e_i]=0) for all (i), and (\mathrm{Ric}_G(X,X)=0).
* If (G) is semisimple, there are no such nontrivial commuting directions, so (\mathrm{Ric}_G) is strictly positive on the unit sphere, hence admits a positive minimum (\kappa_G).

---

### 10.2.3 Ricci of the product configuration manifold

On the lattice configuration manifold
[
M_\Lambda = G^{E(\Lambda)},\qquad g_\Lambda=\bigoplus_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)} g_G,
]
the Ricci tensor is block-diagonal across factors. If (v=(v_\ell)*\ell), then
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{g_\Lambda}(v,v)=\sum_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)} \mathrm{Ric}*G(v*\ell,v_\ell).
]
So if (\mathrm{Ric}*G\ge \kappa_G g_G), then
[
\mathrm{Ric}*{g_\Lambda}(v,v)\ge \kappa_G\sum_\ell g_G(v_\ell,v_\ell)
=\kappa_G, g_\Lambda(v,v).
]

**Key point:** the same (\kappa_G) works for every (\Lambda).
That’s the “uniform in volume” backbone you want later.

---

### 10.2.4 Haar Jacobian and the effective Haar potential in exponential coordinates

Now for the “Haar mass” in its most concrete form: the Jacobian of the exponential map.

Let (U=\exp_G(X)) near (e), with (X\in\mathfrak g). In these coordinates,
[
d\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}(U)=J_G(X),dX,\qquad J_G(0)=1.
]
Define the **Haar/Jacobian potential**
[
S_H(X):=-\log J_G(X),
]
so the volume form becomes (d\mathrm{vol}*{g_G}=e^{-S_H(X)}dX).

#### Normal-coordinate expansion

A standard Riemannian fact in geodesic normal coordinates at a point (p):
[
\sqrt{\det(g_{ij}(x))}
=1-\tfrac16,\mathrm{Ric}_{kl}(p),x^k x^l + O(|x|^3).
]
Hence, for (G) at (e),
[
J_G(X)=1-\tfrac16,\mathrm{Ric}_G(X,X) + O(|X|^3).
]
Taking (-\log) gives
[
S_H(X)=\tfrac16,\mathrm{Ric}_G(X,X)+O(|X|^3),
]
and differentiating twice at (0):
[
\nabla^2 S_H(0)(X,X)=\tfrac13,\mathrm{Ric}_G(X,X).
]
So if (\mathrm{Ric}_G\ge \kappa_G g_G), then
[
\nabla^2 S_H(0)\ \ge\ \tfrac13\kappa_G,\mathrm{Id}.
]

That (\tfrac13\kappa_G) is the “mass-like” quadratic convexity you’re harvesting.

#### Product Haar potential

For the lattice product manifold, near the vacuum (U^{(0)}) you write (U_\ell=\exp(X_\ell)). Then
[
d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}(U)=\prod_{\ell}J_G(X_\ell),dX
=\exp!\Big(-\sum_{\ell}S_H(X_\ell)\Big),dX.
]
Define
[
S_{H,\Lambda}(X):=\sum_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)} S_H(X_\ell).
]
Its Hessian at (0) is block-diagonal with the same (\tfrac13\kappa_G) bound per block, hence again **uniform in (\Lambda)**.

#### Conceptual warning (important)

This quadratic term is **not** a gauge-fixing Proca mass you inserted by hand. It’s the Jacobian from using invariant measure in non-flat coordinates—so it preserves gauge invariance rather than breaking it.

---

### Tiny “why we care” punchline

This entire §10.2 is the geometric reason you later get to say:

* “Even before I touch the action, the configuration manifold already has a built-in positive curvature contribution (Haar/Ricci).”
* “So as long as the action’s Hessian isn’t too negative, the Bakry–Émery tensor stays positive—at least locally near the vacuum—and that feeds directly into local CD((\rho,\infty)) and then into local functional inequalities.”

On to §10.3, the gauge-orbit/horizontal machinery, where the Wilson Hessian’s kernel starts behaving like a mischievous imp.




## 10.3 Gauge orbits, horizontal subspaces, and the Wilson Hessian at the vacuum 

This section explains why the curvature estimate in Part 10 is fundamentally **horizontal**: gauge-invariant observables “live” in directions orthogonal to gauge orbits, and the Wilson action’s quadratic form has a large kernel tied to gauge freedom (and, on a torus, topology).

We re-derive:

1. the vertical/horizontal splitting from the gauge action;
2. the discrete cochain model at the vacuum;
3. the quadratic expansion (Hessian) of the Wilson action and its relation to discrete Hodge operators.

---

### 10.3.1 Gauge group action and vertical tangent space

The lattice gauge group is
[
\mathcal G_\Lambda := G^{V(\Lambda)}.
]
For (g=(g_x)*{x\in V(\Lambda)}\in\mathcal G*\Lambda) and (U=(U_{x\to y})*{(x\to y)\in E(\Lambda)}\in M*\Lambda), the standard endpoint-conjugation action is
[
(g\cdot U)*{x\to y} := g_x,U*{x\to y},g_y^{-1}.
]

**Isometry.** Because the metric (g_G) on (G) is **bi-invariant**, both left and right multiplications are isometries of ((G,g_G)). Hence each coordinate map
[
U_{x\to y}\mapsto g_x U_{x\to y} g_y^{-1}
]
is an isometry, and so the product action is an isometry of ((M_\Lambda,g_\Lambda)). In particular it preserves (d\mathrm{vol}*{g*\Lambda}) (product Haar/Riemannian volume).

---

### 10.3.2 Infinitesimal gauge action and horizontal gradients for gauge-invariant observables

Let (\xi=(\xi_x)*{x\in V(\Lambda)}\in\mathfrak g^{V(\Lambda)}). Consider the one-parameter gauge transformation (g(t)\in\mathcal G*\Lambda) with
[
g_x(t)=\exp(t\xi_x).
]
Define the **infinitesimal gauge vector field** at configuration (U) by
[
\mathcal V_\xi(U):=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|*{t=0}(g(t)\cdot U)\in T_U M*\Lambda.
]

On a single link (\ell=(x\to y)), differentiating gives
[
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|*{0}\Big(\exp(t\xi_x),U*\ell,\exp(-t\xi_y)\Big)
= (\xi_x)^L(U_\ell)-(\xi_y)^R(U_\ell),
]
where ((\xi)^L,(\xi)^R) are the left-/right-invariant vector fields generated by (\xi).

Define the **vertical subspace**
[
\mathrm{Vert}*U:={\mathcal V*\xi(U):\xi\in\mathfrak g^{V(\Lambda)}}\subset T_U M_\Lambda,
]
and the **horizontal subspace** as the orthogonal complement
[
\mathrm{Hor}_U:=\mathrm{Vert}*U^\perp
\qquad(\text{orthogonal w.r.t. }g*\Lambda).
]

**Lemma (gauge invariance (\Rightarrow) horizontal gradient).**
If (f\in C^1(M_\Lambda)) is gauge-invariant, (f(g\cdot U)=f(U)) for all (g\in\mathcal G_\Lambda), then
[
\nabla f(U)\in \mathrm{Hor}_U\quad\text{for every }U.
]

*Proof.* Gauge invariance implies
[
0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|*{0} f(g(t)\cdot U)=df(U)[\mathcal V*\xi(U)].
]
But (df(U)[w]=g_\Lambda(\nabla f(U),w)), so (g_\Lambda(\nabla f(U),\mathcal V_\xi(U))=0) for all (\xi), i.e. (\nabla f(U)\perp \mathrm{Vert}_U). ∎

This is why all curvature/functional-inequality statements ultimately need to be interpreted on horizontals (or, equivalently, on the quotient (M_\Lambda/\mathcal G_\Lambda), if one develops that calculus).

---

### 10.3.3 Tangent identification at the vacuum and discrete cochains

Let (U^{(0)}\in M_\Lambda) be the trivial configuration (U^{(0)}_\ell=e) for all links (\ell).

At (U^{(0)}), using translation to identify (T_eG\cong\mathfrak g), we have
[
T_{U^{(0)}} M_\Lambda \cong \bigoplus_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)} \mathfrak g
=: \mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g),
]
the space of (\mathfrak g)-valued 1-cochains.

Equip cochains with the product inner products
[
\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathcal C^1}:=\sum_{\ell\in E(\Lambda)}\langle X_\ell,Y_\ell\rangle_G,\qquad
\langle F,G\rangle_{\mathcal C^2}:=\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\langle F_p,G_p\rangle_G,
]
etc.

---

### 10.3.4 Discrete exterior derivative (d_1) and linearized curvature

Define the coboundary (discrete exterior derivative)
[
d_1:\mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)\to \mathcal C^2(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)
]
by oriented summation around plaquettes. For a plaquette (p=(x;\mu,\nu)) with standard orientation,
[
(d_1X)*p := X*{x,\mu}+X_{x+\hat\mu,\nu}-X_{x+\hat\nu,\mu}-X_{x,\nu}.
]

Now parameterize configurations near the vacuum by exponential coordinates:
[
U_\ell=\exp_G(X_\ell)\qquad (X_\ell \text{ small}).
]
The plaquette holonomy is an ordered product of these exponentials, and a Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion yields the key linearization:
[
U_p(U)=\exp_G\big((d_1X)_p+\mathcal O(|X|^2)\big),
]
uniformly for plaquettes in a sufficiently small neighborhood of (X=0).

This is the lattice analogue of (F=dA+A\wedge A): at quadratic order, the leading term is the “curl” (d_1X).

---

### 10.3.5 Quadratic expansion of the Wilson action and its Hessian

Take the Wilson plaquette action (standard normalization)
[
S_W(U)=\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)} \mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Tr}(I-U_p(U)),
\qquad \beta=\frac{2N}{g_0^2}.
]

Expanding (S_W(\exp(X))) around (X=0), using:

1. (U_p=\exp((d_1X)_p+\mathcal O(|X|^2))), and
2. Taylor expansion of the class function (U\mapsto \mathrm{Re,Tr}(I-U)) near (e),

one obtains
[
S_W(U)=S_W(U^{(0)})+S_W^{(2)}(X)+\mathcal O(|X|^3),
]
where the quadratic term has the form
[
S_W^{(2)}(X)=\frac{\beta,c_{HS}}{N}\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)} |(d_1X)*p|*G^2
=\frac{\beta,c*{HS}}{N},|d_1X|*{\mathcal C^2}^2,
]
for some constant (c_{HS}>0) depending only on the normalization conventions.

Hence the Hessian at the vacuum (as an operator on (\mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g))) is
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)})=2,\frac{\beta,c_{HS}}{N},d_1^*d_1,
]
and in particular
[
\nabla^2 S_W(U^{(0)})(X,X)=2,\frac{\beta,c_{HS}}{N},|d_1X|_{\mathcal C^2}^2\ge 0.
]

So the Wilson action is **positive semidefinite to second order** at the vacuum.

---

### 10.3.6 Kernel structure and discrete Hodge decomposition at the vacuum

Because (d_1^*d_1) is positive semidefinite, its kernel is
[
\ker(d_1^*d_1)=\ker(d_1),
]
the closed 1-cochains (zero discrete curl). This kernel includes:

* **Exact 1-forms** (pure gauge directions): (\mathrm{im}(d_0)), coming from infinitesimal gauge transformations.
* **Harmonic 1-forms**, depending on topology (e.g. on a torus): (\ker(d_1)\cap \ker(d_0^*)).

Define
[
d_0:\mathcal C^0(\Lambda;\mathfrak g)\to\mathcal C^1(\Lambda;\mathfrak g),
\qquad (d_0\phi)_{x\to y}:=\phi_y-\phi_x,
]
and its adjoint (d_0^*) (negative divergence under the counting/product inner products). The 1-cochain Hodge Laplacian is
[
\Delta_1:=d_0d_0^*+d_1^*d_1.
]
Finite-dimensional Hodge theory (pure linear algebra) gives the orthogonal decomposition
[
\mathcal C^1
=\mathrm{im}(d_0)\ \oplus\ \ker(\Delta_1)\ \oplus\ \mathrm{im}(d_1^*),
\qquad
\ker(\Delta_1)=\ker(d_0^*)\cap\ker(d_1).
]

Now relate this to vertical/horizontal at the vacuum:

* At (U^{(0)}), the infinitesimal gauge action is exactly (d_0\phi), so
  [
  \mathrm{Vert}_{U^{(0)}}=\mathrm{im}(d_0).
  ]
* Therefore
  [
  \mathrm{Hor}_{U^{(0)}}=(\mathrm{im}(d_0))^\perp=\ker(d_0^*)
  =\ker(\Delta_1)\ \oplus\ \mathrm{im}(d_1^*).
  ]

So: on horizontal directions at the vacuum, the Wilson Hessian is nonnegative, and it vanishes precisely on the harmonic 1-forms (which exist only if the lattice topology allows them).

---

That’s the whole 10.3 block. The next step (10.4) is where this structure is used to give a clean lower bound for (\nabla^2 S_\Lambda) on horizontals at (U^{(0)}), which then plugs into the Bakry–Émery tensor estimate.



### 10.4 Euclidean-time decay and the fixed-cutoff OS mass parameter (bridge to Part 11)

Section 10.1–10.3 establishes an **exponential clustering bound at fixed lattice spacing** of the schematic form
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{\Lambda,a}}(F,G)\big|
\ \le\
C(F,G),e^{-\eta(a),\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}F,\mathrm{supp}G)}
\ +\ \text{(localization error)},
]
with constants uniform in the volume and a distance exponent (\eta(a)>0) coming from the massive-Maxwell inverse (Part 9).  

The purpose of this subsection is to record the **standard conversion of Euclidean-time exponential decay into a Hamiltonian mass gap**, in the fixed-cutoff (finite lattice spacing) setting. This conversion is logically simple—spectral theorem plus OS reconstruction—but it is the step that translates a geometric/functional-inequality estimate into a physical “mass parameter.” It is also exactly the interface later used in Part 11 (thermodynamic limit + OS permanence). 

---

#### 10.4.1 From link-distance clustering to Euclidean-time exponential decay

Fix a distinguished Euclidean time direction on the periodic lattice (\Lambda), with lattice spacing (a>0). Let (\tau_{na}) denote translation by (n\in\mathbb N) time steps (so physical time separation is (t=na)). Let (F,G) be local observables supported in time slabs of uniformly bounded thickness (independent of (\Lambda)).

Because the interaction range of the relevant “massive Maxwell” operator is finite in the link-adjacency metric, the link-distance separation of supports grows linearly with time translation: there exist constants (c_0\ge 0) and (c_1>0), depending only on the slab thickness conventions (not on (\Lambda)), such that
[
\mathrm{dist}\big(\mathrm{supp}_E(F),\mathrm{supp}*E(\tau*{na}G)\big)
\ \ge\
c_1 n - c_0.
\tag{10.19}
]
(In the cleanest convention one may take (c_1=1); an additive shift (c_0) is harmless since it only modifies the prefactor.)

Inserting (10.19) into the clustering bound gives
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{\Lambda,a}}(F,\tau_{na}G)\big|
\ \le\
C(F,G),e^{\eta(a)c_0},e^{-\eta(a)c_1,n}
\ +\ \text{(localization error)}.
\tag{10.20}
]
Equivalently, in physical time (t=na),
[
\big|\mathrm{Cov}*{\mu*{\Lambda,a}}(F,\tau_{t}G)\big|
\ \le\
C'(F,G),\exp!\Big(-m_{\mathrm{Euc}}(a),t\Big)
\ +\ \text{(localization error)},
\tag{10.21}
]
where we define the **Euclidean-time decay rate**
[
m_{\mathrm{Euc}}(a)\ :=\ \frac{\eta(a)c_1}{a}.
\tag{10.22}
]
This is the “mass parameter” that will later be compared to the OS Hamiltonian gap.

---

#### 10.4.2 OS reconstruction input and the transfer-semigroup representation

We now invoke the OS reconstruction framework set up earlier (reflection positivity + time translation structure). Concretely, OS reconstruction provides:

* a Hilbert space (\mathcal H_\Lambda),
* a cyclic vacuum vector (\Omega_\Lambda),
* a self-adjoint Hamiltonian (H_\Lambda\ge 0),
* and a map (F\mapsto [F]\in\mathcal H_\Lambda) from positive-time observables,

such that the Euclidean two-point function along time translations is represented by the semigroup (e^{-tH_\Lambda}) (for (t\ge 0)):

#### 10.4.3 Spectral-theorem lemma: Euclidean exponential decay implies a Hamiltonian gap

We state the standard lemma in a form tailored to our application.

**Lemma 10.4 (Euclidean exponential decay implies an OS Hamiltonian gap).**
Assume OS reconstruction holds so that (10.23) is available. Suppose there exists (m>0) such that for every positive-time local observable (F) with (\mu_{\Lambda,a}(F)=0),
[
\Big|\mathbb E_{\mu_{\Lambda,a}}\big[\theta F\cdot\tau_t F\big]\Big|
\ \le\
C(F),e^{-mt}
\qquad\forall t\ge 0.
\tag{10.24}
]
Then the spectrum of (H_\Lambda) restricted to (\Omega_\Lambda^\perp) is contained in ([m,\infty)). Equivalently,
[
\mathrm{gap}(H_\Lambda)\ \ge\ m.
\tag{10.25}
]

*Proof.*
Let (\psi_F:=[F]\in\mathcal H_\Lambda). The condition (\mu_{\Lambda,a}(F)=0) implies (\psi_F\perp \Omega_\Lambda) (the vacuum component corresponds to the constant observable). By OS,
[
\langle \psi_F, e^{-tH_\Lambda}\psi_F\rangle
============================================

\mathbb E_{\mu_{\Lambda,a}}\big[\theta F\cdot\tau_t F\big],
]
so (10.24) gives
[
\langle \psi_F, e^{-tH_\Lambda}\psi_F\rangle \le C(F)e^{-mt}.
]
By the spectral theorem, (\langle \psi_F, e^{-tH_\Lambda}\psi_F\rangle) is the Laplace transform of the spectral measure (\nu_F) of (H_\Lambda) associated to (\psi_F):
[
\langle \psi_F, e^{-tH_\Lambda}\psi_F\rangle=\int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-t\lambda},d\nu_F(\lambda).
]
If (\nu_F) had any mass on ([0,m)), then the Laplace transform would decay strictly slower than (e^{-mt}), contradicting (10.24) for large (t). Hence (\mathrm{supp}(\nu_F)\subset[m,\infty)) for all such (\psi_F). Since OS-cylindrical vectors span a dense subspace of (\Omega_\Lambda^\perp), it follows that (\sigma(H_\Lambda)\cap(0,m)=\emptyset), i.e. (\mathrm{gap}(H_\Lambda)\ge m). (\square)

This is the “spectral theorem implication” flagged in the dependency ledger.
