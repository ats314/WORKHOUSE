Title: Mass Gap as Rigidity – Intuition That Survives Critique

This document summarizes the conceptual picture we distilled for the Yang–Mills mass gap
as a form of “rigidity” of the vacuum, in a way that remains compatible with real physics.


1. Mass Gap as No Cheap Gauge-Invariant Deformation

Consider the Hamiltonian H acting on the physical (gauge-invariant) Hilbert space H_phys.

- The vacuum |0⟩ is the lowest-energy state, with energy E_0.
- The mass gap is m = E_1 - E_0, where E_1 is the next energy level.

Conceptual rephrasing:

    “There is no sequence of non-vacuum, gauge-invariant states whose energy converges
     to E_0. All nontrivial physical excitations cost at least m > 0 in energy.”

In geometric terms:

- Think of the space of gauge-invariant states as a high-dimensional landscape,
- H defines an energy functional on that landscape,
- the mass gap means the vacuum is an isolated minimum separated from all other
  gauge-invariant states by a finite energy barrier.

This is a useful way to think about “rigidity” of the vacuum.


2. Gauss’s Law and Elimination of Soft Modes

Gauss’s law:

    G_x^a |ψ⟩ = 0

projects the full space of gauge-field configurations down to H_phys.

- Many candidate “soft” modes are pure gauge and thus unphysical.
- Physical excitations must be gauge-invariant combinations, e.g. loops, Wilson-loop operators,
  field-strength composites.

This is why:

- In U(1), Gauss’s law still allows transverse plane waves → massless photons.
- In SU(3), the combination of gauge invariance and self-interactions means the simplest
  excitations are extended, nonlocal objects (glueballs) with finite energy.


3. Non-Abelian Curvature as Source of Rigidity

The field strength in Yang–Mills is:

    F_{μν} = ∂_μ A_ν - ∂_ν A_μ + [A_μ, A_ν].

The commutator term [A, A] is what distinguishes non-Abelian from Abelian theory.

Intuitive consequences:

- The [A, A] term couples different modes and does not disappear at long wavelengths.
- A would-be “soft” deformation of A_μ generally produces nontrivial F_{μν}.
- Therefore, creating a nontrivial, gauge-invariant excitation typically implies finite curvature,
  which costs finite energy.

This is the physical sense in which “non-Abelian curvature makes the vacuum rigid” and supports
a mass gap and confinement.


4. Confinement as Frustration of Non-Abelian Constraints (Analogy)

With static color sources:

- Gauss’s law with nonzero charge forces chromoelectric flux out of the sources.
- In U(1), flux can spread, giving Coulomb 1/r potentials.
- In SU(3), flux lines tend to form tubes; the Wilson loop area law reflects that:

    ⟨W(C)⟩ ~ e^{-σ·Area(C)}.

Interpretable as:

- Plaquettes “want” minimal curvature,
- color sources enforce nontrivial holonomy around large loops,
- this “frustration” cannot be locally removed, so it extends as a flux tube,
- energy grows linearly with separation → confinement.

This is a good analogy to “frustrated constraints” in a discrete constraint system, but the
real object in YM is the Wilson loop, not a simple neighbor mismatch.


5. Rigidity Intuition vs. Rigorous Proof

The rigidity picture:

- “There are no arbitrarily cheap physical deformations of the SU(3) vacuum; all gauge-invariant
  excitations cost a finite amount of energy.”

is a compact, physically accurate intuition for the mass gap.

Turning this into a rigorous statement means:

- defining H precisely in the continuum,
- showing H has a strictly positive spectral gap on H_phys,
- establishing uniform coercivity bounds (spectral-gap inequalities) for lattice H(a),
  stable under the continuum/infinite-volume limits.

Thus, the intuition is compatible with real Yang–Mills; the missing piece is a nontrivial
operator-analytic argument, not a new physical mechanism.
