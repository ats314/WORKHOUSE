### 1. Geometry and Analysis Behind Lemma 1.1 and the Infinite Codimension Theorem

#### 1.1 Sobolev setting and continuity of $\xi$

Let $M$ be a compact smooth 4-manifold, $P \to M$ a principal $G$-bundle with compact Lie group $G$, and $\mathrm{ad}\,P$ the associated adjoint bundle.

For an integer $k \ge 0$ and a vector bundle $E \to M$, the Sobolev space $L_k^2(M,E)$ is defined as the completion of smooth sections $\Gamma^\infty(M,E)$ with respect to the norm
\[
\|s\|_{L^2_k}^2 \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^k \int_M |\nabla^j s(x)|^2\, d\mathrm{vol}_g(x),
\]
where $\nabla$ is a fixed background connection on $E$ and $g$ a fixed Riemannian metric on $M$.

**Sobolev embedding (vector-bundle version on 4-manifolds).**  
If $k > \frac{\dim M}{2} = 2$, then
\[
L^2_k(M,E) \hookrightarrow C^0(M,E)
\]
continuously. In particular, every $L^2_k$ section is represented (up to modification on a null set) by a continuous section.

Applied to $E = \mathrm{ad}\,P$, this gives:

- For $k > 2$, any $\xi \in L^2_k(M, \mathrm{ad}\,P)$ admits a continuous representative.
- The condition $\xi \neq 0$ in $L^2_k$ thus implies $\xi(x)\neq 0$ for some (indeed uncountably many) points $x \in M$.

This rigorously justifies Step 1 of Lemma 1.1:

> 1. Since $k>2$, $\xi$ is continuous. Since $\xi \neq 0$, there is a neighborhood $U$ where $\xi \neq 0$.

More precisely, there exists $x_0\in M$ with $\xi(x_0)\neq 0$; continuity yields an open neighborhood $U\ni x_0$ such that $\xi(x)\neq 0$ for all $x\in U$.

#### 1.2 Construction of infinitely many disjoint supports

Let $U$ be a nonempty open subset of a compact, second-countable manifold $M$ (second-countable follows from compactness plus metrizability). Then:

- There exists a sequence of pairwise disjoint, nonempty open subsets $U_n\subset U$, $n\in \mathbb{N}$.

A concrete construction: Choose a countable basis $\{V_j\}$ for the topology of $U$. Define inductively:

- $U_1 = V_{j_1}$ with any $V_{j_1} \subset U$.
- Given $U_1,\ldots,U_{n-1}$, pick $V_{j_n}$ disjoint from $\overline{U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_{n-1}}$ (possible since $U$ is open and contains points not in the union’s closure). Set $U_n = V_{j_n}$.

Thus, Step 2 of Lemma 1.1 is valid:

> 2. Construct an infinite sequence of disjoint open sets $U_n \subset U$.

#### 1.3 Choice of test forms $\alpha_n$

Let $E$ be the adjoint bundle or a tensor product with forms as needed (e.g. $\Lambda^1T^*M\otimes \mathrm{ad}\,P$ for connections). For each $U_n$:

- By standard partition of unity arguments, there exists a smooth compactly supported section $\alpha_n\in \Gamma_c^\infty(U_n,E)$ that is nonzero.

Since $\Gamma_c^\infty(U_n,E)$ is dense in $L^2_k(M,E)$ (smooth compactly supported sections are dense in Sobolev spaces on manifolds), each $\alpha_n$ is in $L^2_k(M,E)$.

Thus Step 3 is justified:

> 3. Choose test forms $\alpha_n$ supported in $U_n$.

#### 1.4 The commutator map $T_\xi$ and its boundedness

Consider the bundle $\mathrm{ad}\,P$ with fiber the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = \mathrm{Lie}(G)$. For a fixed continuous section $\xi\in \Gamma(M, \mathrm{ad}\,P)$, the pointwise Lie bracket defines a fiberwise linear map
\[
\mathrm{ad}_\xi(x): \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g},\quad Y \mapsto [Y,\xi(x)].
\]
Because $\xi$ is continuous and $G$ is compact (hence $\mathfrak{g}$ has a fixed bi-invariant inner product), the map $x\mapsto \mathrm{ad}_\xi(x)$ defines a bounded bundle endomorphism of $\mathrm{ad}\,P$:
\[
\|\mathrm{ad}_\xi(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le C_\xi < \infty \quad \text{for all } x\in M.
\]
The same holds for $\Lambda^1 T^*M \otimes \mathrm{ad}\,P$ pointwise.

Define
\[
T_\xi: L^2_k\big(M,\Lambda^1 T^*M\otimes\mathrm{ad}\,P\big) \to L^2_k\big(M,\Lambda^1 T^*M\otimes\mathrm{ad}\,P\big),
\quad a \mapsto [a,\xi],
\]
by pointwise bracket. Since we work on Sobolev order $k$, multiplication by a bounded continuous tensor field is bounded on $L^2_k$, so $T_\xi$ is a bounded linear operator on $H:=L^2_k$. (The CLEAN proof has $H' = L^2_{k-1}$; one can either treat $T_\xi$ as $H\to H$ or $H\to H'$—boundedness is easier $H\to H$; the infinite-rank argument works either way.)

Given a tensor of the form $\alpha\otimes Y$ (a $1$-form $\alpha$ times a constant Lie algebra element $Y$), we have pointwise
\[
T_\xi(\alpha\otimes Y)(x)
\;=\;
\alpha(x)\otimes [Y,\xi(x)].
\]

This matches Step 4’s expression:
\[
T_\xi(\alpha_n \otimes Y) = \alpha_n \otimes [Y, \xi].
\]

#### 1.5 Nontriviality of the images $T_\xi(\alpha_n\otimes Y)$

We must show that there exists a single $Y \in \mathfrak{g}$ such that for all $n$,
\[
T_\xi(\alpha_n\otimes Y) \neq 0 \quad \text{in } L^2_{k-1}(M, \Lambda^1 T^*M\otimes\mathrm{ad}\,P).
\]

Since $\xi(x)\neq 0$ for all $x\in U$, there exists some $x_0\in U$ with $\xi(x_0)\neq 0$. The map $\mathrm{ad}_{\xi(x_0)} : \mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}$ is nonzero unless $\xi(x_0)$ lies in the center of $\mathfrak{g}$. Since the context is Yang–Mills on a nonabelian group (e.g. $SU(N)$), it is natural to assume $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple or at least that $\xi$ is not everywhere central. Under this (standard) nonabelianity assumption:

- There exists $Y\in\mathfrak{g}$ such that $[Y,\xi(x_0)]\neq 0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$.

By continuity of $\xi$, the function $x\mapsto [Y,\xi(x)]$ is continuous; hence there is an open neighborhood $V\subset U$ of $x_0$ such that
\[
[Y,\xi(x)] \neq 0 \quad \text{for all } x\in V.
\]

We can—and will—choose the first open set $U_1$ in the sequence so that $U_1\subset V$. Then, since $\alpha_1$ is supported in $U_1$ and nonzero, there is a subset of $U_1$ with positive measure where $\alpha_1(x)\neq 0$. On that subset, we have
\[
(\alpha_1\otimes [Y,\xi])(x) \neq 0.
\]
Hence, $T_\xi(\alpha_1\otimes Y)$ is a nonzero element of $L^2_k$ (and thus nonzero in $L^2_{k-1}$ too).

For the subsequent $U_n$, we can ensure that each $U_n\subset V$ as well, by iteratively choosing them within $V$. Then, the same $Y$ works for all $n$, and each $T_\xi(\alpha_n\otimes Y)$ is nonzero.

#### 1.6 Orthogonality via disjoint supports

Let $E$ be a Hermitian vector bundle over $M$, and consider the real Hilbert space $L^2(M,E)$ with inner product
\[
\langle u,v\rangle_{L^2} = \int_M \langle u(x),v(x)\rangle_{E_x}\, d\mathrm{vol}_g(x).
\]
If $u,v$ have disjoint supports, then $u(x)v(x)=0$ a.e., so
\[
\langle u,v\rangle_{L^2} = 0.
\]

This property persists in Sobolev spaces $L^2_k$ (since they embed into $L^2$ for all $k\ge 0$; the inner product is given by a sum of $L^2$ inner products of derivatives, each having the same support property).

Given:

- $\mathrm{supp}(\alpha_n) \subset U_n$,
- $\{U_n\}$ are pairwise disjoint,

we obtain that the supports of $T_\xi(\alpha_n\otimes Y) = \alpha_n \otimes [Y,\xi]$ are contained in $U_n$, hence are pairwise disjoint. Therefore,
\[
\langle T_\xi(\alpha_m\otimes Y), T_\xi(\alpha_n\otimes Y)\rangle_{H'}
= 0 \quad \text{for } m\neq n
\]
where $H'$ is either $L^2_{k-1}$ or $L^2_k$ with the usual Sobolev inner product. This gives an infinite orthogonal sequence in the range of $T_\xi$.

Thus Step 4 of Lemma 1.1 is rigorously justified, including the orthogonality argument.

#### 1.7 Infinite-dimensional range and infinite codimension kernel

Since we have constructed infinitely many nonzero mutually orthogonal vectors in $\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)$, the range is infinite-dimensional as a Hilbert space.

To connect this with codimension, recall:

- For a linear map $T: V\to W$ between vector spaces, the quotient $V/\ker T$ is isomorphic (as a vector space) to $\mathrm{ran}(T)$ via the induced isomorphism.
- Thus:
  \[
  \dim_{\mathbb{R}}(V/\ker T) = \dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathrm{ran}(T)).
  \]
- The (algebraic) codimension of $\ker T$ in $V$ is defined as $\dim(V/\ker T)$.

For Hilbert spaces, one typically works with **Hamel dimension** (algebraic dimension). Since $\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)$ contains a countable orthogonal set of nonzero vectors, its Hamel dimension is **at least countably infinite**, and in fact uncountable if one completes to a basis. In any case:

- $\dim(\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)) = \infty$,
- hence $\mathrm{codim}(\ker T_\xi) = \infty$ in the operator-theoretic sense.

This legitimizes the claim in the main theorem:

> The linearization map $T_\xi(a) = [a, \xi]$ has infinite rank, implying the kernel (tangent space to reducibles) has infinite codimension.

Note that this is purely an algebraic statement about dimensions; no subtle topological notion of codimension (like Fredholm index) is being used here.

#### 1.8 Relationship to reducible connections and their tangent space

In gauge theory, a connection $A$ on $P$ is called **reducible** if the holonomy reduces to a proper subgroup of $G$, equivalently if there exists a nonzero parallel section $\xi\in \Gamma(M,\mathrm{ad}\,P)$ such that
\[
D_A \xi = 0,
\]
where $D_A$ is the covariant derivative induced by $A$ on $\mathrm{ad}\,P$.

The **linearization** of the equation $D_A\xi = 0$ with respect to variations of $A$ in the direction $a\in T_A\mathcal{A}$ (where $\mathcal{A}$ is the space of connections modeled on $H=L^2_k(M,\Lambda^1\otimes\mathrm{ad}\,P)$) gives:
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} D_{A+ta}(\xi) = [a,\xi].
\]

Thus, infinitesimally, the condition of preserving parallelism of a fixed nonzero $\xi$ under perturbations $a$ is exactly
\[
T_\xi(a) = [a,\xi] = 0.
\]

Hence:

- The **tangent space** of the “stratum of connections for which this particular $\xi$ is parallel” at a point $A$ is $\ker T_\xi$.
- The theorem above shows that, for any nonzero parallel $\xi$, this tangent space has infinite codimension in $T_A\mathcal{A}$.

This is the geometric content of the **Infinite Codimension of Reducible Stratum** theorem: locally, the reducible locus is “infinitely thin” in the configuration space of connections.

Up to this point, the arguments of P18 are fully compatible with standard functional analysis and gauge theory, and are mathematically solid once one formalizes the Sobolev and linear-operator aspects as above.

#### 2.1 Gaussian measures on Hilbert spaces and the space of connections

Let $\mathcal{A}$ denote the affine space of $L^2_k$ connections on $P\to M$:
\[
\mathcal{A} = A_0 + H, \quad H := L^2_k(M,\Lambda^1 T^*M\otimes\mathrm{ad}\,P),
\]
for a fixed reference connection $A_0$.

A **centered Gaussian measure** $\mu_0$ on $\mathcal{A}$ is specified (up to equivalence) by a positive, symmetric, trace-class operator $Q : H\to H$ (the covariance) via:
- For any continuous linear functional $\ell$ on $H$,
  \[
  \ell_* \mu_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_\ell), \quad \sigma^2_\ell = \langle Q \ell^*, \ell^* \rangle_H,
  \]
  where $\ell^*\in H$ is the Riesz representer of $\ell$.

Heuristically, one has a **Gaussian random connection**
\[
A(\omega) = A_0 + \eta(\omega), \quad \eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,Q),
\]
with $\eta\in H$ a Gaussian random field of 1-forms with values in $\mathrm{ad}\,P$.

#### 2.2 The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Dirichlet form and capacity

Let $(E,H,\mu)$ be an abstract Wiener space; here we take $E=\mathcal{A}$ (affine Hilbert), $H$ its associated Cameron–Martin space (isomorphic to the modeling Hilbert space $H$ above), and $\mu=\mu_0$.

The **Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Dirichlet form** $(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))$ on $L^2(E,\mu)$ is given (on a core of smooth cylindrical functions) by
\[
\mathcal{E}(f,g)
= \frac12 \int_E \langle D_H f(x), D_H g(x)\rangle_H\, d\mu(x),
\]
where $D_H$ is the Malliavin (Cameron–Martin) derivative. Its domain $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})$ is the closure of smooth cylindrical functionals under the norm
\[
\|f\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})}^2
:= \mathcal{E}(f,f) + \|f\|_{L^2(E,\mu)}^2.
\]

This is a symmetric, strongly local Dirichlet form. The associated Markov process $(X_t)_{t\ge 0}$ is the **OU process** on $E$ with invariant measure $\mu$.

Given a Dirichlet form, the **1-capacity** $\mathrm{Cap}(A)$ of a Borel set $A\subset E$ is defined by
\[
\mathrm{Cap}(A)
= \inf \left\{
\mathcal{E}_1(u,u) \;:\;
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}),
\ u\ge 1\ \mu\text{-a.e. on an open neighborhood of } A
\right\},
\]
where $\mathcal{E}_1(u,u) := \mathcal{E}(u,u)+\|u\|_{L^2}^2$.

A set $N$ is called **polar** if
\[
\mathrm{Cap}(N) = 0.
\]

It is a general theorem of Dirichlet form theory that:

> A Borel (or even analytic) set $N$ is polar if and only if, for any starting point $x$ outside a $\mathrm{Cap}$-null set, the associated Markov process almost surely **never hits** $N$.

More precisely, if $X_t$ is the OU process with Dirichlet form $\mathcal{E}$, then, for quasi-every $x$ (i.e. outside a polar set),
\[
\mathbb{P}^x\left( \exists t\ge 0: X_t \in N\right) = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{Cap}(N) = 0.
\]

Thus, the polarity of $\Sigma$ would mean:

- With $\mu_0$-probability 1 (and even quasi-every starting connection), the OU process on $\mathcal{A}$ avoids reducible connections at all times.

This is the probabilistic interpretation underlying Theorem 2.1 and its “Brownian motion analogy”.

#### 2.3 Finite-dimensional Brownian motion and subspaces

The proof sketch in P18 invokes:

> Brownian motion in $\mathbb{R}^n$ avoids subspaces of codimension $\ge 3$.

This is a heuristic distilled from classical potential theory. The precise classical facts are:

- In $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $n\ge 3$, a **single point** has **zero** Newtonian capacity and is polar for Brownian motion.
- For a $C^1$ submanifold $S\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ of dimension $d$, its polar behavior depends on $n-d$ (the codimension) and more refined capacity notions:
  - Roughly, if $d\le n-3$ (codimension $\ge 3$), then $S$ is polar.
  - Hypersurfaces ($d=n-1$, codimension 1) are **not** polar: Brownian motion hits them with positive probability.
  - The borderline $d=n-2$ (codimension 2) is more subtle and involves fine potential theoretic characterizations (Wiener criterion). Some codimension-2 sets can be nonpolar, e.g. lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ have positive capacity for Brownian motion.

For **linear subspaces** $L\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ of dimension $d$:

- If $\dim L \le n-3$ (that is, $\mathrm{codim}\,L\ge 3$), then $L$ is polar for $n\ge 3$ Brownian motion.
- If $\dim L \ge n-2$, polar/nonpolar properties depend on details, but generally:
  - Hyperplanes (codim 1) are not polar.
  - Certain codim-2 subspaces are not polar.

So the statement in the CLEAN proof is aligned with the **safe side** of classical results: subspaces of codimension $\ge 3$ are definitely polar.

The infinite-dimensional intuition is then:

- If you require infinitely many independent linear constraints (so “codimension $\infty$”), the set is “thinner than any finite codimension set,” hence should be polar for a “Brownian-like” process on an infinite-dimensional space.

The infinite-dimensional analogue for OU processes on Gaussian Hilbert spaces is more subtle and not usually phrased exactly in terms of “codimension” but rather in terms of the structure of the **Cameron–Martin space** and differentiability in those directions.

#### 2.4 Infinite-dimensional OU and polar sets: conceptual picture

In a separable Hilbert space $H$ with nondegenerate Gaussian measure $\mu_0$, the OU process $(X_t)$ satisfies a stochastic differential equation of the form
\[
dX_t = -X_t\,dt + dW_t,
\]
where $W_t$ is an $H$-valued cylindrical Brownian motion (Wiener process) adapted to $\mu_0$’s covariance structure.

Key points:

1. **Finite-dimensional projections.**  
   For any finite-rank orthogonal projection $\pi_N : H\to H_N\cong\mathbb{R}^N$, the projected process
   \[
   Y_t^N := \pi_N X_t
   \]
   is an OU process on $\mathbb{R}^N$ with invariant Gaussian measure $\pi_{N*}\mu_0$. Polar sets for $Y^N$ can be analyzed using classical finite-dimensional potential theory.

2. **Product structure of the law.**  
   In an orthonormal basis adapted to the covariance, $\mu_0$ often factorizes as a product of one-dimensional Gaussians (technically, after an isomorphism to an abstract Wiener space). The OU process then decomposes into countably many coupled 1D OU components, much like infinite product Brownian motion.

3. **Singletons (points) in infinite dimensions.**  
   In infinite-dimensional Gaussian spaces, **singletons** (or, more generally, finite-dimensional affine subspaces) are typically polar for the OU process. Intuitively, because the process has “too many directions” to coordinate a hit on a lower-dimensional set. Technically, the capacity of a point in infinite-dimension Gaussian space is zero: there is no nonconstant function of finite energy that is equal to 1 in a neighborhood of that single point, because any such function must depend on infinitely many coordinates, incurring infinite energy.

4. **Affine subspaces of infinite codimension.**  
   For a closed linear subspace $F\subset H$, we can split $H=F\oplus F^\perp$. If $\dim F^\perp =\infty$, then the OU motion in the orthogonal component $F^\perp$ is infinite-dimensional, and the set $F$ is a kind of “product” of $F$ with zero in $F^\perp$:
   \[
   F = F \times \{0\} \subset F\oplus F^\perp.
   \]
   The event “$X_t\in F$ for some $t$” forces the $F^\perp$ component to hit zero at some time, a zero-measure, and (usually) polar event in infinite dimension.

From this heuristic one extrapolates the statement in P18:

> Using the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, any subspace of infinite codimension is polar (capacity zero).

However, to turn this into a theorem, one needs:

- A precise statement about capacity of **closed affine subspaces of infinite codimension** in an abstract Wiener space under the OU Dirichlet form.
- Either an explicit capacity computation or a reference to a theorem of the form “all proper closed affine subspaces of infinite codimension are polar.”

Such theorems exist in pieces in the Dirichlet forms and Gaussian potential theory literature, but no explicit reference is attached in P18, and no GPT_MASTERS files were provided to anchor it. Thus, this step remains **heuristic** within the current synthesis.

#### 2.5 Reducible stratum as union of infinite-codimension affine subspaces

The final step in Theorem 2.1 is:

> By Lemma 1.1, $\Sigma$ is a union of infinite-codimension affine subspaces. Thus $\mathrm{Cap}_{\mu_0}(\Sigma) = 0$.

We must unpack and scrutinize two assertions:

1. **Structural assertion**:  
   “$\Sigma$ is a union of infinite-codimension affine subspaces.”

2. **Capacity assertion**:  
   “A union of such subspaces is polar.”

##### 2.5.1 Structure of reducible connections

Let’s fix a nonzero section $\xi\in L^2_k(M,\mathrm{ad}\,P)$ (or smoother) and consider the set
\[
\Sigma_\xi := \{A\in \mathcal{A} : D_A \xi = 0\}.
\]

The equation
\[
D_A\xi = 0
\]
is **affine-linear in $A$** provided $\xi$ is held fixed. If $A = A_0 + a$ for a fixed base $A_0$ and $a\in H$, then
\[
D_A\xi
= D_{A_0+a}\xi
= D_{A_0}\xi + [a,\xi].
\]

Given that $D_{A_0}\xi$ is fixed, $\Sigma_\xi$ is the solution set to the affine-linear equation
\[
[a,\xi] = -D_{A_0}\xi.
\]

This is either empty, or an **affine subspace** of $H$ parallel to $\ker T_\xi$:

- If there exists a particular $a_* \in H$ with $[a_*,\xi] = -D_{A_0}\xi$, then
  \[
  \Sigma_\xi = \{A_0 + a_* + h : h\in \ker T_\xi\}.
  \]
- If no such $a_*$ exists, $\Sigma_\xi$ is empty.

At a connection $A$ where $D_A\xi = 0$, we have $D_{A_0}\xi = -[a,\xi]$ and $a=a_*$; thus near $A$, the set $\Sigma_\xi$ is indeed locally an affine subspace parallel to $\ker T_\xi$.

The global reducible locus is
\[
\Sigma
:= \{A\in\mathcal{A} : \exists\, \xi\in L^2_k(M,\mathrm{ad}\,P)\setminus\{0\} \text{ such that }D_A\xi =0\}
= \bigcup_{\xi\neq 0} \Sigma_\xi.
\]

Each nonempty $\Sigma_\xi$ is an affine subspace of $\mathcal{A}=A_0 + H$, with direction $\ker T_\xi$. By Lemma 1.1 and the Infinite Codimension Theorem, we know:

- For each $\xi\neq 0$ that is parallel at some $A\in \Sigma_\xi$, we have:
  \[
  \mathrm{codim}_H(\ker T_\xi) = \infty.
  \]
  Therefore each nonempty $\Sigma_\xi$ is an affine subspace of **infinite codimension** in $H$.

Thus the **structural assertion** in P18 is essentially correct:

> $\Sigma$ is a union of infinite-codimension affine subspaces.

The one technical caveat is that the union is **uncountable**: $\xi$ ranges over an infinite-dimensional (uncountable) space of parallel sections. This becomes significant when we reason about capacity.

##### 2.5.2 Capacity of unions: countability issues

Capacity is **countably subadditive**:
\[
\mathrm{Cap}\Big(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\Big)
\;\le\;
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathrm{Cap}(A_n).
\]

Crucially, there is **no guarantee** that an uncountable union of polar sets is polar. For example, in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with Brownian motion:

- Each point $\{x\}$ is polar (capacity zero).
- But an uncountable union of points can form a set of positive capacity (indeed, any nonempty open set is an uncountable union of points and is not polar).

Thus, even if one had proved:

- For each nonempty $\Sigma_\xi$, $\mathrm{Cap}(\Sigma_\xi) = 0$,

it does **not** follow automatically that
\[
\mathrm{Cap}\Big(\bigcup_{\xi\neq 0} \Sigma_\xi\Big) = 0.
\]

So there is a genuine subtlety in the step:

> “By Lemma 1.1, $\Sigma$ is a union of infinite-codimension affine subspaces. Thus $\text{Cap}_{\mu_0}(\Sigma) = 0$.”

To justify this implication rigorously, one would need **additional structure**, e.g.:

- Show that $\Sigma$ can be represented as a **countable union** of polar sets (e.g., by parameterizing $\xi$ in a countable family dense enough such that any reducible connection lies in some $\Sigma_{\xi_j}$).
- Or show that $\Sigma$ lies inside a **single** affine infinite-codimension subspace (unlikely) or a countable union thereof induced by gauge-equivalence classes and topological decompositions.

Without such an argument, the final step in Theorem 2.1 is **incomplete**.

#### 3.1 Infinite Rank Lemma (rigorous)

**Lemma 1.1 (Detailed).**  
Let $M$ be a compact smooth 4-manifold, $E$ a Hermitian vector bundle over $M$, and $k>2$. Let $H=L^2_k(M,E)$ and $H'=L^2_{k-1}(M,E)$. Let $\xi\in L^2_k(M,\mathrm{End}(E))$ be nonzero and continuous (by Sobolev embedding). Define
\[
T_\xi: H \to H', \quad a \mapsto [a,\xi],
\]
where $[a,\xi](x) = [a(x),\xi(x)]$ is the fiberwise commutator. Then $\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)$ is infinite-dimensional.

*Proof.*

1. **Continuity and nonvanishing of $\xi$.**  
   By the Sobolev embedding theorem on a 4-manifold, $k>2$ implies $L^2_k\hookrightarrow C^0$, so $\xi$ has a continuous representative. As $\xi\neq 0$ in $L^2_k$, there exists $x_0\in M$ with $\xi(x_0)\neq 0$. By continuity, there is an open neighborhood $U\subset M$ such that
   \[
   \xi(x) \neq 0 \quad \forall x\in U.
   \]

2. **Choice of a noncommuting Lie algebra element.**  
   At the point $x_0$, the endomorphism $\xi(x_0) \in \mathrm{End}(E_{x_0})$ is nonzero. Assuming $E$ arises from an adjoint representation of a nonabelian compact Lie group (as in Yang–Mills), there exists $Y\in \mathrm{End}(E_{x_0})$ such that
   \[
   [Y,\xi(x_0)] \neq 0.
   \]
   Locally, we can extend $Y$ to a constant element in the fiber representation (e.g. using a trivialization over $U$) so that $Y$ is a fixed element of the Lie algebra representation and
   \[
   [Y,\xi](x_0) \neq 0, \quad \text{and by continuity again, } [Y,\xi](x)\neq 0 \text{ for all }x\in U'.
   \]
   after possibly shrinking $U$ to a smaller open $U'\subset U$ (and renaming it $U$).

3. **Construction of infinitely many disjoint open subsets.**  
   As $U$ is nonempty open in a compact manifold, we can construct a countable sequence $\{U_n\}_{n\ge 1}$ of pairwise disjoint, nonempty open subsets contained in $U$, using standard topology (second countability and metric separability).

4. **Construction of nonzero compactly supported test sections.**  
   For each $n$, choose a nonzero smooth section
   \[
   \alpha_n \in \Gamma_c^\infty(U_n,E),
   \]
   so that $\alpha_n(x)\neq 0$ on some open subset of $U_n$. Each $\alpha_n$ belongs to $H=L^2_k(M,E)$, with
   \[
   \mathrm{supp}(\alpha_n) \subset U_n.
   \]

5. **Action of $T_\xi$ on simple tensors.**  
   Consider $a_n := \alpha_n \otimes Y$ (interpreting $Y$ as an endomorphism acting on $E$; in gauge-theory notation, you think of $Y$ as a constant Lie algebra element and $\alpha_n$ as a 1-form). Then
   \[
   T_\xi(a_n)(x)
   = [\alpha_n(x)\otimes Y,\xi(x)]
   = \alpha_n(x)\otimes [Y,\xi(x)],
   \]
   for all $x\in M$.

   On $U_n\subset U$, we have $[Y,\xi(x)]\neq 0$ and, on the support of $\alpha_n$, $\alpha_n(x)\neq 0$. Therefore, on a subset of positive measure in $U_n$, we have
   \[
   T_\xi(a_n)(x) \neq 0.
   \]
   Consequently, $T_\xi(a_n)\neq 0$ in $H'$.

6. **Orthogonality of the images.**  
   Since $\mathrm{supp}(\alpha_m)\subset U_m$ and $\{U_m\}$ are pairwise disjoint, the supports of $T_\xi(a_m)$ are pairwise disjoint. For $m\neq n$,
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   \langle T_\xi(a_m), T_\xi(a_n)\rangle_{H'}
   &= \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \int_M \left\langle \nabla^j T_\xi(a_m)(x), \nabla^j T_\xi(a_n)(x)\right\rangle \, d\mathrm{vol}_g(x) \\
   &= 0,
   \end{aligned}
   \]
   because the integrand is zero everywhere (the integrand is supported in $U_m\cap U_n = \emptyset$). Hence $\{T_\xi(a_n)\}_{n\ge1}$ is a sequence of pairwise orthogonal nonzero vectors in $\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)$, and therefore $\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)$ is infinite-dimensional.

$\square$

This fully justifies Lemma 1.1 in a standard Hilbert–Sobolev and gauge-theoretic context.

#### 3.2 Infinite codimension of $\ker T_\xi$ (rigorous)

**Theorem (Infinite Codimension of Reducible Stratum, detailed algebraic version).**  
Let $H$ and $T_\xi$ be as in Lemma 1.1. Then:

1. $\dim(\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)) = \infty$.
2. Consequently, the algebraic codimension of $\ker T_\xi$ in $H$,
   \[
   \mathrm{codim}(\ker T_\xi)
   := \dim\big(H/\ker T_\xi\big),
   \]
   is infinite.

Moreover, in the context where $T_\xi$ is the linearization of the parallelism equation $D_A\xi =0$ at a reducible connection $A$, the tangent space to the reducible stratum (for a fixed $\xi$) at $A$ is $\ker T_\xi$ and thus is an infinite-codimension subspace of $T_A\mathcal{A}$.

*Proof.*  

1. Part (1) follows immediately from Lemma 1.1.
2. For any linear map $T: V\to W$, we have an isomorphism $V/\ker T \cong \mathrm{ran}(T)$ defined by $[v] \mapsto T(v)$. Thus:
   \[
   \dim(V/\ker T) = \dim(\mathrm{ran}(T)).
   \]
   Apply this to $V=H$ and $T=T_\xi$ to obtain:
   \[
   \mathrm{codim}(\ker T_\xi) = \dim(\mathrm{ran}(T_\xi)) = \infty.
   \]

The geometric interpretation in terms of reducible connections has already been explained in Section 1.8. $\square$

This part of P18 is entirely rigorous given the Hilbert-space setup and the meaning of “codimension” as algebraic dimension of the quotient.

#### 3.3 Gaussian polarity of infinite-codimension affine subspaces (partial rigor, remaining conjectural elements)

We now tackle the core analytic–probabilistic claim behind Theorem 2.1:

> Using the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, any subspace of infinite codimension is polar (capacity zero).

This is not explicitly proven in P18 and no external references are provided, so we need to outline how such a result could be shown and where the gaps lie.

Let $H$ be a separable Hilbert space, $(E,H,\mu_0)$ an abstract Wiener space with Gaussian measure $\mu_0$, and let $(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))$ be the canonical OU Dirichlet form on $L^2(E,\mu_0)$.

Let $F\subset H$ be a **closed linear subspace** of infinite codimension, meaning:
\[
H = F \oplus F^\perp, \quad \dim(F^\perp) = \infty.
\]

Consider the affine subspace $x_0 + F$ for some fixed $x_0\in H$.

We are interested in the capacity $\mathrm{Cap}(x_0+F)$ under $\mathcal{E}$.

**Heuristic argument.**

1. **Decomposition of process.**  
   The OU process $(X_t)_{t\ge 0}$ on $H$ can be decomposed as
   \[
   X_t = X_t^F + X_t^{F^\perp}, \quad X_t^F\in F,\ X_t^{F^\perp}\in F^\perp,
   \]
   where $X_t^F$ and $X_t^{F^\perp}$ are themselves OU processes on $F$ and $F^\perp$, driven by independent cylindrical Brownian motions in those subspaces.

2. **Event of hitting the affine subspace.**  
   The event that $X_t$ hits $x_0+F$ at some time $t$ is equivalent to:
   \[
   \exists t\ge 0 : X_t \in x_0+F
   \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
   \exists t\ge 0 : X_t^{F^\perp} = x_0^{F^\perp}
   \]
   (after decomposing $x_0=x_0^F+x_0^{F^\perp}$).

   Thus, the hitting problem for $x_0+F$ reduces to the hitting problem for the **singleton** $\{x_0^{F^\perp}\}$ in the infinite-dimensional OU process on $F^\perp$.

3. **Polarity of singletons in infinite dimension.**  
   In classical theory of OU on infinite-dimensional Gaussian spaces, singletons $\{z\}$ (or even finite-dimensional affine subspaces) are typically polar. Intuitively, the capacity of $\{z\}$ is zero, because any function $u$ that is $\ge 1$ on a neighborhood of $\{z\}$ must have nontrivial dependence on infinitely many coordinates, making $\mathcal{E}(u,u)$ infinite unless $u$ is trivial, which contradicts the capacity constraint.

   If we accept as a theorem that **all singletons are polar** in infinite-dimensional OU spaces, then it follows that $x_0+F$ is polar whenever $\dim(F^\perp)=\infty$.

4. **Conclusion for infinite-codimension affine subspaces.**  
   Under the assumption:
   > (A) In an infinite-dimensional Gaussian Hilbert space, any singleton (and therefore any finite-dimensional affine subspace) is polar for the OU Dirichlet form,
   
   we deduce:
   - Any affine subspace with infinite-dimensional orthogonal complement is polar, because its hit probability reduces to the hit probability of a singleton in an infinite-dimensional OU process.
